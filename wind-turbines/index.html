Wind turbines: how much of the wind can be caught

A wind turbine takes energy out of moving air by slowing it down. That simple fact sets a hard limit: if the rotor stopped the air completely there would be no flow through it at all, and if it did not slow the air there would be nothing to extract. In 1919 Albert Betz showed that the best compromise slows the wind to two thirds of its speed at the rotor and captures 16/27, or 59.3%, of the power in the wind passing through the rotor’s area, whatever the design. Real blades approach that limit closely: modern turbines reach about 80% of it. They do it with long, slender, twisted blades turning so that their tips move seven or eight times faster than the wind, which is also why the biggest turbines turn so slowly. These experiments follow the air through the stream tube of a turbine, design a blade by blade element momentum theory, and watch the helical wake its tips leave behind.

1. The Betz limit: the stream tube

Follow the air that passes through the rotor. Upstream it moves at \(U\); at the rotor it has slowed to \(U(1-a)\), and far behind to \(U(1-2a)\), where \(a\) is the axial induction factor. The tube of air widens as it slows, because the same mass flows through every cross-section. The force on the air is the drop in its momentum and the power taken is that force times the speed at the rotor, which gives \(C_P=4a(1-a)^2\). It is largest at \(a=1/3\), where the wake moves at a third of the wind speed and the tube has doubled its area: \(C_P=16/27\). Asking for more slowing makes the air spill around the rotor instead of through it.

Worked example. For a rotor of radius 60 m in a 10 m/s wind, the air passing through the disc carries \(\tfrac12\times1.225\times\pi\times60^2\times10^3=6.93\) MW. At the Betz optimum the rotor takes 16/27 of it, 4.11 MW; the wind leaves at 3.3 m/s in a tube of twice the rotor’s area, and the thrust coefficient is \(8/9\), a force of about 610 kN on the tower top. On the axis one radius ahead of the rotor, the wind has already slowed to \(\bigl(1-\tfrac13(1-1/\sqrt2)\bigr)U=0.902\,U\).

Watch out. The actuator disc ignores the blades, the rotation of the wake and viscous mixing; above \(a\approx0.4\) the simple momentum balance fails and real rotors enter a turbulent wake state. The tube is drawn from the velocity on its axis, which is exact for a uniformly loaded disc only on the axis itself. The turbine is a generic three-bladed design; the smoke speed is slowed for viewing.

Why can no turbine extract all of the wind’s energy?

To take all the energy the air would have to leave at zero speed, but then no air could pass through the rotor to be replaced. The power is the product of the force (which grows as the air is slowed) and the flow through the disc (which shrinks), and that product peaks when the air is slowed by a third at the rotor.

Reference: J. F. Manwell, J. G. McGowan and A. L. Rogers · Wind Energy Explained: Theory, Design and Application, 2nd edition, Wiley (2009)

2. Blade element momentum: designing the blade

Cut the rotor into rings. Each ring of blade sees the wind slowed by \(a\) and swirled by \(a^{\prime}\), and the blade section meets the resulting relative wind at the inflow angle \(\varphi\); its lift and drag give a thrust and a torque. Momentum theory gives the same thrust and torque in terms of \(a\) and \(a^{\prime}\), and solving the two descriptions together, ring by ring, is blade element momentum theory. For the best performance every ring should run at \(a\approx1/3\), which fixes the ideal chord and twist: wide and steeply twisted near the hub, where the blade moves slowly, slender and nearly flat at the tip. The blade here was designed this way for \(\lambda=7\).

Worked example. This three-bladed rotor reaches \(C_P=0.453\) at \(\lambda=7\) and its best, 0.456, at \(\lambda\approx7.7\), against Glauert’s ideal 0.579 and the Betz limit 0.593: tip losses, drag and the capped root chord cost about a fifth. A 60 m rotor in a 9 m/s wind at \(\lambda=7\) turns at \(60\times7\times9/(2\pi\times60)=10.0\) rpm and delivers 2.29 MW. At \(\lambda=4\) the root sections see \(33^\circ\) and stall; pitching the blades by \(10^\circ\) at \(\lambda=7\) drops \(C_P\) to 0.10.

Watch out. The airfoil data are a simple model (thin-airfoil lift with a soft stall at \(12^\circ\) and a parabolic drag polar), not a measured section; the theory assumes independent rings, steady flow and no yaw, and uses Prandtl’s tip loss factor \(F\) and Glauert’s correction for heavily loaded rings. Results are of the right size for a modern rotor but are not those of any particular machine.

Why is a wind turbine blade twisted?

Each section moves at its own speed \(\Omega r\), so the relative wind arrives at an angle \(\varphi\) that is steep near the hub and shallow near the tip. To keep every section at its best angle of attack, the blade’s angle must follow \(\varphi\), which means a large twist at the root and almost none at the tip.

Reference: J. F. Manwell, J. G. McGowan and A. L. Rogers · Wind Energy Explained: Theory, Design and Application, 2nd edition, Wiley (2009)

3. Tip-speed ratio and the helical wake

Each blade tip sheds a concentrated vortex, and because the rotor turns while the wind carries the vortex away, the three tip vortices wind into interleaved helices. Their pitch is set by the tip-speed ratio \(\lambda=\Omega R/U\): a fast rotor packs them tightly, which is what lets a few slender blades act on all the air passing through the disc. A rotor that turns slowly must instead deliver its power as a large torque, and the reaction sets the wake swirling, energy that is lost. Glauert’s ideal rotor, which accounts for this, approaches the Betz limit only as \(\lambda\) grows. Tip speed itself is capped by noise and erosion near 80 to 90 m/s, so large rotors turn slowly.

Worked example. At \(\lambda=7\) with \(a=1/3\), successive tip vortices from the three blades are \(2\pi\times\tfrac23/21=0.199\,R\) apart along the wake. Glauert’s ideal power coefficient is 0.579 at \(\lambda=7\), 0.512 at \(\lambda=2\) and only 0.416 at \(\lambda=1\). A 60 m rotor at \(\lambda=7\) in a 10 m/s wind turns at 11.1 rpm with a tip speed of 70 m/s; a 120 m rotor at the same tip speed turns at 5.6 rpm.

Watch out. The wake is drawn as rigid helices convected at \(U(1-a)\) with a modest expansion; real tip vortices also roll up, wander and break down a few diameters downstream. The ideal curve assumes infinitely many blades and no drag.

Why does a slowly turning rotor lose efficiency?

Power is torque times angular speed, so at low \(\Omega\) the rotor must exert a large torque on the air. The equal and opposite torque sets the wake rotating, and that swirl carries away kinetic energy that the rotor could otherwise have extracted.

Reference: J. F. Manwell, J. G. McGowan and A. L. Rogers · Wind Energy Explained: Theory, Design and Application, 2nd edition, Wiley (2009)
