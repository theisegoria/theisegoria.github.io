Algebraic topology: detecting holes

Build complexes, distinguish cycles from holes, and vary scale.

Before you begin: Sets, graphs, and elementary linear algebra

1. Build a complex2. Holes across scales3. Euler characteristic

Predict, manipulate, then check your reasoning against the example and question. Graphs illustrate the mathematics; they do not replace a proof.

1. Build a complex

Add the edges of a triangle, then its filled face. The third edge creates a cycle; the face makes that cycle a boundary and removes the one-dimensional hole.

Worked example. Three vertices and three edges give β₀=1, β₁=1. Adding the face gives β₁=0.

Watch out. A drawn loop is not necessarily a homological hole: it may bound a filled surface.

Why does filling the face not change the number of components?

Its boundary already belongs to one connected component.

Reference: Allen Hatcher · Algebraic Topology, Chapter 2

2. Holes across scales

Eight points lie on a circle. Connect pairs within the threshold and fill every triangle whose three edges are present. Compute homology over the two-element field using boundary-matrix ranks.

Worked example. At a threshold of 0.8, adjacent points form an octagonal loop with β₁=1.

Watch out. Only the two-skeleton is displayed. It suffices for H₀ and H₁, but not for higher-dimensional homology.

Why can a larger threshold destroy a hole?

New triangular faces can fill a previously unfilled cycle.

Reference: Allen Hatcher · Algebraic Topology, Chapter 2

3. Euler characteristic

Choose a triangulated disk or the boundary of a tetrahedron. Count vertices, edges, and faces. The alternating sum agrees with the alternating sum of Betti numbers.

Worked example. A tetrahedron boundary has 4−6+4=2, with β₀=1, β₁=0, β₂=1.

Watch out. The tetrahedron here is its surface, not the solid including its interior.

Why does a disk have Euler characteristic one?

It has one component and no positive-dimensional homology.

Reference: Allen Hatcher · Algebraic Topology, Chapter 2
