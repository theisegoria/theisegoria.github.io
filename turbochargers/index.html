Turbochargers: exhaust that feeds the intake

An engine’s power is limited by how much air it can draw in, since each gram of fuel needs about fifteen grams of air to burn. A turbocharger pushes more air in. It is two small fans on one shaft: one sits in the stream of hot exhaust and is spun by it, and the other, driven by the first, compresses the air on its way to the cylinders. The energy comes from exhaust that would otherwise be thrown away, which makes the arrangement look like something for nothing. The costs are subtler. Compressed air is hot air, and hot air is thin. And the turbine has no link to the crankshaft, so it must be spun up by the very flow it is supposed to increase. These experiments open a turbocharger, separate pressure from density, and time the lag.

1. The turbocharger

Both wheels are radial machines. On the hot side, exhaust enters a snail-shaped housing whose passage narrows as it winds inward, so the gas speeds up and strikes the turbine wheel at its rim, turns it, and leaves along the axis cooler and at lower pressure. On the cold side the reverse happens: air enters along the axis, is caught by the blades of the compressor wheel and flung outward, and is then slowed in a widening scroll, where its speed becomes pressure. The work the compressor does on each kilogram of air is set by the speed of its blade tips, about 0.85 times that speed squared, so a wheel only 50 mm across must turn at well over a hundred thousand revolutions a minute. Left alone, boost would go on rising with engine speed until something broke. So a pressure-operated valve, the wastegate, lets part of the exhaust bypass the turbine once the boost reaches its set value.

Worked example. For 1 bar of boost the pressure ratio is 2, and compressing air from 25 °C at 72 per cent efficiency takes \(1005\times298\times(2^{0.286}-1)/0.72=91\) kJ/kg. That needs a tip speed of \(\sqrt{91\,000/0.85}=327\) m/s, close to the speed of sound, and so \(327/(\pi\times0.05)=2084\) turns a second, 125 000 rpm. At 3000 rpm a 2 litre engine then takes in 98 g/s of air in place of 53 g/s and makes 97 kW in place of 53 kW.

Watch out. The turbocharger is a generic single-scroll unit with its bearing housing simplified and the blades drawn as flat plates; real blades are twisted in three dimensions. The compressor and turbine each have one fixed efficiency, where real ones vary across a map of flow and speed and are bounded by surge and choke, and the engine’s efficiency is taken not to change with boost.

Why does a turbocharged engine have a lower compression ratio than a similar engine without one?

The air already arrives compressed and warm, and the piston then compresses it further. To keep the temperature at the end of the compression stroke below the point where the mixture ignites by itself, the engine’s own share of the compression is reduced.

Reference: N. Watson and M. S. Janota · Turbocharging the Internal Combustion Engine (Macmillan, 1982)

2. Pressure is not density

An engine does not care about the pressure of its intake air as such. It burns fuel in proportion to the mass of air in the cylinder, and for a given volume that is the density. By the gas law, density is proportional to pressure divided by absolute temperature. Compression raises both. Even a perfect compressor heats air as it squeezes it, by a factor of the pressure ratio raised to the power 0.286, and a real compressor, churning the air as it goes, heats it more. So doubling the pressure gives well under double the density. The cure is to cool the air after it leaves the compressor and before it reaches the engine, in a radiator called an intercooler. Cooling at nearly constant pressure raises the density directly, and an intercooler that removes most of the added heat recovers most of what was lost.

Worked example. Compressing 25 °C air to a pressure ratio of 2, a perfect compressor would deliver it at 90 °C and one of 72 per cent efficiency at 116 °C. The density is then \(2\times298/389=1.53\) times that outside, not 2. An intercooler that removes 70 per cent of the rise brings the air to 52 °C and the density to \(2\times298/325=1.83\) times, a gain of 20 per cent in air and so in power.

Watch out. Air is treated as an ideal gas with constant heat capacities, entering at 25 °C. The intercooler is described by one number, the fraction of the temperature rise it removes, and its small loss of pressure is ignored. Heat picked up from the hot housing and pipes is left out.

A turbocharger is replaced with a more efficient one giving the same boost. The pressure gauge reads the same. Does the engine make more power?

Yes. At the same pressure a more efficient compressor delivers cooler air, which is denser, so more mass enters the cylinders. It also takes less work from the turbine, so the exhaust is less obstructed.

Reference: N. Watson and M. S. Janota · Turbocharging the Internal Combustion Engine (Macmillan, 1982)

3. Turbo lag

When the driver opens the throttle, a naturally aspirated engine responds within one or two turns of the crankshaft. A turbocharged one answers in two stages: at once, with what it can draw in unaided, and then again as the turbocharger comes up to speed. The rotor is light, but at a hundred thousand revolutions a minute it stores kilojoules of energy, and that energy can only come from the difference between the power the turbine takes from the exhaust and the power the compressor spends on the air. Early on that difference is small, because with little boost there is little exhaust. As boost builds, the engine breathes more, the exhaust grows stronger and the turbine pulls harder, so the rise accelerates until the wastegate opens. A small turbocharger has little inertia and a tight turbine that develops pressure from a small flow, so it responds quickly, but it chokes the engine at high speed. A large one does the opposite.

Worked example. The rotor has an inertia of \(3\times10^{-5}\) kg m², and at 125 000 rpm, 13 100 rad/s, it holds \(\tfrac12\times3\times10^{-5}\times13\,100^{2}=2.6\) kJ. At 3000 rpm and full boost the turbine can deliver 17.9 kW against the compressor’s 8.9 kW, and the boost reaches nine tenths of 1 bar after 0.40 s. At 2000 rpm it takes 2.0 s, and at 1500 rpm the boost levels off at 0.36 bar. A unit 30 per cent smaller reaches 90 per cent at 2000 rpm in 0.23 s.

Watch out. The engine is held at a fixed speed while the turbocharger spools, as on a test bench; in a car the engine speed rises too, which helps. The exhaust is given a fixed temperature of 1100 K and the turbine a simple law for its pressure drop against flow. The gas held in the manifolds, which adds its own delay, and friction in the bearings are ignored, and the rotor’s inertia is scaled as the fifth power of its size.

Some engines use two turbochargers, a small one and a large one, in sequence. What does each do?

The small one has little inertia and builds boost from the modest exhaust flow at low engine speed, giving a quick response. As the flow grows beyond what it can pass, valves bring in the large one, which can feed the engine at high speed without choking the exhaust.

Reference: N. Watson and M. S. Janota · Turbocharging the Internal Combustion Engine (Macmillan, 1982)
