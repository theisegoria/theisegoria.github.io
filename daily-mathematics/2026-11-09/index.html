Solution for 9 November 2026

The card that day was an original math olympiad problem. Try it first; the solution is folded away below.

Math Olympiad problem

A chord through the midpoint of BC

Let \(ABC\) be a triangle with \(AB\neq AC\) and circumcircle \(\Omega\). Its incircle touches \(BC\), \(CA\), \(AB\) at \(D\), \(E\), \(F\), and \(J\) is the excentre opposite \(A\). Let \(K\) be the foot of the perpendicular from \(D\) to \(EF\). The line \(AK\) meets \(\Omega\) again at \(Z\), and the line through \(Z\) and the midpoint \(M\) of \(BC\) meets \(\Omega\) again at \(W\). Prove that \(\angle AWJ=90^\circ\).

Show the solution

Notation. Write \(a,b,c\) for the side lengths, \(s\) for the semiperimeter, and put \(p=BD=BF=s-b\), \(q=CD=CE=s-c\). Since \(AB\neq AC\) we have \(p\neq q\). Let the excircle opposite \(A\) touch the lines \(AB\) and \(AC\) at \(F'\) and \(E'\); then \(BF'=s-c=q\) and \(CE'=s-b=p\), with \(F'\) on ray \(AB\) beyond \(B\) and \(E'\) on ray \(AC\) beyond \(C\).

Why chasing angles at \(W\) stalls. The points \(Z\) and \(W\) are defined by lines through \(K\) and through \(M\), and neither definition hands over a usable angle at \(W\). The right currency is the ratio \(XB/XC\) for a point \(X\) on \(\Omega\): a point of \(\Omega\) is determined by this ratio together with the arc \(BC\) it lies on, since on each open arc the ratio runs continuously and strictly monotonically from \(0\) to \(\infty\). The proof tracks this ratio from \(K\) to \(Z\) to \(W\), and then recognises the final value at the excircle.

Step 1: \(FK/KE=p/q\). By the tangent-chord angle, \(\angle DFE=\angle EDC=90^\circ-\tfrac{C}{2}\) and \(\angle DEF=\angle FDB=90^\circ-\tfrac{B}{2}\); both are acute, so \(K\) lies strictly between \(F\) and \(E\). The isosceles triangles \(BDF\) and \(CDE\) give \(DF=2p\sin\tfrac{B}{2}\) and \(DE=2q\sin\tfrac{C}{2}\). In the right triangles \(DKF\) and \(DKE\),

\[FK=DF\cos\angle DFE=2p\sin\tfrac{B}{2}\sin\tfrac{C}{2},\qquad KE=DE\cos\angle DEF=2q\sin\tfrac{C}{2}\sin\tfrac{B}{2},\]

so \(FK/KE=p/q\).

Step 2: \(ZB/ZC=p/q\), and \(Z\) lies on the arc \(BC\) not containing \(A\). Since \(K\) is an interior point of segment \(EF\), it lies inside the triangle, so ray \(AK\) crosses the interior of segment \(BC\) and then meets \(\Omega\) on the arc \(BC\) not containing \(A\); this point is \(Z\). Put \(\beta=\angle BAZ\) and \(\gamma=\angle ZAC\). Comparing the areas of triangles \(AFK\) and \(AKE\), which have the same height from \(A\), and using \(AF=AE\),

\[\frac{FK}{KE}=\frac{[AFK]}{[AKE]}=\frac{\tfrac12\,AF\cdot AK\sin\beta}{\tfrac12\,AE\cdot AK\sin\gamma}=\frac{\sin\beta}{\sin\gamma}.\]

By the extended law of sines in \(\Omega\) (radius \(R\)), \(ZB=2R\sin\beta\) and \(ZC=2R\sin\gamma\). With Step 1 this gives \(ZB/ZC=p/q\).

Step 3: a chord through \(M\) inverts the ratio. The chords \(BC\) and \(ZW\) meet at \(M\), which is inside \(\Omega\), so \(MB\cdot MC=MZ\cdot MW\). Hence \(MZ/MC=MB/MW\), and with the vertical angles at \(M\) the triangles \(MZB\) and \(MCW\) are similar, giving \(ZB/CW=MZ/MC\). In the same way triangles \(MZC\) and \(MBW\) are similar, giving \(ZC/BW=MZ/MB\). Because \(MB=MC\), the two right-hand sides agree, so \(ZB\cdot BW=ZC\cdot CW\), that is

\[\frac{WB}{WC}=\frac{ZC}{ZB}=\frac{q}{p}=\frac{BF'}{CE'}.\]

Moreover \(Z\) is strictly on the other side of \(BC\) from \(A\) and \(M\) lies on \(BC\) between \(Z\) and \(W\), so \(W\) lies on the arc \(BAC\). Also \(W\neq A\): otherwise \(c/b=(s-c)/(s-b)\), which simplifies to \(s(c-b)=0\).

Step 4: \(W\) lies on the circle through \(A\), \(F'\), \(E'\). Both \(W\) and \(A\) lie on the arc \(BAC\), so the arc of \(\Omega\) between them inside arc \(BAC\) contains neither \(B\) nor \(C\). Hence \(B\) and \(C\) lie on the same side of line \(WA\), and the inscribed angles give \(\angle WBA=\angle WCA\). As \(F'\) is on ray \(AB\) beyond \(B\) and \(E'\) is on ray \(AC\) beyond \(C\),

\[\angle WBF'=180^\circ-\angle WBA=180^\circ-\angle WCA=\angle WCE'.\]

Together with \(WB/BF'=WC/CE'\) from Step 3, the triangles \(WBF'\) and \(WCE'\) are similar (SAS). Therefore \(\angle WF'A=\angle WF'B=\angle WE'C=\angle WE'A\). The points \(F'\) and \(E'\) lie on the same side of line \(WA\) as \(B\) and \(C\), so the equal angles show that \(A\), \(W\), \(F'\), \(E'\) are concyclic.

Step 5: conclusion. Since \(JF'\perp AB\) and \(JE'\perp AC\), the circle through \(A\), \(F'\), \(E'\) is the circle with diameter \(AJ\). By Step 4, \(W\) lies on it. Finally \(W\neq J\), because \(J\) lies outside \(\Omega\) (it is on ray \(AI\) beyond the midpoint of the arc \(BC\), which is the midpoint of \(IJ\)), while \(W\) lies on \(\Omega\). By Thales' theorem, \(\angle AWJ=90^\circ\).

Remark. The same argument shows that \(W\) is the point of arc \(BAC\) whose angle bisector of \(\angle BWC\) passes through the point where the excircle touches \(BC\), while \(Z\) is the point of the opposite arc whose bisector of \(\angle BZC\) passes through \(D\).

Check. The statement was tested numerically on 270,067 random triangles (acute and obtuse, with \(AB\neq AC\)): the cosine of \(\angle AWJ\) never exceeded \(3.3\times10^{-12}\), and the ratios \(ZB/ZC=(s-b)/(s-c)\) and \(WB/WC=(s-c)/(s-b)\) hold to a relative error below \(4\times10^{-11}\).

Answer. Proved: \(\angle AWJ=90^\circ\); \(W\) is the second intersection of \(\Omega\) with the circle on diameter \(AJ\).
