Solution for 8 November 2026

The card that day was an original math olympiad problem. Try it first; the solution is folded away below.

Math Olympiad problem

The expected characteristic polynomial of a random permutation matrix

Let \(n\) be a positive integer and let \(P\) be an \(n\times n\) permutation matrix chosen uniformly at random from all \(n!\) of them. With \(P^{\mathsf T}\) the transpose of \(P\), find all roots of the polynomial

\[p(x)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\det\bigl(xI-P-P^{\mathsf T}\bigr)\bigr].\]
Show the solution

The roots are \(x=2\) and \(x=2\cos(k\pi/n)\) for \(k=1,\dots,n-1\). In fact \(p(x)=(x-2)\,U_{n-1}(x/2)\), where \(U_m\) is the Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind, so \(p\) has \(n\) distinct real roots.

Why the obvious route fails. The expectation of a determinant is not the determinant of the expectation. Since \(\mathbb{E}[P]=\frac1n J\) (all entries \(1/n\)), the matrix \(\mathbb{E}[P+P^{\mathsf T}]=\frac2n J\) has characteristic polynomial \(x^{n-1}(x-2)\), which is wrong already for \(n=2\): there \(p(x)=\frac12\bigl((x-2)^2+(x^2-4)\bigr)=x(x-2)\). Averaging \(n!\) characteristic polynomials one by one is hopeless, so we organise the average by cycle type.

Step 1: one factor per cycle. Let \(\sigma\) be the permutation of \(P\), with cycles of lengths \(k_1,\dots,k_r\). Relabelling the indices so that every cycle uses consecutive indices conjugates \(P\) by a permutation matrix, which changes no determinant. After relabelling, \(P\) is block diagonal with blocks \(C_{k_1},\dots,C_{k_r}\), where \(C_k\) is the \(k\times k\) cyclic shift (for \(k=1\) it is the \(1\times1\) matrix \((1)\)). Hence

\[\det\bigl(xI-P-P^{\mathsf T}\bigr)=\prod_{i=1}^r f_{k_i}(x),\qquad f_k(x)=\det\bigl(xI_k-C_k-C_k^{\mathsf T}\bigr).\]

Step 2: the cycle factor in the variable \(t\). Write \(x=t+t^{-1}\). The matrix \(C_k\) is orthogonal, so \(C_k^{\mathsf T}=C_k^{-1}\), and it is diagonalisable with eigenvalues \(\omega^j\), \(j=0,\dots,k-1\), where \(\omega=e^{2\pi i/k}\). So \(C_k+C_k^{\mathsf T}\) has eigenvalues \(\omega^j+\omega^{-j}\); this covers \(k=1\) (the block \((2)\)) and \(k=2\) (the block with eigenvalues \(\pm2\)) as well. Using \((t-\omega^j)(t-\omega^{-j})=t\,(x-\omega^j-\omega^{-j})\),

\[f_k=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\bigl(x-\omega^j-\omega^{-j}\bigr)=t^{-k}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(t-\omega^j)(t-\omega^{-j})=t^{-k}\,(t^k-1)^2=t^k+t^{-k}-2 .\]

So every cycle, of any length, contributes the same simple Laurent polynomial in \(t\).

Step 3: the exponential formula. The number of permutations of \(\{1,\dots,n\}\) with \(m_k\) cycles of length \(k\) for each \(k\) (where \(\sum k\,m_k=n\)) is \(n!/\prod_k k^{m_k}m_k!\). Therefore

\[p_n:=p(x)=\sum_{\sum k m_k=n}\ \prod_k\frac{1}{m_k!}\Bigl(\frac{f_k}{k}\Bigr)^{m_k},\]

which is the coefficient of \(z^n\) in \(\prod_k\exp(f_kz^k/k)\). With \(p_0=1\), as formal power series in \(z\) with coefficients in \(\mathbb{Q}[t,t^{-1}]\),

\[\sum_{n\ge0}p_nz^n=\exp\Bigl(\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(tz)^k+(z/t)^k-2z^k}{k}\Bigr)=\frac{(1-z)^2}{(1-tz)(1-z/t)} .\]

Step 4: extracting the coefficient. The coefficient of \(z^m\) in \(1/\bigl((1-tz)(1-z/t)\bigr)\) is \(u_m=\sum_{a+b=m}t^{a-b}=\dfrac{t^{m+1}-t^{-m-1}}{t-t^{-1}}\), which is \(U_m(x/2)\). Setting \(u_{-1}=u_{-2}=0\), we get \(p_n=u_n-2u_{n-1}+u_{n-2}\). Now put \(t=e^{i\theta}\) with \(0<\theta<\pi\), so that \(x=2\cos\theta\) runs over \((-2,2)\) and \(u_m=\sin((m+1)\theta)/\sin\theta\). Since \(\sin((n+1)\theta)+\sin((n-1)\theta)=2\sin(n\theta)\cos\theta\),

\[p_n=\frac{2\sin(n\theta)\,(\cos\theta-1)}{\sin\theta}=(x-2)\,\frac{\sin(n\theta)}{\sin\theta}=(x-2)\,U_{n-1}(x/2).\]

Both sides are polynomials in \(x\) that agree on the interval \((-2,2)\), so they are equal identically. (Equivalently, \(p(2\cos\theta)=-2\tan(\theta/2)\sin(n\theta)\).)

Step 5: the roots. The polynomial \(U_{n-1}(x/2)\) has degree \(n-1\) and vanishes at \(x=2\cos(k\pi/n)\), \(k=1,\dots,n-1\), since \(\sin(k\pi)=0\) while \(\sin(k\pi/n)\ne0\). These are \(n-1\) distinct numbers in \((-2,2)\), hence all its roots. The factor \(x-2\) adds the root \(2\). So \(p\) is monic of degree \(n\) with the \(n\) distinct real roots \(2\) and \(2\cos(k\pi/n)\), \(1\le k\le n-1\).

Remarks. The root \(2\) is visible directly: every row of \(P+P^{\mathsf T}\) sums to \(2\), so each individual \(\det(2I-P-P^{\mathsf T})\) vanishes. The other roots are the eigenvalues of the path graph on \(n-1\) vertices, so \(p(x)\) is \(x-2\) times the characteristic polynomial of that path, although no single \(P+P^{\mathsf T}\) need have these eigenvalues.

Check. The averaged characteristic polynomial was computed exactly by brute force over all permutations for \(n\le7\), and by the exact cycle recursion \(n\,p_n=\sum_{k=1}^n f_k\,p_{n-k}\) for \(n\le25\); in every case the roots are \(2\) and \(2\cos(k\pi/n)\), and the identity \(p(2\cos\theta)=-2\tan(\theta/2)\sin(n\theta)\) holds at random \(\theta\).

Answer. \(x=2\) and \(x=2\cos(k\pi/n)\) for \(k=1,\dots,n-1\); indeed \(p(x)=(x-2)\,U_{n-1}(x/2)\).
