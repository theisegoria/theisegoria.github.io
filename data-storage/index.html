Data storage: spinning discs and trapped charge

A computer keeps its files in one of two ways. A hard disc writes them as magnetised patches on a spinning platter and reaches them with a head on a moving arm, so every read waits for metal to move. A solid-state drive stores them as small amounts of electric charge trapped in billions of transistors, with nothing to move at all, but each of those cells wears a little every time it is rewritten and slowly leaks. Almost everything about how storage behaves follows from those two mechanisms: why a disc manages only about a hundred scattered reads a second, why the outside of a disc is faster than the inside, why cheaper flash holds more bits per cell and wears out sooner, and why the same job can take three seconds on one drive and an hour on another. These experiments open a hard disc, look at the voltage levels inside a flash cell, and run one workload on three kinds of drive.

Before you begin: Rotation and circular motion, basic electricity, probability (normal distributions) for the second experiment

1. Inside a hard disc: seek and spin

A hard disc stores bits as tiny magnetised regions along circular tracks on both sides of each platter. To read a block the drive must do two mechanical things. It swings the arm so the head sits over the right track, the seek, driven by a flat coil in a magnetic field; the arm accelerates for half the move and brakes for the other half, so the time grows as the square root of the distance. Then it waits for the spinning disc to bring the block round to the head, on average half a revolution. Once there, data streams past at the speed of the track, and because bits are written at the same spacing everywhere, an outer track, which moves faster, delivers data faster. The head itself does not touch the disc: it flies a few nanometres above it on the air the platter drags round.

Worked example. With a settle time of 0.8 ms and a full-stroke seek of 16 ms, the mean seek between two random tracks is \(0.8+15.2\times\tfrac{8}{15}=8.9\) ms. At 7200 rpm one turn takes 8.33 ms, so the mean wait is 4.17 ms and the drive manages \(1/(8.9+4.17\ \mathrm{ms})=76\) random reads a second; at 15000 rpm the wait falls to 2 ms and the figure rises only to 92. At 7200 rpm the outermost track (46 mm) passes the head at 125 km/h and reads 250 MB/s; the innermost (20.5 mm) reads 111 MB/s.

Watch out. The drive is a generic 3.5 inch design with three platters, drawn with its cover and upper magnet plate removed, and its spin is slowed about a thousand times. Seek and density figures are typical of a desktop drive, not a particular model; real drives also reorder queued requests and cache data, which the third experiment touches on.

Why does doubling a disc’s spin speed not double the number of random reads it can do?

A random read costs a seek plus a rotational wait, and spinning faster shortens only the wait. With a mean seek of about 9 ms and a wait of about 4 ms, halving the wait removes 2 ms of 13, a gain of under a fifth.

Reference: R. H. Arpaci-Dusseau and A. C. Arpaci-Dusseau · Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces (chapters on hard disk drives and flash-based SSDs)

2. Flash memory: levels of trapped charge

A flash cell is a transistor with an extra, electrically isolated layer beside its channel. Electrons forced into that layer stay for years and raise the gate voltage needed to switch the transistor on, its threshold voltage. One bit per cell needs only two states, charged or not. To store more, the drive programs each cell to one of 4, 8 or 16 threshold voltages and reads it back by comparing against reference voltages placed between the levels. The levels are not sharp: cells differ, each write disturbs the insulator a little, and trapped charge slowly leaks, so every level is a bell curve that widens with use and drifts with time. Where neighbouring curves overlap, cells are misread. Packing in more bits narrows the gaps, so the same wear causes far more errors.

Worked example. Three bits per cell means 8 levels: the seven programmed ones share a window of 4.8 V, 0.8 V apart, and after 1000 cycles each is about 125 mV wide, so a reference halfway between two neighbours is 2.9 standard deviations from each and the raw bit error rate is about \(8\times10^{-4}\). The same cells at 3000 cycles reach \(5\times10^{-3}\), the limit this model takes for error correction. With these representative numbers the limit is reached near 51000 cycles for one bit per cell, 10000 for two, 3000 for three and 1000 for four.

Watch out. The distributions are idealised as Gaussians with widths and drifts chosen to give typical endurance figures; real cells have asymmetric tails, interfere with their neighbours and depend strongly on temperature. The error-correction limit is indicative. Voltages are representative of a NAND cell, not a specification.

Why does flash that stores more bits per cell wear out sooner?

More bits means more voltage levels inside the same window, so the gaps between them are narrower. Wear widens every level by about the same amount, and a narrow gap is closed, and the data becomes unreadable, after far fewer rewrites than a wide one.

Reference: R. H. Arpaci-Dusseau and A. C. Arpaci-Dusseau · Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces (chapters on hard disk drives and flash-based SSDs)

3. One job, three drives

How long a read takes depends less on how much data there is than on how it is laid out. Data in one long run streams at the drive’s sequential rate. Data scattered in small pieces costs one access each, and here the drives part company. A disc needs about 13 ms of mechanical motion per access. A SATA solid-state drive needs about 90 microseconds and can work on 32 requests at once; an NVMe drive, attached directly to the PCIe bus, needs about 25 microseconds and accepts many more. Queueing helps a disc only a little: it can pick the nearest waiting request, but it still has one arm. So the gap between drives is about thirty-fold for a film copied in one piece and several thousand-fold for a database or an operating system starting up.

Worked example. One gibibyte is 262144 blocks of 4 KiB. Read in one run it takes 5.4 s on the disc (200 MB/s), 2.0 s on SATA (550 MB/s) and 0.15 s on NVMe (7000 MB/s). Read as scattered blocks one at a time it takes \(262144/76=3427\) s, 57 minutes, on the disc, 23.6 s on SATA and 6.6 s on NVMe. With 32 requests in flight the disc improves to 267 reads a second, the SATA drive reaches 356000 and the NVMe drive 1.28 million.

Watch out. Access times and rates are round, typical figures. The model ignores caches, read-ahead, the file system and the fact that a busy flash drive must also erase and move data in the background, which can slow sustained writes considerably. The queueing gain for the disc is a simple approximation.

Why does replacing a disc with an SSD speed up starting programs far more than copying a large film?

A film is one long run, which a disc reads at a steady 200 MB/s, only a few times slower than flash. Starting a program reads thousands of small pieces from all over the drive, and each costs the disc a seek and half a turn but costs flash almost nothing.

Reference: R. H. Arpaci-Dusseau and A. C. Arpaci-Dusseau · Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces (chapters on hard disk drives and flash-based SSDs)
