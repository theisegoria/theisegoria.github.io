Violins: the bow, the bridge and the box

A plucked string is given one push and left to die away. A bowed string is pushed afresh in every vibration, and so can sing for as long as the bow lasts. The bow does this without any timing of its own: its hair grips the string and drags it aside until the string tears loose and springs back, and the string itself decides when. The motion that results is not the gentle arc the eye sees but a sharp corner chasing round the string hundreds of times a second. That corner jolts the bridge, the bridge rocks on the thin wooden top, and the box beneath, with its own preferred frequencies, turns the jolts into sound. These experiments follow the corner, bow a violin too hard and too lightly, and listen through the body.

1. The corner on the string

In 1860 Helmholtz looked at a bowed string through a vibrating microscope and found that it moves in a way nobody had guessed. At any instant the string is two straight lines meeting at a corner. The corner travels along the string at the speed of waves on it, reflects at the nut, comes back, reflects at the bridge, and so goes round once in each period, tracing a lens-shaped outline made of two parabolas. That outline is all the eye can follow. The bow fits into this as a clutch. While the corner is away toward the nut, the piece of string under the bow moves steadily with the hair, stuck to it by friction. When the corner arrives and passes under the bow, the string’s velocity there reverses abruptly, the grip of the rosin is broken, and the string slides back against the hair until the corner returns from the bridge and it is caught again. The slip lasts the fraction of the period that the bow’s distance from the bridge is of the string’s length.

Worked example. An A string 325 mm long sounds 440 Hz, so the corner travels at \(2\times0.325\times440=286\) m/s. Bowed at 30 cm/s a tenth of the way from the bridge, the string sticks for nine tenths of each period and slips back at \(30\times0.9/0.1=270\) cm/s. Its middle swings \(0.30/(8\times440\times0.1)=0.85\) mm each way. With a tension of 54 N the string’s wave resistance is 0.19 kg/s, and the push on the bridge swings by \(0.19\times0.30/0.1=0.57\) N.

Watch out. This is the ideal Helmholtz motion of a perfectly flexible string with a bow of no width. In a real string the corner is rounded by stiffness and by losses at the ends, and the more the bow presses the sharper it becomes, which is how bow force brightens the tone. Small ripples caused by waves trapped between bow and bridge are left out.

A violinist bows twice as fast without changing anything else. What happens to the loudness and to the pitch?

The swing of the string and the force on the bridge both double, so the note is louder. The pitch is unchanged: the corner still travels at the speed of waves on the string and takes the same time to go round.

Reference: J. C. Schelleng · The bowed string and the player (Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 1973)

2. Bowing: too hard, too light

The stick-slip cycle only keeps time if the bow presses within limits. Friction must be strong enough to hold the string through the whole sticking part of the cycle, against a restoring force that grows as the string is dragged aside; press too lightly and the string breaks away early, slips more than once per period, and gives a thin, glassy sound with little fundamental. But the arriving corner must also be able to break the grip. Press too hard and the string stays stuck when the corner comes, the motion loses its period and the note collapses into a rasp. Schelleng worked out both limits. The upper one falls as one over the bow’s distance from the bridge and the lower one as one over its square, so on logarithmic scales they are two straight lines that meet near the bridge and open out toward the fingerboard. Both rise in proportion to bow speed.

Worked example. On the A string at 30 cm/s, with rosin giving a difference of 0.5 between sticking and sliding friction and a bridge resistance of 15 kg/s, a bow a tenth of the way along needs between 0.07 N and 2.3 N, a range of thirty to one. At a twenty-fifth of the way, 13 mm from the bridge, the limits are 0.45 N and 5.7 N, a range of thirteen. They would meet where \(\beta=Z/4R=0.3\) per cent, closer than a bow can play.

Watch out. The violin is a generic full-size instrument drawn to scale with a flat top and back; real plates are arched. The two limits are Schelleng’s formulas with one friction difference and one bridge resistance for all strings and frequencies, so they give the shape of the playable region, not its exact edges. Tilting the bow, which changes how much hair touches the string, is not modelled.

To play loudly, violinists move the bow toward the bridge and press harder. Why do they need to do both?

Moving toward the bridge raises the force on the bridge for the same bow speed, which is what makes the note louder. But both limits of bow force rise as the bow nears the bridge, the lower one fastest, so the pressure that worked further out is now too light and must be increased to stay inside the wedge.

Reference: J. C. Schelleng · The bowed string and the player (Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 1973)

3. What the body adds

A string is far too thin to push much air, so on its own it is almost silent. The bridge passes its sideways jolts to the top plate as a rocking motion, and the plates, with the air they enclose, do the radiating. But a box is not an even-handed loudspeaker. It has resonances, frequencies at which it moves readily, and between them it responds weakly. Lowest is the air resonance, in which air pumps in and out of the f-holes against the springiness of the air inside, as in a bottle blown across its mouth. Next come two strong resonances of the whole body, and above them a crowd of plate resonances. Near 2.5 kHz the bridge itself rocks on its narrow waist, lifting a broad band of frequencies to which the ear is most sensitive. The string supplies harmonics falling off as one over their number; the body weights each by its response at that frequency. The result is the instrument’s voice.

Worked example. Two f-holes with a combined area of 12 cm², opening from 2.0 litres of air through an effective neck of 22 mm, resonate at \((343/2\pi)\sqrt{0.0012/(0.002\times0.022)}=285\) Hz, close to the open D string. The open G string, at 196 Hz, lies below every resonance, so its fundamental is weak: the fourth harmonic, at 784 Hz, is the strongest, and the fundamental is 72 per cent of it despite being four times stronger in the string.

Watch out. The response curve is a schematic sum of seven resonances with frequencies and widths typical of a good violin, not a measurement of any instrument. It ignores the direction in which sound is radiated, which changes with frequency, and the interaction between string and body that produces a wolf note. The air resonance uses the simple bottle formula with one effective neck length.

A viola is tuned a fifth below a violin but is only about a seventh longer. What does that do to its lowest notes?

To keep the same match between strings and body, every dimension would have to grow by half. It grows much less, so its resonances are too high for its tuning, and the fundamentals of its lower strings fall well below them. That is the source of the viola’s darker, more veiled sound.

Reference: J. C. Schelleng · The bowed string and the player (Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 1973)
