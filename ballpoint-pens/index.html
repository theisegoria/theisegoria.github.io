Ballpoint pens: a ball, a socket and a very thick ink

A ballpoint pen is a bearing that leaks on purpose. At its tip a hard ball a millimetre across turns in a socket, wetted on the inside by ink and rolled dry on the outside by the paper. The film it leaves is less than a thousandth of a millimetre thick, which is why a few drops of ink write for kilometres. Everything depends on the ink. It must be thick enough not to run out past the ball or soak into the paper, and yet arrive at the ball as fast as it is used and let the ball turn freely. The answer is a paste ten thousand times thicker than water that turns thin where it is sheared hardest. These experiments open the tip, balance the supply of ink against the writing, and follow the ink as it thins in the gap.

1. The tip

The ball is made of tungsten carbide, one of the hardest materials that can be shaped into a sphere, because it has to roll for kilometres on paper, which is mildly abrasive, without losing its roundness. It sits in a socket machined into the end of a brass or steel tube, and the rim of the socket is pressed inward just beyond the middle of the ball to hold it. The clearance between ball and socket is about a thousandth of a millimetre. Ink reaches the back of the ball through a narrow channel. When the pen moves, friction with the paper turns the ball without slipping, so its surface moves at the speed of the pen. The side that was inside, coated with ink, comes round to the paper and prints a line about half as wide as the ball. A ball of diameter \(d\) turns once for every \(\pi d\) of line.

Worked example. A 1.0 mm ball leaves a line 0.45 mm wide and a film 0.40 μm thick, so a metre of writing uses \(1050\times0.45\times10^{-3}\times0.40\times10^{-6}=1.9\times10^{-7}\) kg, or 189 μg. A refill holding 0.4 g therefore writes 2.1 km. The ball turns 318 times a metre, 1910 rpm at a writing speed of 10 cm/s, and about 670 000 times over the life of the refill. A 0.7 mm ball writes 4.3 km on the same ink.

Watch out. The width of the line and the thickness of the film are fits to an ordinary medium ballpoint and are taken to scale with the ball. In practice they also change with pressure, speed, paper and temperature. The tip is drawn to scale, with the clearance round the ball enlarged so that the ink film can be seen.

Why does a ballpoint skip on a glossy photograph or a greasy patch of paper?

The ball is turned only by friction with the surface. On something smooth or greasy it slides instead of rolling, so no fresh inked surface comes round, and the little ink already outside is soon used up.

2. Feeding the ball

There is no pump in the pen. With the point down, the weight of the ink column pushes it toward the ball, and the ink flows along its tube as any liquid flows along a pipe: at a rate proportional to the pressure pushing it and to the fourth power of the radius, and inversely proportional to its viscosity. For a thin liquid that rate would be enormous compared with the trickle that writing uses, and the pen would blot. Ballpoint ink is a paste of dye in oil with a viscosity around ten thousand times that of water, chosen so that the tube can deliver a few times what the ball takes and no more. The price is that gravity is all there is. Tilt the pen past horizontal and the column creeps slowly back from the ball. Pens meant to write in any position put compressed gas behind the ink, and use a still thicker ink to match.

Worked example. For a tube of radius 0.8 mm and ink of 10 Pa s, point down, \(Q=\pi(0.0008)^{4}\times1050\times9.81/(8\times10)=1.66\times10^{-10}\) m³/s, or 0.166 μL/s. Writing at 10 cm/s with a 1 mm ball uses 0.018 μL/s, so supply is 9 times demand. Left alone the column would move at 0.08 mm/s. Held point-up it retreats at the same speed. Ink as thin as water would arrive at 1660 μL/s.

Watch out. The ink is treated here as a plain liquid of one viscosity in a tube of constant bore, driven by its own weight over its whole length. The narrow channel just behind the ball, surface tension at the open end, and the follower plug of grease that some refills carry are left out. The gas-pressure line uses the same ink; a real pressurised refill uses one many times thicker, so that it does not leak.

A ballpoint left point-up in a pot for a week often will not start. Why, and why does scribbling revive it?

Point-up, gravity draws the ink slowly away from the ball, leaving a gap, and what remained on the ball has dried. Scribbling point-down turns the ball, breaks the dried film and gives gravity time to bring the column back.

Reference: L. J. Bíró · Writing instrument, US Patent 2,390,636 (1945)

3. Ink that thins when sheared

A paste thick enough to stay in the tube would seem too thick to let the ball turn. The gap between ball and socket is about a micrometre, and the ball’s surface crosses it at the speed of writing, so the ink there is sheared at a rate of about a hundred thousand per second. For an ordinary liquid the drag is the viscosity times that rate times the area, and with ten pascal-seconds it would come to more than a newton, more than the hand presses down with. Pen inks are not ordinary liquids. They contain long resin molecules that are tangled when the ink is still and line up along the flow when it is sheared, so the viscosity falls as the shear rate rises. A common description is a power law: viscosity proportional to the shear rate raised to the power \(n-1\), with \(n\) less than one. The same ink is then a paste in the tube and an oil in the gap.

Worked example. Writing at 10 cm/s over a gap of 1 μm gives \(\dot\gamma=0.1/10^{-6}=10^{5}\) per second. With \(K=10\) and \(n=0.6\) the viscosity there is \(10\times(10^{5})^{-0.4}=0.10\) Pa s, against 25 Pa s when barely stirred at 0.1 per second: 250 times thinner. The stress is \(0.10\times10^{5}=10^{4}\) Pa, and over the 1.57 mm² of ball inside the socket the drag is 16 mN. With no thinning it would be 1.6 N.

Watch out. A power law has no limits at either end, where real inks level off, and the ink in the gap is taken to be sheared uniformly over half the surface of the ball. The values of \(K\) and \(n\) are representative, not those of any one product, and heating of the ink in the gap is ignored.

Gel pens use a water-based ink, yet they do not leak like a fountain pen held point-down with its feed removed. What keeps the ink in?

The gel thins far more steeply than ballpoint paste: at rest it is close to a soft solid and will not flow under its own weight, while in the gap under the ball it is sheared so fast that it runs almost like water. The ball starts the flow and stops it.
