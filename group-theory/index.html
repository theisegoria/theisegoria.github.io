Group theory: symmetry as algebra

Compose symmetries, inspect group tables, and count orbits.

Before you begin: Functions and composition

Predict, manipulate, then check your reasoning against the example and question. Graphs illustrate the mathematics; they do not replace a proof.

1. Order matters

Label a regular polygon, then compare a rotation after reflection with reflection after rotation. Labels let us distinguish transformations that leave the outline unchanged.

Worked example. For a square, a quarter-turn and a reflection do not commute.

Watch out. An unchanged outline does not mean every vertex stayed fixed.

When do the two displayed compositions agree?

When 2k is divisible by n, including zero rotation and a half-turn for even n.

Reference: Thomas Judson · Abstract Algebra: Theory and Applications

2. A multiplication table

Explore the cyclic subgroup of rotations. A cell in row a and column b records a rotation by a+b steps modulo n. Highlight a pair and check its inverse.

Worked example. In C₅, 3+4=2 modulo 5, and the inverse of 3 is 2.

Watch out. This rotation subgroup is commutative; the full dihedral group generally is not.

Why does each row contain every group element once?

Adding a fixed element is a bijection, with subtraction as its inverse.

Reference: Thomas Judson · Abstract Algebra: Theory and Applications

3. Orbits, stabilizers, and cosets

Generate rotations by k steps and mark the vertices reachable from vertex zero. Other colors of position are avoided: reachable vertices are filled, others open. The subgroup cosets partition the vertices into equal orbits.

Worked example. For n=6 and k=2, the orbits are {0,2,4} and {1,3,5}.

Watch out. For the full dihedral action on vertices the stabilizer has two elements; for a rotation subgroup it is trivial.

How many cosets does the generated subgroup have?

gcd(n,k), since each coset has n/gcd(n,k) elements.

Reference: Thomas Judson · Abstract Algebra: Theory and Applications
