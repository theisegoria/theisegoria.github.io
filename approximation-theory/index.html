Approximation theory: how well polynomials fit

Every continuous function on an interval can be approximated as closely as we like by polynomials: that is the Weierstrass theorem of 1885. Approximation theory asks the sharper questions. Which polynomial of a given degree is the best, and how do we recognise it? How fast does the error fall as the degree grows, and what property of the function decides the speed? And why does the most obvious method, interpolating at equally spaced points, sometimes get worse the harder we try? The answers are unexpectedly geometric. The best approximation is certified by an error curve that swings evenly between its extremes; convergence is a law of large numbers in disguise; and the fate of interpolation is decided by where the function has singularities in the complex plane, measured against a landscape built from the interpolation points themselves. These ideas sit under every numerical library that evaluates a special function.

1. The best polynomial: equioscillation

Among all polynomials of degree at most \(n\), exactly one makes the largest error \(\lVert f-p\rVert_\infty\) on the interval as small as possible. Chebyshev found how to recognise it: \(p^{*}\) is the best approximation if and only if the error \(f-p^{*}\) reaches its largest size, with alternating signs, at \(n+2\) points or more. The reason is a counting argument. If another polynomial did better, its difference from \(p^{*}\) would have to change sign between each pair of those points, giving \(n+1\) roots to a polynomial of degree \(n\). The Remez algorithm turns this into a method: level the error on \(n+2\) trial points, move the points to where the error is really largest, and repeat.

Worked example. For \(f(x)=|x|\) and degree 2 the best polynomial is \(p^{*}(x)=x^2+\tfrac18\): the error \(|x|-x^2-\tfrac18\) equals \(-\tfrac18\) at \(0\) and \(\pm1\) and \(+\tfrac18\) at \(\pm\tfrac12\), five alternations, so \(E_2=\tfrac18\). The interpolant at the three Chebyshev points has error 0.217, less than twice as large. For \(e^x\) and degree 1 the best line has slope \(\sinh 1\) and \(E_1=0.2788\).

Watch out. Best in the uniform norm is not best in other norms: the least-squares polynomial is different. For an even or odd function the best polynomials of two consecutive degrees coincide. The algorithm works on a fine grid, so the last digits of \(E_n\) are limited by the grid and, for very small errors, by rounding.

How can you tell that a polynomial is the best uniform approximation?

Look at its error curve. If the error reaches its largest absolute value at \(n+2\) points with alternating signs, no polynomial of degree at most \(n\) can do better, and that polynomial is the unique best approximation.

Reference: L. N. Trefethen · Approximation Theory and Approximation Practice, SIAM (2013; extended edition 2019)

2. Bernstein polynomials: Weierstrass by coin tossing

Toss a coin that lands heads with probability \(x\), \(n\) times, and evaluate \(f\) at the fraction of heads. The expected value is a polynomial in \(x\) of degree \(n\), the Bernstein polynomial \(B_nf\). As \(n\) grows the fraction of heads concentrates around \(x\) with spread \(\sqrt{x(1-x)/n}\), so \(B_nf(x)\) tends to \(f(x)\), uniformly, for every continuous \(f\). This is Bernstein’s 1912 proof of the Weierstrass theorem. It also shows the cost of so simple a construction: the error is governed by that spread, and falls only like \(1/n\) for smooth functions and \(1/\sqrt n\) at a corner, however smooth the function is elsewhere.

Worked example. For \(f(x)=|x-\tfrac12|\) at \(x=\tfrac12\), \(B_{100}f(\tfrac12)\) is the mean distance of the fraction of heads from one half, \(\binom{100}{50}/2^{101}=0.0398\), close to \(1/\sqrt{2\pi n}=0.0399\): the error at the corner. Halving it takes four times the degree. For \(\sin 2\pi x\) the largest error is 0.0385 at \(n=100\) and 0.0195 at \(n=200\), halving when the degree doubles.

Watch out. Bernstein polynomials are not interpolants and not best approximations; they trade accuracy for guaranteed, shape-preserving convergence, which is why they are the basis of Bézier curves. The dashed guide lines show slopes only.

Why do Bernstein polynomials converge so slowly?

\(B_nf(x)\) averages \(f\) over a window of width about \(\sqrt{x(1-x)/n}\) around \(x\). Averaging a curved function over such a window shifts it by an amount proportional to the variance, \(x(1-x)/n\), so the error cannot fall faster than \(1/n\) unless \(f\) is linear.

Reference: L. N. Trefethen · Approximation Theory and Approximation Practice, SIAM (2013; extended edition 2019)

3. Runge’s phenomenon and the potential landscape

Interpolating a smooth function at more and more equally spaced points can diverge: the polynomials oscillate ever more wildly near the ends. The explanation lives in the complex plane. The size of the node polynomial \(\prod(z-x_j)\) grows like \(e^{nu(z)}\), where \(u\) is the logarithmic potential of the density of nodes, as if each node carried a charge. The interpolation error at a point \(x\) behaves like \(e^{n(u(x)-u(z_0))}\), where \(z_0\) is the nearest singularity of \(f\). For equally spaced nodes the potential sags in the middle of the interval and is highest at its ends, so a pole near the middle lies lower than the ends and the error there grows. For Chebyshev nodes, which crowd towards the ends like \(1/\sqrt{1-x^2}\), the potential is constant along the interval and every singularity lies above it.

Worked example. Runge’s function \(1/(1+25x^2)\) has poles at \(\pm0.2i\). For equally spaced nodes \(u(0.2i)=-0.706\), below the level \(u(\pm1)=\log2-1=-0.307\), so the error near the ends grows by a factor \(e^{0.399}=1.49\) per degree: it is 1.9 at degree 10, 60 at degree 20 and \(4.9\times10^{8}\) at degree 60. The danger region reaches \(\pm0.5255i\) on the imaginary axis. For Chebyshev nodes the same poles lie on the ellipse with \(\rho=0.2+\sqrt{1.04}=1.220\) and the error falls by that factor per degree: 0.018 at degree 20, \(6.3\times10^{-6}\) at degree 60.

Watch out. The potentials are those of the limiting node densities, so the predicted slopes hold for large \(n\); at small \(n\) the curves wander. Heights are clipped far from the interval. Even where equally spaced interpolation converges in exact arithmetic, rounding errors are amplified by about \(2^n\), which is why the error curve turns upward near degree 50.

Why do Chebyshev points avoid Runge’s phenomenon?

Their density \(1/(\pi\sqrt{1-x^2})\) is the equilibrium distribution of charge on the interval: it makes the logarithmic potential constant there. With no point of the interval higher than another, any singularity off the interval lies above all of it, and the interpolants converge geometrically everywhere on the interval.

Reference: L. N. Trefethen · Approximation Theory and Approximation Practice, SIAM (2013; extended edition 2019)
