Pin-tumbler locks: five pins and a hundred thousand keys

The lock on most front doors is a brass cylinder inside a brass cylinder. The inner one, the plug, would turn freely and draw the bolt, except that a row of spring-loaded pins crosses from one into the other. Each pin is cut in two, and the key is a strip of metal whose notches lift every pin by a different amount, so that all the cuts in the pins line up with the gap between the cylinders at once. The idea is four thousand years old and the modern form dates from the 1860s. Its strength is arithmetic: a handful of pins with a handful of heights gives a very large number of keys. Its weakness is manufacture: no two holes are drilled exactly in line. These experiments cut a key for a lock sawn in half, count the keys, and pick the lock one pin at a time.

1. The lock cut open

The boundary between the plug and the shell is called the shear line. With no key in the lock, springs push each stack of pins down until the upper pin, the driver, sits half in the shell and half in the plug, and the plug cannot turn. A key pushed into the slot lifts each stack by the height of the blade under it. The lower pins, the key pins, are made in different lengths, one length for each depth of cut. With the right key, a deep cut under a long pin and a shallow cut under a short one bring the top of every key pin exactly to the shear line. The driver pins are then wholly in the shell, the key pins wholly in the plug, and nothing crosses the gap. A cut one step too shallow leaves its key pin poking up into the shell; one step too deep lets its driver pin hang down into the plug.

Worked example. This lock is pinned 3 6 2 7 4 and each step is 0.381 mm. The key cut 3 6 2 5 4 fits four of its pins. Under the fourth, the cut is two steps too shallow, so the key pin stands \(2\times0.381=0.76\) mm into the shell and the plug is held. Cutting that position two steps deeper, to 7, frees it.

Watch out. The cylinder is a generic five-pin design drawn to scale with ten depths 0.381 mm apart, the spacing of a common American key. The warding of the keyway, the grooves along the blade that stop the wrong blank entering, is left out, and so is the small clearance that lets a slightly worn key still work.

Why is each stack two pins and not one?

A single pin would always cross the shear line somewhere along its length. Cutting it in two creates one height at which the stack has a gap, and making the lower piece a different length in each hole puts that gap at a different lift for each pin.

Reference: M. Blaze · Rights amplification in master-keyed mechanical locks (IEEE Security and Privacy, 2003)

2. How many keys

Each pin can be set for any one of several depths, independently of the others, so the number of different keys is the number of depths multiplied by itself once for every pin. Adding a pin multiplies the count by the number of depths. Not all of those keys can be made: the cutter leaves a slope between neighbouring cuts, and a very deep cut beside a very shallow one would carve away the shallow one, so makers forbid neighbours that differ by more than a set number of steps. Counting what is left is done one pin at a time, keeping track of how many valid keys end at each depth. Master keying reduces security in a different way. A building owner wants one key to open every door, so a thin wafer is added to some stacks, giving them two joins and so two depths that work. A lock with wafers in \(k\) stacks opens to \(2^{k}\) different keys.

Worked example. Five pins with ten depths give \(10^{5}=100\,000\) combinations. If neighbouring cuts may differ by at most 7 steps, 79 666 of them can be cut. A sixth pin raises that to 753 754. Master three of the five stacks and \(2^{3}=8\) keys open the lock, so the chance that a random key works rises from 1 in 79 666 to about 1 in 9958.

Watch out. Real makers remove further keys: ones with all cuts alike, ones too close to a key already issued, and ones that fail other rules of their own. Tolerances also mean that a key one step off in one position sometimes works in a worn lock, which reduces the number of keys that are safely different.

Which adds more keys to a five-pin, six-depth lock: a sixth pin, or a seventh depth?

The sixth pin. It multiplies \(6^{5}=7776\) by 6, giving 46 656. A seventh depth gives \(7^{5}=16\,807\), a little over twice as many. Length of key is worth more than depth of cut.

Reference: M. Blaze · Rights amplification in master-keyed mechanical locks (IEEE Security and Privacy, 2003)

3. Why locks can be picked

If the five pin holes were drilled perfectly in line, turning the plug gently would press all five driver pins against the edges of their holes at once, and nothing could be learned from outside. They never are. Each hole is a few hundredths of a millimetre ahead of or behind the line, so as the plug starts to turn, one pin is pinched before the others while the rest stay free on their springs. Push the pinched pin upward and, at the moment its join reaches the shear line, it stops resisting: the plug turns a fraction further, the driver pin is left sitting on the ledge of the shell, and a different pin is now the one pinched. Repeat for each pin in turn and the lock is open. The search that would have taken up to a hundred thousand keys takes five pins and at most ten heights each.

Worked example. With a plug of radius 6.35 mm and 20 μm of clearance round each pin, the plug turns \(0.020/6.35=3.1\) milliradians, 0.18°, before the first pin binds. If the next hole is 8 μm further back, the plug turns another \(0.008/6.35\) rad, 0.07°, when the first pin sets. Guessing a five-pin, ten-depth key means up to 100 000 tries; picking means at most \(5\times10=50\) positions.

Watch out. The hole errors are random numbers drawn for each lock; change the lock number to draw another set. Friction, the spring forces and the skill needed to feel a step of a few hundredths of a degree are left out, as are the security pins, shaped like spools or with grooves, that catch at the shear line and give a false click.

Why does making the pin holes more accurately not make a lock unpickable?

Picking needs only that the holes differ at all, so that the pins bind in some order. Better accuracy shrinks the steps in rotation and makes them harder to feel, but as long as any two errors differ, one pin still binds first.
