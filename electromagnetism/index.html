Electromagnetism: fields and induction

Electric fields, magnetic forces, and changing flux.

Before you begin: Vectors and calculus

1. Electric field lines2. Lorentz force3. Faraday induction

Predict, manipulate, then test yourself: every experiment ends with practice questions that are generated fresh at three levels. Graphs illustrate the mathematics; they do not replace a proof.

1. Electric field lines

Two point charges and their field in space. Drag either charge, in the 3D view or in the slice beside it, and change their sizes. Each charge sends out a number of field lines proportional to its charge, evenly in every direction, so the density of the lines measures the strength of the field. The slice through both charges shows the potential and its equipotentials, which the field lines cross at right angles.

Worked example. Field lines leave positive charges and enter negative charges.

Watch out. The point-charge field diverges at the charge location.

Why does the density of field lines in 3D measure the field strength, when a flat drawing cannot?

A charge's lines spread over a sphere of area 4πr², so their density falls as 1/r², exactly like the field. In a flat drawing they spread over a circle and thin out only as 1/r.

Reference: MIT OpenCourseWare · Electricity and Magnetism

2. Lorentz force

A positive charge in a uniform magnetic field B, with an electric field E that can be switched on across it. Change B and the velocity along B to turn the circle into a helix, then add E: the circle drifts sideways at E/B, perpendicular to both fields, whatever the charge.

Worked example. A velocity perpendicular to B bends continuously; parallel velocity is unchanged. With E across B the guiding centre moves at E/B.

Watch out. The nonrelativistic model assumes speeds well below c.

Which component does magnetic force not change?

The component of velocity parallel to the magnetic field.

Reference: MIT OpenCourseWare · Electricity and Magnetism

3. Faraday induction

A small magnet moves along the axis of a wire loop. Its field lines are drawn exactly, and those that pass through the loop are bold, so their number follows the flux. Drag the magnet or press Play to sweep it through, and watch the induced emf and current: largest where the flux changes fastest, and reversing as the magnet passes.

Worked example. Faster flux change produces larger induced emf.

Watch out. The loop is idealized with constant resistance and no self-inductance.

Why is there a minus sign?

Lenz’s law: the induced effect opposes the flux change.

Reference: MIT OpenCourseWare · Electricity and Magnetism
