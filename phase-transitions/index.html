Phase transitions: melting, boiling and latent heat

Heat a pan of water and its temperature climbs steadily until, at 100 °C, it stops. The flame is as hot as before and heat is still pouring in, but the thermometer does not move until the last of the water has gone. Something other than warming is using the energy. Matter comes in phases, solid, liquid and gas, and passing from one to another means rearranging how its molecules hold on to each other, at a price in energy paid at a fixed temperature. Which phase is stable depends on pressure as well as temperature, and the map of that dependence explains pressure cookers, freeze-drying, the weather, and why tea is poor on a mountain.

1. A map of the phases

Fix a temperature and a pressure and a pure substance settles into whichever phase has the lowest free energy there. Plot the answer and the plane divides into regions, one for each phase, separated by lines on which two phases can sit together in balance. The line between liquid and vapour is the boiling curve: it gives the pressure of vapour that the liquid can sustain at each temperature, or equally, the temperature at which the liquid boils under each pressure. For water it runs from the triple point, where it meets the other two lines, up to the critical point, where it ends. Beyond that end, liquid and gas are not distinct. Water is unusual in one respect: its ice-liquid line leans toward lower temperature as pressure rises, because ice takes up more room than the water it melts into.

Worked example. At one atmosphere water boils at 100 °C. At 3000 m the air pressure is 70 kPa and water boils at 90 °C; on the summit of Everest, at 31 kPa, it boils at 70 °C, too cool to cook rice. At twice atmospheric pressure, as in a pressure cooker, it boils at 121 °C. At 20 °C the vapour pressure is only 2.3 kPa: pump a flask of water down to that and it boils at room temperature.

Watch out. The liquid-vapour line is an accurate fit to measurements. The ice lines are simplified: one form of ice, a straight melting line good to a few hundred atmospheres, and a single latent heat of sublimation. At very high pressures ice has more than a dozen other crystal forms, with melting lines that lean the ordinary way. The diagram says what is stable, not how fast it is reached: clean water can be cooled below zero or heated above boiling without changing phase.

On Mars the air pressure at the surface is about 600 Pa. What happens to a block of ice put out in the sun there?

It is just below the triple-point pressure, so liquid water is not stable. As the ice warms it turns straight to vapour without melting, as dry ice does on Earth.

Reference: R. P. Feynman, R. B. Leighton and M. Sands · The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. I, ch. 45: Illustrations of thermodynamics

2. Heat that does not warm

Joseph Black noticed in the 1760s that snow does not all melt the instant the air rises above freezing, as it would if a trifle of heat were enough. Melting needs a great deal of heat, which goes in without any rise in temperature; he called it latent, hidden. For water, melting a kilogram of ice takes 334 kJ, as much as heating the resulting water to 80 °C. Boiling it away takes 2257 kJ, more than five times the heat that brought it from 0 to 100 °C. The energy has not vanished. It is the work of loosening molecules from their places in the crystal, and then of separating them completely against their mutual attraction, and all of it comes back when the vapour condenses and the liquid freezes. While two phases are present together the temperature is pinned.

Worked example. A kilogram of ice at −20 °C needs \(2.09\times20=42\) kJ to reach 0 °C, 334 kJ to melt, \(4.186\times100=419\) kJ to reach 100 °C and 2257 kJ to boil away: 3051 kJ in all, of which 85 per cent is latent. A 2 kW element supplies 2 kJ each second, so the stages take 0.3, 2.8, 3.5 and 18.8 minutes.

Watch out. Heat capacities are taken as constant, though that of water varies by about one per cent and that of ice rather more. Everything is at one atmosphere and no heat is lost. Real heating is uneven: the water at the bottom of a pan can boil while ice still floats on top.

Why does a burn from steam at 100 °C do far more harm than one from water at 100 °C?

Steam condensing on the skin gives up its latent heat, 2257 kJ for each kilogram, before it has cooled at all. The same mass of boiling water cooling to skin temperature gives up only about 270 kJ.

Reference: R. P. Feynman, R. B. Leighton and M. Sands · The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. I, ch. 45: Illustrations of thermodynamics

3. The slope of the boiling curve

The boiling curve and the latent heat are two views of one thing. Clapeyron showed in 1834, by running an imaginary engine on a boiling liquid, that the slope of the curve is the latent heat divided by the temperature and by the change of volume on boiling. Clausius added that a vapour far from its critical point is nearly an ideal gas, whose volume is far larger than the liquid’s, and with that the relation simplifies: the logarithm of the vapour pressure falls in a straight line against one over the absolute temperature, with a slope equal to the molar latent heat over the gas constant. So a set of boiling points measured at different pressures, with no calorimeter at all, yields the latent heat. The exponential is the same one that appears wherever molecules must climb an energy barrier.

Worked example. Water’s vapour pressure is 70.2 kPa at 90 °C and 101.4 kPa at 100 °C. Then \(L_m=8.314\times\ln(101.4/70.2)/(1/363.15-1/373.15)=41.5\) kJ/mol, or 2300 kJ/kg, within 2 per cent of the measured 2257. Between 20 and 30 °C the same sum gives 2450 kJ/kg: latent heat is larger at lower temperature. Dividing the latent heat by the boiling temperature gives 109 J/mol K for water and 111 for ethanol.

Watch out. The straight line assumes an ideal vapour, a liquid of negligible volume and a constant latent heat. All three fail as the critical point is approached, where the latent heat falls to zero. Near one atmosphere the error is a few per cent, mostly because the vapour is slightly denser than an ideal gas. The ethanol curve is an Antoine fit, valid only over the range shown.

Most simple liquids have nearly the same entropy of boiling, about 88 J/mol K (Trouton’s rule). Water and ethanol come out near 110. What does that say about them?

Their liquids are more ordered than usual. Hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in preferred arrangements, so boiling releases them into disorder from a more organised start, and the entropy gained is larger.

Reference: R. P. Feynman, R. B. Leighton and M. Sands · The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. I, ch. 45: Illustrations of thermodynamics
