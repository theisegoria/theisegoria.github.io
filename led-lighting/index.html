LED bulbs and their drivers

An LED bulb is three machines in one shell. The first is a set of small blue light-emitting diodes under a phosphor that turns part of their blue into yellow and red, so that the mixture looks white; they convert electricity to light about ten times better than a glowing filament did. The second is a power supply, because an LED wants a few volts of steady direct current and the wall offers a hundred or more volts that reverse direction a hundred times a second. The third is a heat sink: an LED makes less heat than a filament but, unlike a filament, it must stay cool to survive, and nearly all the engineering of a bulb goes into getting six watts of heat out of a shell the size of an egg. These experiments follow the current into white light, turn mains into steady light, and take the bulb’s temperature.

1. From current to white light

Inside an LED, electrons crossing a junction between two kinds of semiconductor drop into empty states and give up their energy. Three things can happen to each one. It can be captured by a defect and its energy lost as heat, at a rate proportional to the number of carriers \(n\). It can meet a hole and emit a photon, at a rate proportional to \(n^2\). Or three carriers can collide so that one carries the energy away, at a rate proportional to \(n^3\). At low current defects win, at high current the three-body process wins, and in between is a peak; the fall beyond it is called droop. The photons are blue, with an energy near 2.7 electron-volts. A phosphor absorbs some and re-emits yellow and red, each time giving up the difference in energy as heat. Finally the eye is far more sensitive to green than to deep red or blue, so the same watt of light is worth more or fewer lumens depending on its colour.

Worked example. At 100 mA and a junction temperature of 85 °C the chip drops 2.84 V; 87 per cent of electrons make a photon, and 67 per cent of the electrical power leaves as blue light. A warm-white phosphor (3600 K) keeps 73 per cent of that as light worth 311 lumens per watt of light, so the result is 152 lumens per electrical watt, 43 lumens from this one chip. At its most efficient current of 20 mA the same chip gives 165 lm/W, at 1 A only 107. A cool-white phosphor (6200 K) at 100 mA gives 168 lm/W.

Watch out. The three-process model with fixed coefficients is the usual first description of droop; the true cause of droop is still argued over. The spectrum is two or three smooth bands, not a measured one, and colour quality (how well the light renders colours) is not computed: adding red improves it at the cost of lumens. The colour-matching functions are a published analytic fit.

Why is a warm-white LED less efficient than a cool-white one made from the same chip?

Warm white needs more of the blue light converted to red. Each red photon carries about a quarter less energy than the blue photon that made it, and the difference becomes heat. The eye is also less sensitive to red than to green, so the red light counts for fewer lumens.

Reference: U.S. Department of Energy · Solid-State Lighting R&D Plan (2016)

2. The driver: from mains to steady light

The mains is a sine wave that passes through zero a hundred or a hundred and twenty times a second. An LED responds to its current in well under a millisecond, so unless something stores energy across those zeros, the light goes out each time. The simplest driver rectifies the mains with four diodes and stores charge in a capacitor, which is refilled at each peak and drained in between; its voltage sags by an amount set by how much charge the lamp takes in half a cycle. What happens next decides the quality of the light. If the LEDs are fed through a resistor, their current follows the sag, magnified, and the light flickers at twice the mains frequency. If a switching converter regulates the current, the sag is hidden. Either way the capacitor draws from the wall only in brief pulses near each peak, which is a poor way to load the supply and is measured by the power factor.

Worked example. On 230 V at 50 Hz, a 9 W lamp with a 4.5 µF capacitor lets the capacitor fall 66 V from its 325 V peak. Fed through a resistor the light swings by 36 per cent at 100 Hz, and the lamp draws 92 mA in pulses for a power factor of 0.55. A 22 µF capacitor cuts the ripple to 20 V and the flicker to 9 per cent, but the power factor falls to 0.41. With a constant-current converter the flicker is zero for any capacitor above about 1.5 µF. On 120 V at 60 Hz the same 4.5 µF lamp with a resistor flickers by 77 per cent, since at the lower voltage the same power needs twice the current.

Watch out. The diodes are ideal and the converter is treated as a load that draws constant power, with no losses of its own beyond a fixed 10 per cent. Real drivers shape the input current to raise the power factor, some put the storage after the converter, and dimmers add problems of their own. Flicker at 100 Hz is not seen directly by most people but can be noticed on moving objects and by cameras.

Why do some cheap LED bulbs flicker when a filament bulb on the same supply did not?

A filament stays hot through the zero crossings of the mains, so its glow barely changes. An LED follows its current instantly. If the driver has only a small capacitor and a resistor, the LED current dips deeply twice per mains cycle and the light dips with it.

Reference: U.S. Department of Energy · Solid-State Lighting R&D Plan (2016)

3. Inside the bulb: heat and life

Roughly a third of the power going into a white LED leaves as light. The rest is heat, and unlike a filament, which radiates its heat away at 2500 °C, an LED must shed it by conduction while staying below about 100 °C. Heat flows from the junction through the chip’s package into an aluminium-cored circuit board, from the board into a metal body, and from the body into the room air. Each step behaves like a resistor: the temperature rise across it is the heat flow times its thermal resistance. The last step, from a small surface into still air, is by far the largest. Temperature matters because the processes that dim an LED and dry out the electrolytic capacitor in its driver both speed up exponentially with it, roughly doubling for every ten degrees.

Worked example. A 9 W bulb makes 5.9 W of heat. With 12 K/W from heat sink to air and 1.8 K/W inside, in a room at 25 °C the heat sink is at 95 °C and the junction at 106 °C; the light is 84 per cent of its cold value. The LEDs fade to 70 per cent after about 15 000 hours and the capacitor, at 90 °C, lasts about 14 000: thirteen years at three hours a day. In a closed fitting where the air reaches 50 °C the junction is at 131 °C and the capacitor fails after about 2500 hours.

Watch out. The bulb is a generic A60 lamp with representative dimensions, not a particular product, and its glow in the scene only indicates power. The thermal resistances and the life laws are typical round figures; makers rate life from standard tests at fixed temperatures, and a real bulb often fails at a solder joint or a single component well before the figures here.

Why does an LED bulb last a much shorter time in a closed light fitting?

The bulb gets rid of its heat by warming the air round it. In a closed fitting that air cannot escape and heats up by tens of degrees, and every part of the bulb sits that much hotter. Since wear roughly doubles for each ten degrees, a rise of 25 degrees cuts life to about a sixth.

Reference: U.S. Department of Energy · Solid-State Lighting R&D Plan (2016)
