Escalators: a belt that folds into stairs

An escalator is a conveyor belt with one extra trick. The belt is a chain of separate steps, and as the chain climbs the slope each step rises above the one behind while its tread stays perfectly level; at the top and bottom the steps sink back into line and slide away under the floor as a flat surface. Nothing in a step senses the slope or adjusts itself. The levelling is done by geometry alone: every step runs on two tracks, one for each pair of its wheels, and the two tracks are deliberately not parallel. These experiments run the loop of steps round, lay the two tracks side by side to see why the treads stay flat, and count the people an escalator moves and the power it takes.

1. The loop of steps

The steps are bolted at equal intervals, 400 mm, to a pair of endless chains, one on each side. A motor in the upper landing turns two toothed wheels that pull the chains up the slope; the chains then pass round those wheels, run back down underneath carrying the steps upside down, and turn round a second pair of wheels at the bottom. So the steps are always the same distance apart along the chain. On the level that distance is all tread, and the steps form a flat floor. On a slope the same 400 mm is split into a horizontal part and a vertical part: the vertical part is the riser, the height of each stair, and the horizontal part is the tread left showing, the rest being hidden under the step in front. Between level and slope the chain follows a gentle arc, so that the stairs appear and disappear gradually under the passengers’ feet.

Worked example. With steps every 400 mm on a slope of 30°, the riser is \(400\times\sin30^\circ=200\) mm and the tread showing is \(400\times\cos30^\circ=346\) mm. At 35° the riser is 229 mm. For a rise of 3 m with arcs of radius 1.5 m, the straight part of the slope is \((3-2\times1.5\times0.134)/0.5=5.2\) m and the passenger run is 11.4 m. The whole loop is 25.1 m and holds 63 steps, of which 28 are in use at once.

Watch out. The escalator is a short generic one rising 3 m, drawn to scale with the near skirt and the truss left off. The chain turns round plain wheels of 375 mm radius at each end, where a real machine uses toothed sprockets and a curved return guide, and the steps on the return run are shown simply following the chain. The cleats on the treads and the combs they mesh with are not drawn.

Why do the steps flatten out for a metre or so before the comb, instead of meeting it on the slope?

So that passengers step on and off a surface moving horizontally with no risers appearing under them, and so that the grooved treads can slide straight under the teeth of the comb. A step still rising or falling at the comb would open a gap that could catch a shoe.

Reference: J. W. Reno · Endless conveyer or elevator, US Patent 470,918 (1892)

2. Two tracks keep the treads level

A step hung from the chain by one axle would be free to swing, and one fixed rigidly to the chain would tilt with the slope. So each step has a second pair of wheels, behind and below the first, and they run on a track of their own. Think of the step as a rigid body that must move without rotating. Then every point of it traces the same curve, merely displaced, and in particular the trailing wheels must follow a path that is the chain’s path shifted bodily backward and downward by the distance between the two axles. That is what the second track is. On the level the two tracks lie one above the other. On the slope, the backward shift carries the lower track up toward the upper one, and the gap between them, measured square to the slope, shrinks. Where the tracks are closest the steps stand as stairs, and where they are furthest apart the steps lie flat.

Worked example. With the trailing wheels 200 mm behind and 200 mm below the chain wheels, the tracks are 200 mm apart on the level and \(200\cos30^\circ-200\sin30^\circ=73\) mm apart on a 30° slope. They would meet at \(\arctan(200/200)=45^\circ\). Move the wheels to 250 mm behind and 140 mm below and the tracks cross at 29.2°, just short of the slope.

Watch out. The step is a rigid body and the wheels are points on their tracks. In a real escalator the two pairs of wheels are set at different widths, so the tracks can pass beside one another, and upthrust rails above the wheels stop a step lifting. The same idea, with the track offsets chosen differently, makes the flat pallets of a moving walkway.

If the trailing-wheel track were made exactly parallel to the chain track all the way, what would the steps do on the slope?

They would tilt with the slope, each tread at 30° to the horizontal, because the step would rotate by exactly as much as its two tracks turn. Level treads need the gap between the tracks to change as the slope changes.

Reference: J. W. Reno · Endless conveyer or elevator, US Patent 470,918 (1892)

3. How many people, how much power

A step arrives every 400 mm of travel, so at half a metre a second one and a quarter steps pass each second. Multiply by the number of people a step holds and by the seconds in an hour and that is the most an escalator can carry; in practice people leave gaps and about half the places are used. The power has two parts. One is the cost of keeping the machine turning: the friction of chains, wheels and handrails, which is there whether or not anyone is riding. The other is the useful work, the weight of the passengers times the height they are raised, each second. A descending escalator receives that work back from its passengers and must absorb it. Because the first part does not depend on traffic, an escalator is very economical per person when busy and wasteful when empty.

Worked example. At 0.5 m/s with steps wide enough for two, the limit is \(0.5/0.4\times2\times3600=9000\) people an hour; with half the places taken, 4500. Raising them 6 m at 75 kg each takes \(1.25\times75\times9.81\times6/0.8=6.9\) kW on top of 2.4 kW of idling, which is 2.1 Wh a passenger. The ride lasts \(6/(0.5\times0.5)=24\) s, so 30 people are on it at once. A thirteen-person lift making a trip every 40 s carries 1170 an hour, so the escalator does the work of nearly four.

Watch out. The idling power is a rough rule, 0.6 kW plus 0.3 kW for each metre of rise, and the drive is given a flat efficiency of 80 per cent. Passengers weigh 75 kg and stand still. The slope is taken as 30° for the ride time. Nobody walks; where one side is left clear for walkers, measured capacity is in fact lower, because walkers space themselves further apart.

A busy down escalator can return electricity to the building. Where does that energy come from?

From the passengers’ loss of height. Each one loses potential energy equal to their weight times the rise, and once that exceeds the friction of the machine, the steps drive the motor instead of the reverse and it runs as a generator.

Reference: J. W. Reno · Endless conveyer or elevator, US Patent 470,918 (1892)
