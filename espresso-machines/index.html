Espresso machines: nine bar through a centimetre of coffee

Espresso is made by forcing hot water through a tightly packed bed of finely ground coffee in about half a minute. The bed is so fine that water will not pass through it under its own weight; it takes around nine times the pressure of the air, and the whole of that pressure is used up across a layer of coffee a centimetre deep. Three things then decide the drink: how fast the water gets through, which depends on the square of the grain size; how quickly the grains give up what is soluble in them; and how the pump, which weakens as it is asked to push harder, comes to terms with the resistance of the coffee. These experiments set the grind, open the machine from tank to cup, and put the pump, the puck and the relief valve on one diagram.

1. Flow through the puck

Water threading its way between packed grains moves slowly enough for viscosity to rule, and the flow then follows Darcy’s law: it is proportional to the pressure difference and to the area of the bed, and inversely proportional to its depth and to the viscosity of the water. Everything about the bed itself is gathered into one number, the permeability \(k\), which goes as the square of the size of the gaps and so as the square of the grain size. That is why the grinder is the most sensitive control on the machine. While the water passes, soluble material diffuses out of the grains. The fraction extracted rises quickly at first and then levels off, and smaller grains are emptied sooner. A shot is judged by how much of the dry coffee ends up dissolved, with 18 to 22 per cent the usual target.

Worked example. An 18 g dose in a 58 mm basket makes a puck 11.0 mm deep. With 300 μm grains, 9 bar and water at 93 °C the flow is 1.51 mL/s, so a 36 g shot takes 24 s and extracts 19.8 per cent of the coffee, giving a drink that is 9.9 per cent dissolved solids. Grind to 250 μm and the same shot takes 34 s and extracts 26.8 per cent; at 350 μm it takes 17.5 s and extracts 13.3 per cent.

Watch out. The permeability is fitted to a normal shot, not predicted from the grain size: a real grind is a mixture of coarse grains and fine dust, the grains swell when wet, and the fines move, so a formula from grain size alone overestimates the flow many times over. Extraction is a single exponential with a ceiling of 30 per cent. Channelling, where water finds an easy path and bypasses most of the bed, is left out.

A shot runs in 15 seconds and tastes sour. Which way should the grind go, and why does a small change do so much?

Finer. The flow goes as the square of the grain size, so grains 20 per cent smaller cut the flow by about a third and lengthen the shot by half. The finer grains also extract faster, so both effects push the same way.

Reference: M. I. Cameron et al. · Systematically improving espresso: insights from mathematical modeling and experiment (Matter, 2020)

2. The machine from tank to cup

The machine does three jobs in a row. A pump takes cold water from the tank and raises its pressure; in small machines it is a vibratory pump, a piston flicked back and forth by an electromagnet fifty times a second. A boiler or heated block then brings the water to just over 90 °C, hot enough to extract well and not so hot as to scald the coffee. Finally the group head spreads the water through a perforated screen onto the top of the puck, which sits in a metal basket locked underneath. Up to that point the water is all at pump pressure. Below the basket it is at the pressure of the room. So the entire pressure drop falls across the coffee, and the puck must be packed evenly or the water will break through at its weakest place.

Worked example. At 9 bar across 11.0 mm the gradient is 0.82 bar per millimetre. The flow of 1.51 mL/s through a basket of area 26.4 cm² that is one fifth open space moves between the grains at 2.9 mm/s, so each drop is in the coffee for 3.8 s. Heating that flow from 20 to 93 °C takes \(0.00151\times4186\times73=461\) W. The water presses on the puck with \(9\times10^{5}\times0.00264=2380\) N, the weight of 240 kg.

Watch out. The machine is a generic single-boiler design drawn to scale with the case opened; real machines add a three-way valve, thermostats and often a second boiler for steam. The open fraction of the puck is taken as one fifth, and the grind is fixed at 300 μm in this experiment.

Why is the coffee pressed down hard and level with a tamper before the shot?

All nine bar is lost across the puck, so any thin or loose spot has the same pressure across less resistance and takes far more than its share of the flow. Water rushing through one channel over-extracts the coffee there and leaves the rest hardly touched.

Reference: M. I. Cameron et al. · Systematically improving espresso: insights from mathematical modeling and experiment (Matter, 2020)

3. Pump, puck and relief valve

A pump does not supply a fixed pressure. A vibratory pump moves a lot of water against no resistance and less and less as the pressure it works against rises, until at about 15 bar it moves none. The puck behaves the opposite way: the harder it is pushed, the more it passes. Plot both as pressure against flow and the machine must run where the two lines cross, because only there does the pump deliver exactly what the puck accepts. For a normal puck that crossing is well above nine bar. So the machine carries an over-pressure valve, a spring-loaded outlet between pump and boiler that opens at a set pressure and returns water to the tank. With the valve open, the pump sits further along its curve, the puck gets the set pressure, and the difference in flow goes round again.

Worked example. The pump reaches 15 bar at no flow and 10.8 mL/s at no pressure. A normal puck has a resistance of 5.96 bar per mL/s, so the lines cross at \(15\times5.96\times10.8/(15+5.96\times10.8)=12.2\) bar. With the valve set to 9 bar the puck takes 1.51 mL/s, the pump delivers \(10.8\times(1-9/15)=4.32\) mL/s, and 2.81 mL/s goes back to the tank. A coarse 600 μm grind has a quarter of the resistance: the lines cross at 7.8 bar and the valve never opens.

Watch out. The pump curve is a straight line through the two published end points of a common vibratory pump; the real curve bows a little. The puck is taken as a fixed resistance, though in a real shot it falls as the coffee dissolves. Rotary pumps, used in larger machines, hold their flow nearly constant and are regulated by an internal bypass in the same way.

A machine with no over-pressure valve is loaded with a very fine grind. What pressure does the puck see, and what does the shot do?

The resistance is large, so the crossing point slides up the pump curve toward 15 bar while the flow falls toward zero. The shot drips out slowly at a pressure well above nine bar and over-extracts.

Reference: M. I. Cameron et al. · Systematically improving espresso: insights from mathematical modeling and experiment (Matter, 2020)
