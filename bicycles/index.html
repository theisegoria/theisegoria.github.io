Bicycles: balance, gears, shifting and brakes

A bicycle is the most efficient vehicle ever built, and almost every part of it is a piece of engineering that can be understood with first-year mechanics. It stays upright by steering itself into a fall, which is why a riderless bicycle given a push will run on by itself, and why that works only between two particular speeds. Its gears do not make power; they match the narrow range of speeds at which legs work well to the wide range of speeds at which roads are ridden. The derailleur that changes gear is positioned to a fraction of a millimetre, by a cable and a spring or by a small motor. And its brakes are limited less by their own strength than by the height of the rider above the road. These five experiments take the bicycle apart in that order: balance, gearing and power, shifting by cable and by wire, braking, and the rim and hydraulic disc brakes that do it.

1. Balance: why a moving bicycle stays up

Push a riderless bicycle fast enough and it will not fall: knocked sideways, it wobbles and recovers. The mechanism is that a leaning bicycle steers itself towards the lean, which drives the wheels back under the centre of mass. Three things turn the front assembly into a fall. The front wheel is a gyroscope, and rolling it over makes it precess about the steering axis. The tyre touches the road behind the point where the tilted steering axis meets the ground, a distance called the trail, so the road force twists it. And the mass of the fork and handlebars sits ahead of the steering axis and falls into the turn. Linearised, the motion obeys two coupled equations for the lean \(\varphi\) and the steer \(\delta\), and has four modes: a fast castering mode that always dies away, a weave that oscillates, and a slow capsize. Below the weave speed the oscillation grows; above the capsize speed the bicycle slowly spirals over; between them it balances itself.

Worked example. For the benchmark bicycle (wheelbase 1.02 m, trail 0.08 m, steering axis tilted \(18^\circ\), 94 kg with a rigid rider) the weave speed is 4.29 m/s and the capsize speed 6.02 m/s: it is self-stable between 15.5 and 21.7 km/h. At 5 m/s the four eigenvalues are \(-14.08\), \(-0.775\pm4.465i\) and \(-0.323\) per second, so a knock dies away as a 0.71 Hz weave. With the wheels’ spin removed there is no stable speed at all; with zero trail the stable range shrinks to 2.93 to 3.13 m/s.

Watch out. The equations are the benchmark linearised model: small angles, knife-edge wheels that roll without slipping, a rider rigidly attached to the frame and hands off the bars. The numbers are those of the benchmark bicycle, with only trail, tilt and wheel spin inertia changed; the bicycle drawn is an ordinary road bicycle with that trail and tilt. A real rider balances at any speed by steering, and neither gyroscopic effect nor trail is strictly necessary: bicycles with neither have been built that are still self-stable.

Why does a moving bicycle stay upright while a stationary one falls?

When a moving bicycle starts to lean, its front wheel steers towards the lean, and because the bicycle is moving this carries the wheels back underneath the centre of mass. The correction needs forward speed to work: at rest, steering moves nothing sideways, and too slowly the correction overshoots and the wobble grows.

Reference: J. P. Meijaard, J. M. Papadopoulos, A. Ruina and A. L. Schwab · Linearized dynamics equations for the balance and steer of a bicycle: a benchmark and review, Proceedings of the Royal Society A 463 (2007)

2. Gears, cadence and power

The chain carries the motion of the chainring to the sprocket tooth for tooth, so one turn of the cranks turns the rear wheel by the ratio of the tooth counts, and the bicycle advances by that ratio times the wheel’s circumference. Changing gear changes how far each pedal stroke carries the bicycle, and so how hard the pedal must be pushed: power is force times speed, and a gear only trades one for the other. What sets the power needed is the road. Air drag grows with the cube of the speed and dominates on the flat; rolling resistance and the work of climbing grow in proportion to speed and dominate on a hill. A trained rider can hold about 200 W for an hour, best at a cadence of 70 to 100 turns per minute, and the gears exist to keep the legs there.

Worked example. On the 50-tooth ring and 17-tooth sprocket at 90 rpm the bicycle moves \(1.5\times\tfrac{50}{17}\times2.105=9.29\) m/s, 33.4 km/h, which on the flat takes 198 W: 157 W of air drag and 36 W of rolling resistance at the wheel, divided by a drivetrain efficiency of 0.975. Holding 40 km/h takes 320 W. On a 10% climb in the lowest gear, 34/32 at 70 rpm, the speed is 9.4 km/h and the power 223 W, of which 204 W is the work of climbing.

Watch out. The rider is a typical one on the brake hoods: 80 kg with the bicycle, \(C_dA=0.32\ \mathrm{m^2}\), \(C_{rr}=0.005\), still air, constant speed. The cassette is an 11 to 32 with a 50/34 crankset and 700×25 tyres. Real chain efficiency varies with load and with how far the chain runs out of line, and the mean pedal force quoted hides a large variation around each turn.

Do lower gears make climbing a hill take less energy?

No. The work of lifting rider and bicycle up the hill is the same in any gear. A lower gear spreads that work over more pedal turns, so each stroke needs less force and the legs can keep turning at a cadence where they produce power efficiently.

Reference: J. P. Meijaard, J. M. Papadopoulos, A. Ruina and A. L. Schwab · Linearized dynamics equations for the balance and steer of a bicycle: a benchmark and review, Proceedings of the Royal Society A 463 (2007)

3. Shifting: indexed cables and electronic derailleurs

A rear derailleur is a sprung parallelogram that carries the chain sideways from one sprocket to the next. Eleven sprockets sit 3.74 mm apart, and the guide pulley must be centred under the chosen one to within a few tenths of a millimetre or the chain rubs its neighbour. In a mechanical system that position is set by a length of cable: each click of the shifter’s ratchet pulls or releases 2.7 mm of cable, and the linkage turns it into one sprocket spacing of sideways travel. Everything depends on the cable’s length staying true, so a cable that has bedded in, or drags in its housing, shifts every position; the barrel adjuster, a hollow screw that lengthens the housing, takes the slack out. An electronic system removes the cable from the problem. The shifter is a switch, and a geared motor in the derailleur drives the parallelogram to a stored position measured by its own sensor.

Worked example. With 0.5 mm of slack in the cable the pulley sits \(1.385\times0.5=0.69\) mm outboard of every sprocket: the chain ticks against the next smaller sprocket and is slow to climb to a larger one. Lengthening the housing by 0.5 mm with the barrel adjuster removes it. A shifter that pulls 2.5 mm per click moves the pulley 3.46 mm, 0.28 mm short each time, so after ten shifts it is 2.8 mm out, most of a sprocket spacing, and no adjustment can make all eleven gears work.

Watch out. The spacing and cable pull are typical of an 11-speed road group; the quiet band of 0.4 mm and the limit of 1.3 mm are indicative, not a manufacturer’s tolerance. The linkage is drawn schematically and its ratio is taken as constant, whereas real derailleurs vary slightly across their range. Friction is modelled as a fixed lag in the cable. Electronic derailleurs are shown as exact; in practice they are set once with a trim adjustment in steps of a fraction of a millimetre.

Why does a new gear cable need adjusting after a few rides?

The cable strands and the housing ends settle under load, which makes the cable effectively longer. The derailleur’s position is set by cable length, so every gear moves outboard by the slack times the linkage ratio. Turning the barrel adjuster lengthens the housing by the same amount and restores the indexing.

Reference: J. P. Meijaard, J. M. Papadopoulos, A. Ruina and A. L. Schwab · Linearized dynamics equations for the balance and steer of a bicycle: a benchmark and review, Proceedings of the Royal Society A 463 (2007)

4. Braking: load transfer, skidding and going over the bars

A braking force acts where the tyres touch the road, well below the centre of mass of rider and bicycle. The pair of forces, braking at the ground and inertia at the centre of mass, tips the bicycle forward, taking load off the rear wheel and putting it on the front by \(mah/w\). This decides everything about how to stop. The rear brake can only use the grip of a wheel that is being unloaded as it works, so it gives a weak stop however hard it is squeezed. The front brake gains load as it works and can use almost all the grip available, up to the point where the rear wheel carries nothing and the bicycle begins to rotate over the front wheel. On dry tarmac that point comes before the tyre slides.

Worked example. With the centre of mass 1.05 m high and 0.62 m behind the front contact on a 1.02 m wheelbase, the rear wheel lifts at \(a=0.62/1.05=0.59\,g\). On dry tarmac (\(\mu=0.8\)) the front brake reaches that limit and stops from 30 km/h in 6.0 m; the rear brake alone gives \(0.8\times0.62/(1.02+0.84)=0.27\,g\) and takes 13.3 m. In the wet (\(\mu=0.3\)) the front brake skids at \(0.17\,g\) and both brakes together give \(0.30\,g\). Sliding back and down to 0.75 m and 0.95 m raises the dry limit to \(0.79\,g\).

Watch out. The bicycle and rider are one rigid body on a level road, the tyres have a single friction coefficient, and air drag is left out. “Both” means the brakes are shared so that both tyres reach their grip together, which is the best any rider could do. In practice the lifting rear wheel gives a warning, and a skilled rider holds the brake just below it.

Why does the front brake stop a bicycle so much better than the rear?

Braking shifts weight onto the front wheel and off the rear. Friction is proportional to the load on a tyre, so the front tyre can deliver more braking force the harder the bicycle brakes, while the rear tyre can deliver less and soon skids.

Reference: J. P. Meijaard, J. M. Papadopoulos, A. Ruina and A. L. Schwab · Linearized dynamics equations for the balance and steer of a bicycle: a benchmark and review, Proceedings of the Royal Society A 463 (2007)

5. Brake systems: cable rim brakes and hydraulic discs

Every bicycle brake turns the grip of a hand into friction on a surface that turns with the wheel, and the engineering is in how the force is multiplied on the way. A rim brake pulls a steel cable: the lever multiplies the hand’s force about four times, the caliper arms a little more, and rubber pads squeeze the rim, which acts as a brake disc almost as large as the wheel. A hydraulic disc brake pushes fluid: the lever drives a small master piston, and by Pascal’s principle the same pressure acts on two larger pistons in the caliper, multiplying the force by the ratio of their areas. The rotor is far smaller than the rim, so the pads must clamp several times harder to give the same force at the tyre. The price of multiplying force is always movement: the more a brake multiplies, the less its pads travel, and the closer they must run to the braking surface.

Worked example. With 60 N at the lever, the rim brake’s cable carries \(60\times4\times0.85=204\) N, each pad presses with 265 N, and friction (\(\mu=0.7\)) gives \(2\times0.7\times265\times0.311/0.34=340\) N at the tyre. The disc brake’s master piston sees \(60\times7\times0.95=399\) N, a pressure of 51 bar; each 22 mm piston pushes with \(399\times(22/10)^2=1931\) N, and friction (\(\mu=0.4\)) on a 160 mm rotor gives \(2\times0.4\times1931\times0.068/0.34=309\) N. In the rain the rim brake falls to 146 N and the disc to 270 N. Stopping 80 kg from 40 km/h turns 4.9 kJ into heat: enough to warm a 450 g aluminium rim by 12 K, or a 130 g steel rotor by 83 K.

Watch out. Lever ratios, efficiencies and friction coefficients are typical values, not those of a particular product, and real pads change their friction with temperature and wear. The force at the tyre is what the brake could deliver: the road and the rider’s height limit what can be used, as the previous experiment shows. The temperature rises assume all the heat stays in the rim or rotor during one stop. The brake drawn is a generic design, with clearances exaggerated as the caption says.

Why must a disc brake clamp so much harder than a rim brake?

Braking torque is friction force times the radius at which it acts. A rotor’s braking track is about 70 mm from the axle, against about 310 mm for a rim, so for the same torque the friction force, and with it the clamping force, must be four to five times larger. Hydraulics supply that multiplication easily.

Reference: J. P. Meijaard, J. M. Papadopoulos, A. Ruina and A. L. Schwab · Linearized dynamics equations for the balance and steer of a bicycle: a benchmark and review, Proceedings of the Royal Society A 463 (2007)
