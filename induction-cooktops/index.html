Induction cooktops: heating the pan, not the hob

An induction hob has no hot element. Under its glass is a coil of wire carrying a current that reverses tens of thousands of times a second, and the changing magnetic field of that coil drives currents in the metal of whatever pan sits above it. Those currents meet resistance and the pan heats itself. Whether this works depends almost entirely on the pan: in iron the current is crowded into a very thin layer and meets a large resistance, while aluminium and copper conduct so well that the same field makes hardly any heat. The electronics have their own trick, tuning coil and capacitor to ring together and setting the power by how close to that note they drive. These experiments open the hob, compare pan metals, and drive the circuit.

1. The hob and the pan

The coil is a flat spiral, and its field loops up through the glass, through the base of the pan and back down outside. Because the field is changing, Faraday’s law says a voltage appears round any loop it threads, and in a sheet of metal that voltage drives a current round in circles, a mirror image of the current in the coil above which it flows. Coil and pan are the two windings of a transformer, the pan being a single turn shorted on itself. The pan’s resistance is what turns the current into heat, and seen from the coil it looks like a resistor of a few ohms. The heat is made only where the current flows, in a ring under the winding; none is made at the centre. A base that conducts heat well spreads the ring out before it reaches the food.

Worked example. With a winding from 3 to 9 cm radius, the heated ring has an area of \(\pi(0.09^2-0.03^2)=226\) cm². A cast-iron pan looks like 4 Ω to the coil, whose own resistance is 0.08 Ω, so 98 per cent of the power reaches the pan. Its thick base spreads the heat over about 2 cm, and the centre of the pan gets about 15 per cent of the peak. An aluminium pan looks like 0.16 Ω: only 67 per cent would reach it, and the hob refuses it.

Watch out. The hob is a generic single-zone design. The field lines are a sketch, the equivalent resistances are representative round values, and the spreading of heat in the base is a simple smoothing whose width grows with the base’s thickness and conductivity. The food and the water in it take heat away unevenly, which is not modelled.

Why does the glass of an induction hob stay much cooler than the pan?

The heat is produced by currents flowing in the metal of the pan. Glass does not conduct electricity, so the changing magnetic field passes through it without driving any current and makes no heat in it. The glass warms only by contact with the hot pan above.

Reference: O. Lucía, P. Maussion, E. J. Dede and J. M. Burdío · Induction heating technology and its applications (IEEE Transactions on Industrial Electronics, 2014)

2. Why only some pans work

An alternating field does not fill a conductor. The current it induces at the surface makes a field of its own that opposes the change deeper in, so both field and current die away with depth, falling by a factor \(e\) in one skin depth. The skin depth is smaller at higher frequency, in a better conductor and, above all, in a magnetic material, where the metal’s own magnetisation multiplies the opposing field a hundred-fold or more. A thin layer is a narrow path, and a narrow path has a high resistance. For a given current in the coil the same current is induced in any pan, so the heat is proportional to this surface resistance. Iron and magnetic steel give a milliohm or more per square. Aluminium and copper give a twentieth of that, so the hob would need several times the current for the same heat.

Worked example. At 24 kHz, cast iron (\(\rho=1.0\times10^{-7}\) Ω m, \(\mu_r=200\)) has a skin depth of 0.073 mm and a surface resistance of 1.38 mΩ. Aluminium (\(2.8\times10^{-8}\) Ω m, not magnetic) has 0.54 mm and 0.052 mΩ, 4 per cent of the iron value. To put 2 kW into the 226 cm² ring takes a sheet current of 11 kA/m in iron and 59 kA/m in aluminium. In non-magnetic stainless steel the skin depth, 2.8 mm, is more than the 0.8 mm base, and the whole thickness carries current: 0.90 mΩ.

Watch out. Permeability is taken as a single number. In real iron it depends on the strength of the field and falls as the metal saturates, and iron also heats a little through magnetic hysteresis; both are left out. A base thinner than the skin depth is treated simply as a sheet carrying uniform current.

Why does an aluminium pan not heat on an ordinary induction hob?

Aluminium conducts electricity very well and is not magnetic, so the induced current spreads through a thick layer and meets very little resistance. The same current that would heat an iron pan strongly makes only a few per cent as much heat, and the hob’s electronics detect this and shut off.

Reference: O. Lucía, P. Maussion, E. J. Dede and J. M. Burdío · Induction heating technology and its applications (IEEE Transactions on Industrial Electronics, 2014)

3. The resonant drive

To push tens of amperes through the coil at tens of kilohertz without wasting power, the hob puts a capacitor in series with it. A coil and a capacitor exchange energy back and forth at a natural frequency, and at that frequency their opposing voltages cancel, leaving only the resistance of the pan to limit the current. Two transistors connect the pair alternately to the rectified mains and to ground, making a square wave. Driven exactly at resonance the circuit takes the most power; driven higher, the coil’s reactance grows, the current falls, and so does the heat. The control knob therefore sets a frequency. The same circuit detects the pan. With no pan there is almost no resistance, and a brief test pulse rings on for many cycles instead of dying at once.

Worked example. With 80 µH and 0.33 µF the circuit rings at 31.0 kHz, and a 325 V supply gives a fundamental of 146 V. A cast-iron pan of 4 Ω driven at 38 kHz takes 1.5 kW with 19 A in the coil and 30 W lost in the coil; at 60 kHz it takes 0.17 kW. An aluminium pan of 0.16 Ω at 38 kHz takes 0.08 kW while the coil carries 23 A. With no pan the quality factor is about 156, and at 33 kHz the coil current would be 74 A.

Watch out. Only the fundamental of the square wave is counted, the pan is a fixed resistance and the coil’s inductance does not change with the pan; in practice a pan lowers the inductance and shifts the resonance, and the controller tracks it. Component values are typical of a 2 kW zone.

How does an induction hob know that there is no pan on it?

The pan is the resistance in the coil’s resonant circuit. Without it the circuit has almost no damping: a short test pulse makes it ring for a long time and draw a large current near resonance. The controller measures this and does not switch the power on.

Reference: O. Lucía, P. Maussion, E. J. Dede and J. M. Burdío · Induction heating technology and its applications (IEEE Transactions on Industrial Electronics, 2014)
