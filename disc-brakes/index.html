Disc brakes and ABS: turning speed into heat

A brake has one job: to take the energy of a moving car and get rid of it. It does so by rubbing two pads against an iron disc until the energy has become heat, and it has to do that faster than the engine could ever put the energy in. Three separate problems are hidden in that. The driver’s foot must be made a hundred times stronger, which is done with a lever and two pistons of different sizes. The heat must go somewhere, which sets how big and heavy the disc has to be. And the road can only push back so hard: ask for more and the wheel stops turning, the tyre slides, and both grip and steering are lost. These experiments follow the force from pedal to pad, heat a disc with repeated stops, and let an anti-lock controller hold a tyre at its best.

1. From pedal to pad

The pedal is a lever that multiplies the push of the foot about four times, and a vacuum or electric booster multiplies it several times again. That force presses on a small piston, the master cylinder, and raises the pressure of the brake fluid. A liquid passes pressure on unchanged to every part of a closed system, so the same pressure arrives at the caliper, where it acts on a piston with a much larger area and so gives a much larger force. The piston presses one pad against the disc, and because the caliper can slide, the reaction pulls the other pad against the opposite face with the same force. Friction at the two faces, acting at the radius of the pads, gives the braking torque. Each stage multiplies the one before, so braking is proportional to pedal force, until the tyres reach the limit of their grip.

Worked example. A push of 100 N through a pedal ratio of 4 and a booster gain of 4 puts 1600 N on a 22 mm master cylinder, giving \(1600/3.80\times10^{-4}=4.2\) MPa, or 42 bar. On a 54 mm caliper piston that is 9.6 kN on each pad, 96 times the push of the foot. With pads of friction 0.4 at a radius of 0.12 m the torque is \(2\times0.4\times9640\times0.12=925\) N m per front wheel. The car slows at 0.67 \(g\) and stops from 100 km/h in 59 m.

Watch out. The car is a generic 1300 kg hatchback with single-piston sliding calipers, and the front brakes are taken to do a fixed 70 per cent of the work. The booster is a plain multiplier, with no limit where it runs out of assistance, and the small pressure needed to take up clearance is ignored. The brake is drawn to scale, with the travel of the pads enlarged so that it can be seen.

Why does a brake pedal feel soft and travel further when there is air in the line?

Brake fluid hardly compresses, so the volume the foot displaces at the master cylinder arrives at the caliper as movement of the piston. Air does compress: part of the pedal’s travel is spent squeezing the bubble before the pressure rises.

Reference: R. Limpert · Brake Design and Safety, third edition (SAE International, 2011)

2. Where the energy goes

A car at speed carries energy of motion equal to half its mass times the square of its speed, and to stop it all of that must go somewhere. Nearly all goes into the brakes as heat, and most of that into the front pair, because the car pitches forward under braking and the front tyres carry more load. A cast-iron disc is in effect a store of heat: its temperature rises by the energy it receives divided by its mass and its heat capacity. In a hard stop the energy arrives in a few seconds, far faster than it can leave, so the rise is set by the disc’s mass alone. Afterwards the disc cools in the air stream, losing a fixed fraction of its excess temperature in each interval of time. If stops come close together, each begins from a higher temperature than the last. Above about 400 °C the resin that binds the pads starts to break down and their friction falls, which is called fade.

Worked example. At 100 km/h a 1300 kg car carries \(\tfrac12\times1300\times27.8^{2}=502\) kJ. Each front disc takes 35 per cent, 176 kJ, and a 6 kg iron disc with a heat capacity of 460 J/(kg K) warms by \(176\,000/(6\times460)=64\) K. From 200 km/h the rise is 254 K. Ten stops from 100 km/h at 30 s intervals, with the disc cooling on a time constant of 120 s, bring it to 284 °C, on the way to a steady 308 °C. Dumped in 4 s, the 502 kJ is 125 kW.

Watch out. All the energy is taken to reach the discs, with none lost to air drag, tyres or engine braking, and none shared with the pads and caliper. The disc is at one temperature throughout, where in fact the rubbing surfaces run much hotter than the middle during a stop. Cooling is a single exponential; a real disc cools faster the faster the car is moving.

On a long mountain descent drivers are told to use a low gear and not to hold the brakes on. Why?

Going downhill the car’s height is steadily turned into heat. Held brakes receive it continuously with no chance to cool, and their temperature climbs until the pads fade or the fluid boils. A low gear lets the engine absorb much of it instead.

Reference: R. Limpert · Brake Design and Safety, third edition (SAE International, 2011)

3. Anti-lock braking

A braked tyre always slips a little: its tread is stretched as it enters the contact patch, so the rim of the wheel moves more slowly than the road. Slip is the fraction by which the wheel is slower, from 0 when rolling freely to 1 when locked. Grip rises steeply with slip at first, peaks at around a tenth to a fifth, and then falls as more of the contact patch slides. That fall makes hard braking unstable. Push the brake past the peak and the road holds the wheel back less, the wheel slows further, and in a few hundredths of a second it has stopped. Anti-lock braking watches the speed of each wheel. When a wheel slows much faster than the car can, a valve releases brake pressure; when the wheel has spun back up, pressure is restored. Repeated several times a second, this keeps the tyre near its peak, and, more important, keeps it rolling so that it can still steer.

Worked example. On a dry road grip peaks at 1.0 at 12 per cent slip and is 0.69 when locked. From 100 km/h the best possible stop is \(27.8^{2}/(2\times1.0\times9.81)=39\) m; the controller manages 43 m and locked wheels take 57 m, at which point the anti-lock car would already have stopped 14 m back. In the wet the figures are 60 m against 94 m, and on ice 276 m against 346 m.

Watch out. One wheel carrying a quarter of the car stands for all four, with no shift of load to the front and no change in grip with speed or temperature. The tyre curves have the right shape but are representative, not measured. The controller is the simplest possible: two thresholds of slip and fixed rates of release and reapplication. Real systems estimate the car’s speed, which they cannot measure directly, and adapt to the surface.

On loose gravel or deep snow a locked wheel can stop a car in a shorter distance than anti-lock brakes do. How can that be?

A sliding tyre ploughs the loose material into a wedge ahead of itself, and pushing that wedge along slows the car more than the tyre’s own grip would. The curve of grip against slip has no falling part on such surfaces, so keeping the wheel turning gives up that help, though it still keeps the steering.

Reference: R. Limpert · Brake Design and Safety, third edition (SAE International, 2011)
