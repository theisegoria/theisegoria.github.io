Random matrices: order in the eigenvalues of noise

Fill a large symmetric matrix with independent random numbers and compute its eigenvalues. The entries are pure noise, yet the eigenvalues are not: they fill an exact semicircle, they keep their distance from one another as if they repelled, and the largest of them sits at a sharply predictable edge. Eugene Wigner found the semicircle in the 1950s while modeling the energy levels of heavy nuclei, whose Hamiltonians were too complicated to write down; the same laws now describe zeros of the Riemann zeta function, the spacings of buses in Cuernavaca, and the noise floor that every principal component analysis has to beat. These experiments watch the semicircle assemble itself, look at the geometry that makes eigenvalues avoid each other, and find the exact signal strength below which a real pattern in data is invisible to the sample covariance.

Before you begin: Linear algebra (eigenvalues of symmetric matrices), probability (variance, the central limit theorem)

1. The semicircle law

Take an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\) of independent entries with mean 0 and variance 1, and symmetrise it, \(H=(A+A^{\mathsf T})/\sqrt{2n}\). Its \(n\) real eigenvalues are random, but their histogram is not: as \(n\) grows it converges to Wigner’s semicircle on \([-2,2]\). The proof by moments shows why. The \(k\)th moment of the histogram is \(\frac1n\operatorname{tr}H^k\), a sum over closed walks of length \(k\) on the indices, and only the walks that retrace a tree survive the averaging; there are Catalan-many of them. That count does not care whether the entries are Gaussian or coin flips, which is universality. Add matrices and watch the histogram fill the curve.

Worked example. For \(n=100\) Gaussian entries the expected second moment is exactly \(1+1/n=1.01\), and the fourth tends to the Catalan number 2. The fraction of eigenvalues in \([-1,1]\) is \(\int_{-1}^1\rho_{sc}=\frac13+\frac{\sqrt3}{2\pi}=0.609\), far more than the \(1/2\) a uniform spread on \([-2,2]\) would give. The largest eigenvalue of a \(100\times100\) matrix is typically within about \(n^{-2/3}=0.046\) of 2, which is the Tracy–Widom scale.

Watch out. The semicircle needs entries of finite variance scaled by \(1/\sqrt n\). With infinite variance, the third choice, a few huge entries dominate and the limit is a different, heavy-tailed law. The histogram converges for each large matrix almost surely, not just on average; the animation adds matrices only to make the bars smooth.

Why are the even moments of the semicircle Catalan numbers?

The moment \(\frac1n\mathbb E\operatorname{tr}H^{2m}\) sums \(\mathbb E\,H_{i_1i_2}H_{i_2i_3}\cdots H_{i_{2m}i_1}\) over closed walks. Each entry has mean zero, so every edge must be used at least twice, and to leading order in \(n\) each exactly twice, along a tree with \(m\) edges walked out and back. Such walks correspond to non-crossing pairings of \(2m\) points, which number \(C_m\).

Reference: Alan Edelman and N. Raj Rao · Random matrix theory, Acta Numerica 14, 233–297 (2005)

2. Level repulsion: why eigenvalues keep apart

Independent random points cluster and leave gaps: their spacings follow the Poisson law \(e^{-s}\), whose most likely spacing is zero. Eigenvalues of random symmetric matrices almost never come close. The reason is geometric and is visible already in \(2\times2\): the traceless real symmetric matrices \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\b&-a\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) have eigenvalues \(\pm\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), a double cone whose tip is the only degenerate matrix. A degeneracy costs two conditions, so the chance of a gap below \(s\) shrinks like the area of a disc, and the density like \(s\). Wigner’s surmise turns this into a formula that fits large matrices remarkably well.

Worked example. For the matrix with \(a=0.6,\ b=0.4\) the eigenvalues are \(\pm0.721\), a gap of \(1.442\); the mean gap of the \(2\times2\) ensemble is \(\sqrt\pi=1.772\), so \(s=0.814\). A gap at least this small has probability \(1-e^{-\pi(0.814)^2/4}=0.405\) for real symmetric matrices and \(1-e^{-0.814}=0.557\) for independent levels. Near zero the difference is dramatic: \(\Pr(S<0.1)\) is \(0.0078\) with repulsion and \(0.095\) without, twelve times larger.

Watch out. The surmise is exact for \(2\times2\) matrices and only an excellent approximation for large ones; the exact large-\(n\) law (Gaudin) differs from it by under one percent. Spacings of large matrices are measured after unfolding, rescaling by the local semicircle density so the mean spacing is 1. The 3D cone shows only the real symmetric case; the Hermitian case needs a third real parameter.

Why does a complex Hermitian matrix repel its levels quadratically?

Its traceless \(2\times2\) part is \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b_1+ib_2\\b_1-ib_2&-a\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) with eigenvalues \(\pm\sqrt{a^2+b_1^2+b_2^2}\). A degeneracy now needs three conditions, so the matrices with gap below \(s\) fill a ball whose volume grows as \(s^3\), and the density of gaps grows as \(s^2\).

Reference: Alan Edelman and N. Raj Rao · Random matrix theory, Acta Numerica 14, 233–297 (2005)

3. Marchenko–Pastur and the hidden signal

Collect \(n\) samples of \(p\) variables that are pure noise of variance 1. The true covariance is the identity, yet the sample covariance \(S=XX^{\mathsf T}/n\) has eigenvalues spread over a whole interval, the Marchenko–Pastur law, wider the closer \(\gamma=p/n\) is to 1. Now hide one real direction of variance \(1+\theta\). For a weak signal the top eigenvalue does not move: the signal is lost in the noise. At \(\theta=\sqrt\gamma\) a phase transition occurs (Baik, Ben Arous and Péché, 2005), and above it an outlier separates from the bulk at an exactly predictable place, biased above the truth.

Worked example. With \(p=100\) variables and \(n=400\) samples, \(\gamma=0.25\) and the noise eigenvalues spread from \(0.25\) to \(2.25\), although every true variance is 1. A signal of variance \(1+\theta=2.5\) is well above the threshold \(\theta=\sqrt{0.25}=0.5\), and its eigenvalue appears at \(2.5\times(1+0.25/1.5)=2.917\), not 2.5; its eigenvector overlaps the true direction with \(|\langle\hat v,e_1\rangle|^2=0.762\). A signal of variance 1.4 (\(\theta=0.4\)) leaves no trace in the spectrum at all.

Watch out. The formulas are limits as \(p,n\to\infty\) with \(p/n\to\gamma\); at the sizes used here the edge and the transition are visibly blurred. Data are Gaussian; the bulk law holds for any entries of finite variance, while the outlier location also needs some regularity. For \(\gamma>1\) the matrix has \(p-n\) zero eigenvalues in addition to the bulk.

Why is it a mistake to treat every sample eigenvalue above 1 as signal?

Pure noise of variance 1 already produces sample eigenvalues up to \((1+\sqrt\gamma)^2\), for example 2.25 when \(p/n=1/4\). Only an eigenvalue clearly beyond that edge indicates structure, and even then its size overstates the true variance, which can be recovered by inverting \(\lambda=(1+\theta)(1+\gamma/\theta)\).

Reference: Alan Edelman and N. Raj Rao · Random matrix theory, Acta Numerica 14, 233–297 (2005)
