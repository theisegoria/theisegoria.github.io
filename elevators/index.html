Elevators: the counterweight and the safety brake

A lift looks like a box hauled up by a rope, and for most of history that is what it was. Two ideas turned it into something a city could be built round. The first is balance: hang a weight on the other end of the rope and the motor no longer lifts the car, only the difference between car and weight, and the rope no longer winds onto anything but simply rides over a wheel, held by friction alone. The second is that a falling car should stop itself. In 1854 Elisha Otis stood on a platform high above a crowd and had the rope cut; a spring drove pawls into the guide rails and the platform stayed where it was. These experiments run a car and its counterweight, test the grip of rope on wheel, and let a car fall until its wedges catch.

1. Car and counterweight

Raising a load takes work equal to its weight times the height. A car alone weighs more than the people it carries, so a lift that wound its rope onto a drum would spend most of its effort raising and lowering its own cabin. Hang a counterweight on the other end of the rope and the two dead weights cancel. The motor then supplies only the difference in weight between the two sides, and when the heavier side is going down it supplies nothing: the load drives the motor, which acts as a generator. The counterweight is made equal to the car plus a little under half its rated load, so that the difference is no greater with the car empty than with it full. A traction machine also needs no drum. The ropes pass over a grooved wheel and are gripped by friction, so the same machine serves a building of any height.

Worked example. A 1200 kg car rated for 1000 kg has a counterweight of \(1200+0.45\times1000=1650\) kg. With thirteen people, 975 kg, the car side is 525 kg heavier, and raising it at 1.6 m/s takes \(525\times9.81\times1.6/0.85=9.7\) kW. Empty, the counterweight is 450 kg heavier and going up returns 6.0 kW. Wound on a drum, the full car would need 40 kW and the empty one 22 kW. Climbing 9.6 m with a full car takes 16 Wh.

Watch out. The lift is a generic thirteen-person traction machine drawn to scale in a four-storey shaft, with a single wrap of rope and the machine directly above. The weight of the ropes themselves, which matters in tall buildings and is balanced by chains hung under the car, is ignored, as are the energy of speeding up and slowing down and the friction in the guides.

A lift is carrying one person upward. Is its motor working or generating?

Generating. One person is far less than the 450 kg that would balance the counterweight, so the counterweight side is the heavier and is going down. It pulls the car up and turns the motor, which has to hold it back.

Reference: E. G. Otis · Improvement in hoisting apparatus, US Patent 31,128 (1861)

2. Why the ropes do not slip

Nothing fastens the ropes to the wheel that drives them. They stay put because a rope wrapped round a curved surface grips it far more strongly than its friction coefficient would suggest. Each short length of rope is pressed into the wheel by the tension in it, so it can hold a slightly larger tension on one side than the other; the next length adds a little more in proportion, and the ratio compounds round the wrap. The result is that the largest ratio of pulls that can be held is \(e\) raised to the friction coefficient times the angle of wrap. Cutting the groove as a V makes the rope wedge between its flanks, so the pressure on it, and the friction, is greater than the rope’s tension alone would give. The lift must be designed so that the ratio between the heavy and the light side stays inside this limit when the car is empty, when it is overloaded, and while it is speeding up or stopping.

Worked example. Steel rope on cast iron has \(\mu\approx0.1\). In a 40° V groove the effective value is \(0.1/\sin20^\circ=0.29\), and over half a turn the limit is \(e^{0.29\times\pi}=2.5\); a round groove would give only \(e^{0.1\pi}=1.37\). With a 1650 kg counterweight, the empty 1200 kg car accelerating at 0.5 m/s² needs \(1650\times10.31/(1200\times9.31)=1.52\), and a car overloaded to 1250 kg needs 1.64. Both are inside 2.5, with a margin of 1.5.

Watch out. The capstan formula assumes a flexible rope and the same friction everywhere. Real grooves are undercut and wear with use, standards prescribe their own friction values, and the weight of rope hanging on each side adds to the tensions. The counterweight is fixed at the car plus 45 per cent of rated load.

If the counterweight comes to rest on its buffer at the bottom of the shaft while the motor keeps turning, what happens to the car?

It stops rising. With the counterweight supported, the rope on that side goes slack, the tension ratio becomes far larger than the wheel can hold, and the wheel turns under the ropes without lifting. The limit on traction keeps the car from being drawn into the top of the shaft.

Reference: E. G. Otis · Improvement in hoisting apparatus, US Patent 31,128 (1861)

3. The safety gear

Lift ropes are several, each able to carry the car alone, and they almost never fail. The safety gear is there for the case in which they all do, or in which the machine lets the car run away. It has two parts. The overspeed governor is a small wheel at the top of the shaft, turned by a light rope fixed to the car. Weights on the wheel swing outward as it spins faster, and at a set speed, a little above the rated speed, they trip a catch that clamps the governor rope. The car carries on moving, so the stationary rope pulls up a lever on the car, and the lever lifts a pair of hardened wedges into the tapering gap between the car frame and each guide rail. Once a wedge touches the rail, friction drags it further in, and the harder the car pulls, the harder the wedges grip. The car is brought to rest in a short slide.

Worked example. With a rated speed of 1.6 m/s and a governor set at 115 per cent, the trip speed is 1.84 m/s. A car in free fall from 1.6 m/s reaches it after \((1.84^{2}-1.6^{2})/(2\times9.81)=4.2\) cm and 24 ms. Slowing at 0.6 \(g\) it slides \(1.84^{2}/(2\times5.88)=29\) cm, for 33 cm in all. Stopping 2200 kg at that rate takes \(2200\times(9.81+5.88)=34.5\) kN of friction, so with \(\mu=0.3\) each of four wedge faces presses with 28.8 kN. A wedge locks itself if its angle is under \(\arctan0.3=16.7^\circ\).

Watch out. The governor is taken to act the instant the trip speed is reached, and the wedges to give a steady deceleration from that moment; real gear takes a few centimetres to bite. The condition for self-locking ignores the rollers behind the wedge, which are there to make the seat nearly frictionless. Buffers in the pit, door locks and the brake on the machine are separate safeguards not shown here.

Why is the safety gear triggered by speed and not by the ropes going slack?

Because a car can run away with its ropes intact: a failed brake or a broken gear in the machine lets it move too fast while still hanging. Measuring speed catches every such case, including broken ropes, with one device that needs no power.

Reference: E. G. Otis · Improvement in hoisting apparatus, US Patent 31,128 (1861)
