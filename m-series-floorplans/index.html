Inside every M-series die

Every M-series chip Apple has announced, drawn as a floorplan you can take apart. Pick a chip from the table, select a block to see what it does, turn on the data paths, or compare two dies side by side. Each chip is marked with how its layout is known, from die photograph to informed estimate.

What the family shows

Memory decides the shape

A memory interface can only sit on the die's edge, and its length grows with bus width. A 128-bit base chip is nearly square. A 512-bit Max needs two long edges lined with PHYs, and the SLC slices follow them, because each slice sits in front of the memory it serves.

The Max was a Pro with more below

For M1 and M2, Apple drew one die and cut it at two heights: the Pro is the top part, and the Max adds a second GPU half, more memory and more SLC underneath. M3 broke the pattern and gave the Max its own die.

The GPU keeps winning floor space

From M1's eight cores to M5 Max's forty with a Neural Accelerator in each, the GPU grows faster than anything else. On a Max die it is by far the largest single structure.

Two ways to exceed one die

UltraFusion joins two identical Max dies at an edge, and the system sees one chip. M5 Pro and M5 Max go further and split one chip into a CPU die and a GPU die. M5 Ultra combines both approaches: four dies.

Sources

Core counts, memory and process come from Apple. Placement comes from the images listed here. For M1, M1 Pro, M2, M2 Pro, M3, M3 Pro and M4 I used die photographs with measured die sizes, and the M4 photograph is fully labelled. For the M3 family I also used High Yield's labelled floorplans. M1 Max and M2 Max are built from their Pro photographs, because that is how Apple built them. M4 Pro, M4 Max, M5, the M5 Pro and Max dies, and M6 have no public die image that I could find, so I arranged them from their nearest relative and marked them that way. Relative block areas are approximate, and no block here was measured by me.
