Symmetry and tilings: the seventeen patterns and the ones that never repeat

A tiled floor, a printed fabric and a crystal all repeat in two independent directions, and their symmetries form a group. The remarkable fact, proved by Fedorov in 1891 and found long before in the tilework of the Alhambra, is that there are exactly seventeen such groups in the plane: every periodic pattern, however ornate, follows one of seventeen rule books for where its motif may be copied. Behind that number is a constraint on rotations: a lattice can be symmetric under quarter-turns and sixth-turns but never under fifth-turns. For decades fivefold order was therefore thought impossible in matter, until Penrose found tilings with fivefold symmetry that never repeat, and Shechtman found crystals that diffract the same way. These experiments let you draw a motif under each of the seventeen groups, test which rotations a lattice can survive in three dimensions, and grow a Penrose tiling to see its forbidden diffraction pattern.

Before you begin: Group theory (groups, generators), linear algebra (rotation and reflection matrices), vectors in the plane

1. The seventeen wallpaper groups

A wallpaper group is a group of isometries of the plane containing two independent translations and no arbitrarily small ones. Each is a lattice together with a finite list of operations \(x\mapsto Ax+t\) taken modulo the lattice: rotations by \(\pi\), \(2\pi/3\), \(\pi/2\) or \(\pi/3\), reflections, and glide reflections, which reflect and then slide along the mirror. Draw a motif with the three gold points and choose a group: the pattern is the motif copied by every operation. Copies by rotations and translations are blue, mirror-image copies orange, so a group without reflections makes a pattern with a handedness. Turn on the symmetry elements to see the mirror lines, the glide lines and the rotation centers marked by their order.

Worked example. In p6m, group 17, one motif becomes 12 copies in each hexagonal cell: 6 by rotations, 6 by reflections. Through every sixfold center pass 6 mirror lines at \(30^\circ\) to each other, and the threefold centers also lie on mirrors. In p4g, group 12, the point group has order 8 like p4m, but the fourfold centers sit off the mirrors, and the mirrors meet only at twofold centers, at right angles. In pg, group 4, no point is fixed by anything: the motif and its mirror image alternate along glide lines, like footprints.

Watch out. The lattice shapes drawn are representative; within each family (oblique, rectangular, centered rectangular, square, hexagonal) any proportions give the same group. If you draw a motif that itself has a symmetry, the pattern can have more symmetry than the group you chose. Glide lines that are also mirrors are drawn as mirrors.

Why do p3m1 and p31m count as different groups, although both have threefold rotations and mirrors?

Because no change of coordinates turns one into the other. In p3m1 every threefold center lies on mirror lines; in p31m the mirrors pass through only one of the three kinds of threefold center, and the other two sit between mirrors. The mirrors of p31m run along the lattice vectors, those of p3m1 across them.

Reference: Doris Schattschneider · The plane symmetry groups: their recognition and notation, American Mathematical Monthly 85, 439–450 (1978)

2. The crystallographic restriction

Turn a lattice about an axis by \(2\pi/n\). For the turn to be a symmetry, every lattice point must land on a lattice point, so in the lattice basis the rotation is a matrix of integers and its trace is an integer. But the trace of a rotation by \(\theta\) is \(2\cos\theta\) in the plane and \(1+2\cos\theta\) in space. That leaves \(2\cos(2\pi/n)\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}\), so \(n\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}\): no lattice, in two dimensions or three, has fivefold, sevenfold or eightfold symmetry. Turn the 3D lattice and watch how many points come home.

Worked example. For \(n=5\) the trace would be \(1+2\cos72^\circ=1.618\), the golden ratio, which is not an integer, so no crystal has a fivefold axis. For \(n=8\) it would be \(1+\sqrt2=2.414\). The simple cubic lattice has fourfold axes along the cube edges, threefold axes along the four body diagonals (turning by \(120^\circ\) about \([111]\) cycles the three edge vectors) and twofold axes along the six face diagonals; the hexagonal lattice adds the sixfold axis.

Watch out. The restriction is about lattices, that is, about periodic order. It does not forbid fivefold symmetry of molecules, of finite clusters or of quasicrystals, which are ordered but not periodic. In dimension four and higher other orders appear: \(n=5\) is allowed in four dimensions, which is how quasicrystals are described as slices of higher-dimensional lattices.

Why is the trace of a lattice symmetry an integer, even though the rotation matrix in Cartesian coordinates has irrational entries?

In the lattice basis each basis vector is sent to a lattice vector, an integer combination of the basis, so the matrix has integer entries. The trace does not depend on the basis, being the sum of the eigenvalues, so the Cartesian matrix has the same integer trace, \(1+2\cos\theta\), even when its individual entries are irrational.

Reference: Doris Schattschneider · The plane symmetry groups: their recognition and notation, American Mathematical Monthly 85, 439–450 (1978)

3. Penrose tilings: order without repetition

Two rhombs with angles \(72^\circ\) and \(36^\circ\), with matching rules on their edges, tile the plane only non-periodically. The tiling can be grown by deflation: split each tile, drawn here as two Robinson triangles, into smaller triangles in the ratio \(\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2\), and rescale. Each thin triangle becomes one thin and one thick, each thick one becomes one thin and two thick, so the counts follow a Fibonacci-like recursion and their ratio tends to the irrational \(\varphi\). Such a tiling has fivefold symmetry that the crystallographic restriction forbids to any lattice, and still its diffraction pattern shows sharp spots: it is ordered, just not periodic.

Worked example. Starting from a wheel of 10 thin triangles, five deflations give 340 thin and 550 thick triangles, a ratio 1.6176 against \(\varphi=1.6180\). The substitution matrix has eigenvalues \(\varphi^2=2.618\) and \(\varphi^{-2}=0.382\), so the number of tiles grows by about 2.618 per step while the tiles shrink by \(\varphi\) in length, \(\varphi^2\) in area, keeping the covered area fixed. Because \(\varphi\) is irrational, no finite block can repeat periodically: the frequencies of the two tiles would then be rational.

Watch out. The tiling grown from one wheel has exact fivefold symmetry about its center; a typical Penrose tiling has only statistical fivefold symmetry. The diffraction is computed from a few hundred vertices, so the spots have a finite width and weak side lobes; for the infinite tiling they are true points, dense in the plane but with only finitely many above any intensity. Matching rules on the edges, not drawn here, are what force non-periodicity on someone tiling by hand.

Why does the diffraction pattern have tenfold symmetry when the tiling has only fivefold symmetry?

Diffraction measures \(|\hat\rho(\mathbf k)|^2\), and for real scatterers \(\hat\rho(-\mathbf k)=\overline{\hat\rho(\mathbf k)}\), so the intensity is always symmetric under \(\mathbf k\mapsto-\mathbf k\), a half-turn. A fivefold symmetry combined with the half-turn gives tenfold symmetry, which is what Shechtman saw in 1982 in an aluminium–manganese alloy.

Reference: Doris Schattschneider · The plane symmetry groups: their recognition and notation, American Mathematical Monthly 85, 439–450 (1978)
