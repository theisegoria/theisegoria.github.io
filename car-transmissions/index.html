Car transmissions: the differential, gears and the CVT

An engine is a poor match for a car. It makes useful power only over a band of speeds, it cannot run slowly enough to start from rest, and it turns one shaft where a car needs to drive two wheels that go round corners at different speeds. Everything between the engine and the road exists to repair that mismatch. The differential splits one shaft into two that can turn at different rates while sharing the torque equally. The gearbox offers a handful of ratios so that the engine can stay in its band from walking pace to motorway speed, and the variable transmission replaces the handful with a continuum. These experiments open a differential on a corner and on ice, lay the gears of a five-speed box against the road, and squeeze the pulleys of a CVT.

1. The differential

In a corner the outer wheel travels round a larger circle than the inner one and must turn faster. If both were fixed to one axle a tyre would have to slip. The differential sits between them. The engine turns a cage, and inside the cage two small bevel gears ride on a pin fixed across it. Each small gear meshes with a larger gear on the end of each half-shaft. When both wheels turn at the same speed the small gears are carried round without turning on their pin and act as a rigid link. When one wheel must go faster, the small gears roll: what they add to one side they take from the other, so the two shaft speeds always average to the speed of the cage. Because the small gears push equally on both sides, each wheel receives the same torque, whatever its speed. That equality is the differential’s one weakness. A wheel on ice can resist very little torque, and the wheel on dry road is given no more.

Worked example. At 30 km/h on 0.31 m wheels the cage turns at 26.9 rad/s, 257 rpm. On a turn of radius 8 m with a track of 1.5 m the wheels need \(26.9\times(1\mp0.094)\): 233 rpm inside and 281 rpm outside, a difference of 19 per cent. With side gears of 16 teeth and small gears of 10, the small gears turn on their pin at \(\tfrac12\times48\times1.6=39\) rpm. With the left wheel on ice it spins at 513 rpm and the right wheel stands still.

Watch out. This is an open differential with straight-cut bevel gears, drawn without its housing or bearings and with the tooth counts simplified. The wheel speeds assume no tyre slip and a turn about a point on the line of the axle. Limited-slip and locking differentials, and the brake-based traction control that most cars now use in their place, are not modelled.

A car is jacked up with the engine off and the gearbox in gear, so the cage cannot turn. One driven wheel is turned forward by hand. What does the other do?

It turns backward at the same speed. With the cage still, the two shaft speeds must add to zero, so the small gears simply reverse the motion from one side to the other.

Reference: H. Naunheimer, B. Bertsche, J. Ryborz and W. Novak · Automotive Transmissions: Fundamentals, Selection, Design and Application (Springer, 2011)

2. Gears: one clutch or two

A gear ratio trades speed for force. If the engine turns \(i\) times for each turn of the wheels, the wheels receive \(i\) times the engine’s torque, less a little lost to friction, and the car moves \(1/i\) as far for each turn of the engine. First gear gives a large push that runs out at low speed, when the engine reaches its limit; top gear gives a small push that lasts to high speed. Plotted against road speed, the gears form a staircase of falling curves, and their envelope approximates the curve of constant power that an ideal transmission would follow. In a manual box the clutch must be opened to change gear, and for that moment the engine pushes nothing. A dual-clutch box puts the odd gears on one shaft and the even gears on another, each with its own clutch. The next gear is engaged in advance on the idle shaft, and the change is made by releasing one clutch while closing the other.

Worked example. With a final drive of 3.9 and wheels of radius 0.31 m, first gear (3.45) turns 200 N m into \(200\times3.45\times3.9\times0.95/0.31=8.2\) kN, more than the 6.9 kN the front tyres can grip, and reaches the red line at 56 km/h. Fifth gear (0.80) gives 1.9 kN and turns the engine at 2670 rpm at 100 km/h, where resistance is 0.45 kN. From rest to 100 km/h takes 7.5 s with half-second gear changes and 7.0 s with none; top speed is 223 km/h.

Watch out. The car is a generic 1300 kg front-drive hatchback with a simple curve for engine torque. Gear losses are a flat 5 per cent, the launch is limited by a fixed grip and by holding the engine at 2500 rpm on the clutch, and the inertia of the engine and wheels is ignored, so the times are a little optimistic.

Why does a car in top gear accelerate so feebly at 50 km/h, when the engine is the same engine?

Top gear multiplies the engine’s torque by the smallest ratio, so the push at the wheels is a quarter of what first gear would give, and at that speed the engine is turning slowly, below the middle of its torque curve. Changing down raises both the ratio and the engine speed.

Reference: H. Naunheimer, B. Bertsche, J. Ryborz and W. Novak · Automotive Transmissions: Fundamentals, Selection, Design and Application (Springer, 2011)

3. The continuously variable transmission

A stepped gearbox can hold the engine at its best speed only for an instant in each gear. A continuously variable transmission removes the steps. In the commonest kind, a steel belt runs between two pulleys, each made of two cones facing one another. Pushing the cones of a pulley together forces the belt outward to a larger radius; letting them apart lets it sink toward the hub. The ratio is the radius on the driven pulley divided by that on the driving pulley, and since the belt has a fixed length, as one radius grows the other must shrink by an amount fixed by geometry. Between the two extremes every ratio exists. The engine can then be held at whichever speed is wanted, most power for acceleration or least fuel for cruising, while the road speed changes underneath it.

Worked example. With the pulleys 160 mm apart and both at 52 mm for a ratio of 1, the belt is \(2\times160+\pi\times104+0=647\) mm long. At the lowest ratio, 2.5, the same belt runs at 29 mm on the driving pulley and 72 mm on the driven one; at the highest, 0.4, the radii are exchanged. The span is \(2.5/0.4=6.25\), against \(3.45/0.80=4.3\) for the five-speed box. The engine makes most power, 109 kW, at 6100 rpm, and held there the car reaches 100 km/h in 7.0 s.

Watch out. The belt length uses the usual approximation for an open belt, good while the radii differ by less than the distance between the shafts. The variator is given a flat efficiency of 88 per cent and changes ratio instantly; a real one loses more at the extremes of its range and takes time to move, and many are programmed to imitate fixed gears because drivers dislike a constant engine note.

Why does the engine of a car with a CVT sound as if it is slipping when the driver floors the accelerator?

The transmission lets the engine jump straight to the speed of most power and stay there, while the car catches up as the ratio changes. Engine speed and road speed are no longer tied together, which is exactly what a slipping clutch sounds like, though nothing is slipping.

Reference: H. Naunheimer, B. Bertsche, J. Ryborz and W. Novak · Automotive Transmissions: Fundamentals, Selection, Design and Application (Springer, 2011)
