Adaptive-cycle engines: one engine, two personalities

A jet engine makes thrust by throwing air backwards faster than it arrived, and there are two ways to do it: a little air very fast, or a lot of air gently. The first gives a great deal of thrust from a small engine; the second wastes less energy in the wake and so burns less fuel. A conventional fighter turbofan has a core stream and one bypass stream and must settle on one compromise for its whole life. The adaptive-cycle engines being developed for sixth-generation fighters add a third, outer stream fed by the front fan stages, and variable geometry (mode valves and variable vanes) that moves air between that stream and the core in flight: high-bypass mode for efficient cruise and loiter, low-bypass mode to push more air through the core for thrust. The third stream is also a heat sink for the aircraft’s electronics and lowers the exhaust temperature. In the United States, GE’s XA100 and Pratt & Whitney’s XA101 were the demonstrators of the Adaptive Engine Transition Program, and GE claimed about 10% more thrust, about 25% better fuel efficiency, about 30% more range and roughly double the thermal management capacity for the XA100; their successors under the Next Generation Adaptive Propulsion programme, GE’s XA102 and Pratt & Whitney’s XA103, both passed Assembly Readiness Review in May 2026, with ground tests later in the decade. Ideal cycle analysis shows why one engine with two personalities is worth the complication.

Before you begin: Thermodynamics (the Brayton cycle), elementary calculus

1. The bypass trade-off

In an ideal turbofan the core compresses its air through the overall pressure ratio \(\pi_c\), burns fuel to the turbine inlet temperature \(T_{t4}\), and lets the turbine take out just the work the compressor and fan need; whatever enthalpy is left becomes the core jet. The fan pushes \(\alpha\) times as much air round the core at the fan pressure ratio \(\pi_f\). The fuel burned per kilogram of core air does not depend on \(\alpha\) or \(\pi_f\) at all, so everything turns on how the same jet energy is shared: one fast jet wastes more kinetic energy in the wake than two slower ones that give the same push. That is why more bypass air lowers the fuel per unit thrust (TSFC) and also the thrust each kilogram of air gives. For a given bypass ratio the best fan makes the two jets equally fast; for a given fan the best bypass ratio leaves the core jet at the average of the fan jet and the flight speed. Move the gold point over the surface and watch both curves.

Worked example. Fly at Mach 0.85 at 11 km (\(T_0=216.65\) K, \(V_0=250.8\) m/s) with \(\pi_c=30\) and \(T_{t4}=1700\) K, so \(\tau_r=1.1445\), \(\tau_\lambda=7.847\), \(\tau_c=2.643\) and \(f=0.02452\). With no bypass the turbojet’s jet leaves at 1211 m/s: 961 N·s/kg of specific thrust, a TSFC of 25.5 mg/(N·s) and a propulsive efficiency of only 34%. A fan of \(\pi_f=3\) with \(\alpha=0.8\) slows the core jet to 1149 m/s and adds a 497 m/s fan jet: 608 N·s/kg and 22.4 mg/(N·s), 12% less fuel per newton. The best bypass ratio for this fan is 7.23, where the core jet has fallen to 374 m/s, exactly \((497+251)/2\): the TSFC halves to 12.9 mg/(N·s), but each kilogram of air gives only 231 N·s/kg. For \(\alpha=5\) the best fan is \(\pi_f=3.85\), with both jets at 545 m/s.

Watch out. Every component is ideal: no compressor, turbine, duct or nozzle losses, complete combustion, fuel mass neglected beside the air, each nozzle expanding exactly to ambient pressure. Real engines therefore burn more fuel per newton than these figures. In the ideal theory the best bypass ratio grows without limit as the fan pressure ratio falls; real fan and duct losses, and the weight and drag of a big nacelle, set a finite optimum. The surface stops where a turbofan would burn more fuel per newton than the plain turbojet with the same core.

Why does an airliner use a bypass ratio near ten while a fighter uses less than one?

An airliner spends its life in cruise, where fuel is the main cost, so it moves a lot of air gently and accepts a big, low-specific-thrust engine. A fighter must fit its engine inside a slim fuselage and still give high thrust for combat and supersonic flight, which needs high specific thrust: fast jets from a small airflow, and so a higher TSFC.

2. The three-stream engine

Here the front fan stages compress all the air to a modest pressure ratio \(\pi_{f3}\). The outer part, \(\alpha_3\) times the core flow, peels off as the third stream; the rest passes a further fan stage to \(\pi_f\) and splits into the inner bypass, \(\alpha_2\) times the core flow, and the core. One turbine drives all three compressions. The mode valve, a ring of variable vanes at the mouth of the third stream, sets \(\alpha_3\) while the inlet airflow stays the same: closed for combat, so most of the air goes through the core where fuel is burned, open for cruise, so a large share leaves as a slow, cool outer jet. On the right the engine’s performance traces a curve between the corners that fixed engines must choose between. The third stream also passes a heat exchanger: with mass flow \(\dot m_3\) it can absorb \(\dot m_3c_p\Delta T\) for an allowed temperature rise, and that heat slightly speeds its jet.

Worked example. Take 100 kg/s of air at Mach 0.9 and 11 km, \(\pi_c=30\), \(\pi_f=3.8\), \(\pi_{f3}=2.2\), \(\alpha_2=0.35\), \(T_{t4}=1800\) K and a 400 kW heat load. In combat mode (\(\alpha_3=0.1\)) the core takes 69.0 kg/s and the engine gives 75.0 kN at 24.5 mg/(N·s); the third stream carries only 6.9 kg/s, so the load heats it by \(400/(6.9\times1.0045)=57.7\) K, more than a 30 K allowance, whose capacity is just 208 kW. In cruise mode (\(\alpha_3=1.2\)) the core takes 39.2 kg/s and the third stream 47.1 kg/s: thrust falls 36% to 48.2 kN, the TSFC falls 12% to 21.7 mg/(N·s), the propulsive efficiency rises from 37.5% to 42.3%, and the same load warms the third stream by only 8.5 K, with 1418 kW of capacity at 30 K.

Watch out. The geometry and the mode schedule are generic and schematic, not those of any real engine, whose figures are not public. Only \(\alpha_3\) changes with mode here; real adaptive engines also reschedule fan pressure ratios, variable vanes in the core and nozzle areas. The cycle is ideal and every point is a design point: an ideal model cannot show the off-design losses a fixed engine suffers when it is throttled back, which is part of what an adaptive engine avoids. Inside the engine the particle speeds are schematic; in the jets they are the computed exhaust speeds.

Why keep the inlet airflow constant when the mode changes?

An inlet is sized for the engine’s full airflow. A fixed engine throttled back for cruise swallows less air than the inlet delivers, and the surplus spills round the inlet lip, which costs drag. An adaptive engine sends that surplus through the third stream instead, where it adds a little thrust, carries away heat and cools the exhaust.

3. The mission: cruise like an airliner, fight like a fighter

On a cruise leg the aircraft gets lighter as it burns fuel, and integrating \(dm/dx=-gSm/(V\,L/D)\) gives Breguet’s range equation: distance is proportional to \(1/S\) and to the logarithm of the mass ratio. A combat leg instead demands a thrust, and an engine that cannot make it cannot fly the mission at all, however frugal it is. Here three engines with the same core and the same 100 kg/s of air fly out, dash at Mach 1.5 for the set time and fly home: the adaptive engine frozen in combat mode (a fixed low-bypass engine), frozen in cruise mode (a fixed high-bypass engine), and the adaptive engine that cruises in one mode and fights in the other. Drag the required dash thrust and press Play to watch the fuel go.

Worked example. A 30-tonne twin-engine aircraft carries 30% of its mass as fuel and cruises at Mach 0.9 and 11 km with \(L/D=9\), so each engine must give \(30000\times9.807/(9\times2)=16.3\) kN. At \(T_{t4}=1800\) K the low-bypass engine cruises at 24.6 mg/(N·s) and can give 61.7 kN in the dash; the high-bypass engine cruises at 21.8 mg/(N·s) but gives only 38.8 kN, short of the 50 kN required. With a 4-minute dash (611 kg of fuel) the low-bypass engine reaches 1647 km; the adaptive engine, cruising at 21.8 and dashing at 61.7 kN, reaches 1858 km, 12.9% farther, or flies the shorter mission on 9.1% less fuel. Its 11.4% lower cruise TSFC explains the radius: \(1/(1-0.114)=1.129\). By the same arithmetic, 25% less fuel per newton would give \(1/0.75=1.33\) times the range, the published pair; the ideal cycle alone supplies a bit under half of it.

Watch out. Each engine’s TSFC at a flight condition is taken from its cycle at the set turbine temperature and held constant when it is throttled back to the cruise thrust, the usual approximation in a first range estimate. The inlet airflow is the same at both flight conditions, \(L/D\) is constant, and take-off, climb, reserves and loiter are left out. The published figures compare a complete new engine with an existing one, so they also include improvements that an ideal cycle cannot contain. Nothing here describes a real aircraft.

Why does 25% less fuel per unit thrust give about a third more range, not a quarter?

Breguet’s equation makes the range inversely proportional to TSFC for the same fuel. Multiplying TSFC by 0.75 multiplies the range by \(1/0.75=1.33\). So “about 25% better fuel efficiency” and “about 30% more range” are two faces of one improvement, rounded, and blurred by parts of a mission, such as a dash, that do not scale with cruise TSFC.
