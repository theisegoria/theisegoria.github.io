Microprocessors: switches, logic and the light that prints them

A modern processor is tens of billions of transistors, each a voltage-controlled switch a few tens of nanometers across, wired into logic gates that flip billions of times a second. The transistor today is a FinFET: a thin silicon fin with the gate wrapped round three of its sides, so that a small change of gate voltage can open or close the channel decisively. Pairs of complementary transistors make CMOS logic, which draws current only while switching, and that is what lets a chip hold billions of gates without melting. The patterns are printed on silicon wafers by projecting light through a mask with lenses of extraordinary precision, layer upon layer, more than eighty times over. These experiments open up a FinFET and its current, build the CMOS inverter at the heart of every logic gate, and follow the light through a lithography scanner to the wafer to see why chipmakers moved to extreme ultraviolet.

1. The FinFET: a switch that wraps the channel

A transistor is a gate electrode separated by a few atoms of insulator from a strip of silicon between a source and a drain. A positive gate voltage pulls electrons into a thin inversion layer at the silicon surface, opening a channel. Below the threshold \(V_T\) a few electrons still diffuse over the barrier, and their number follows the Boltzmann factor, so the current rises by a decade for every \(n(kT/q)\ln10\) of gate voltage: at least 60 mV at room temperature, whatever the transistor is made of. In a planar transistor the drain also tugs on the channel and the switch leaks; wrapping the gate round a tall thin fin gives the gate the upper hand. The EKV model used here covers weak and strong inversion in one formula.

Worked example. With \(n=1.25\) at \(27^\circ\)C the subthreshold swing is \(1.25\times25.9\ \mathrm{mV}\times2.303=74\) mV per decade. At \(V_G=V_D=0.75\) V each fin carries 113 µA; with the gate at 0 V it still leaks 0.42 nA, an on/off ratio of 270 000. At \(85^\circ\)C the swing widens to 89 mV/decade and the leakage grows to 7.8 nA per fin, nearly twenty times more, which is why hot chips waste power. Across ten billion transistors, a nanoamp each is ten amperes.

Watch out. The device parameters are representative of a recent FinFET node, not of a particular process; real transistors add series resistance, velocity saturation and quantum confinement in the fin. The 3D drawing is to scale for the fin and gate dimensions; the electrons are a picture of where the inversion charge sits and how fast it flows (on a logarithmic speed scale), not individual carriers.

Why can a transistor not be switched off more sharply than 60 mV per decade at room temperature?

Below threshold the current is carried by electrons with enough thermal energy to cross the source barrier, a population that follows \(e^{-E/kT}\). The gate can lower the barrier at best one-for-one with its voltage, so the current changes by at most a factor \(e\) per \(kT/q\), that is a decade per \((kT/q)\ln10=60\) mV at 300 K.

Reference: Chris A. Mack · Fundamental Principles of Optical Lithography: The Science of Microfabrication, Wiley (2007)

2. CMOS logic: the inverter and the cost of a switch

Stack an n-type transistor, which conducts when its gate is high, under a p-type transistor, which conducts when its gate is low, and tie their gates together. When the input is low the p-type pulls the output up to \(V_{DD}\); when it is high the n-type pulls it down to zero. One of the pair is always off, so in either state the only current is leakage: this complementary arrangement, CMOS, is why logic can be packed by the billion. Every gate, NAND and NOR included, is a variation on it. The price of a switch is the charge on the wiring and gates it drives, \(CV_{DD}\), dumped once per cycle, so the power is \(\alpha CV_{DD}^2f\). Lowering \(V_{DD}\) saves power quadratically but slows the transistors.

Worked example. With \(V_{DD}=0.75\) V and the p-type transistor twice as wide as the n-type (holes being about half as mobile as electrons), the switching threshold sits at the midpoint, 0.375 V. A chip with switched capacitance 1 nF, activity 0.1 and clock 4 GHz dissipates \(0.1\times10^{-9}\times0.75^2\times4\times10^9=0.225\) W per nanofarad. Dropping to 0.6 V cuts the energy per switch to \((0.6/0.75)^2=64\%\), but each gate becomes about 1.4 times slower.

Watch out. The inverter uses the same compact model for both transistors, with the p-type scaled by its width ratio and a hole mobility half that of electrons; real p-type FinFETs use strained silicon germanium to narrow that gap. The delay curve is the alpha-power law with exponent 1.3, a common empirical fit.

Why does a CMOS gate draw almost no current when it is not switching?

In either steady state one transistor of each complementary pair is off, so there is no conducting path from \(V_{DD}\) to ground; only subthreshold leakage flows. Current flows mainly to charge or discharge the output capacitance during a transition, plus a brief short-circuit current while both transistors are partly on.

Reference: Chris A. Mack · Fundamental Principles of Optical Lithography: The Science of Microfabrication, Wiley (2007)

3. Lithography: printing with light

A scanner shines light through a mask and a lens demagnifies the pattern four times onto a wafer coated with photoresist. A periodic mask diffracts the light into orders leaving at angles \(\sin\theta=m\lambda/p\); the lens, of numerical aperture NA, accepts only those within \(\sin\theta\le\mathrm{NA}\). An image needs at least two orders to interfere, so with on-axis light the finest pitch is \(\lambda/\mathrm{NA}\). Tilting the illumination so that the zeroth order enters at one edge of the pupil lets the first order in at the other, halving the limit to \(\lambda/(2\mathrm{NA})\) for fully off-axis light. Beyond that only a shorter wavelength helps: from 193 nm argon fluoride light, with water between lens and wafer to reach NA 1.35, to 13.5 nm extreme ultraviolet, reflected from mirrors because no glass transmits it.

Worked example. For ArF immersion (\(\lambda=193\) nm, NA 1.35) on-axis light resolves pitches down to 143 nm; a dipole at \(\sigma=0.9\) reaches 75 nm, a half-pitch \(k_1\) of 0.26, near the practical floor of 0.25. At an 80 nm pitch on-axis light passes only the zeroth order and the image is a flat 0.25 of full brightness, while the dipole gives fringes from 0.03 to 0.67. EUV at NA 0.33 reaches 21.5 nm pitch with the same dipole, and high-NA EUV at 0.55 reaches 12.9 nm.

Watch out. The image is computed by Abbe’s method for a one-dimensional grating and a source sampled in one dimension, with an ideal lens and no polarization or resist chemistry; the resist profile is the dose-thresholded image with a small blur. Real processes print below these single-exposure limits by splitting patterns over several exposures and by optical proximity correction of the mask.

Why does an image vanish when only the zeroth diffraction order passes through the lens?

The zeroth order is a plane wave traveling straight down; on its own it gives a uniform intensity \(|c_0|^2\). Fringes come from the interference of two or more orders traveling at different angles, \(|c_0+c_1e^{2\pi ix/p}|^2\), so without a first order there is nothing to modulate the light and no pattern.

Reference: Chris A. Mack · Fundamental Principles of Optical Lithography: The Science of Microfabrication, Wiley (2007)
