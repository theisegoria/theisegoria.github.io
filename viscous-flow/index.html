Viscous flow: pipes, porous beds and capillaries

The fluid dynamics of aircraft and weather is about inertia: fluid that keeps going. In a hypodermic needle, a coffee puck, a sand filter, the ink channel of a pen or the pores of a paper towel, inertia hardly matters. There the fluid moves only as fast as pressure can drag it past the walls against its own stickiness, and stops the moment the pressure does. This is the world of small things and thick liquids, and its laws are simple and severe: flow through a narrow passage falls as the fourth power of its width. At the smallest scales a second effect joins in. The surface of a liquid is under tension, and in a fine enough gap that tension can pull the liquid along with no pump at all.

1. Flow in a pipe

A real fluid sticks to any surface it touches: at the wall of a pipe its speed is exactly zero. Away from the wall each layer slides over the one outside it, and viscosity is the friction between them, a force proportional to how quickly the speed changes from layer to layer. Balance that friction against the pressure pushing each cylinder of fluid along and the speed comes out as a parabola, greatest on the axis. Adding up the flow across the section gives the law found by Hagen and by Poiseuille around 1840: flow is proportional to the pressure difference and inversely to the length and the viscosity, as common sense suggests, but it goes as the fourth power of the radius. Two of the four powers are the area. The other two come from the walls: in a wider pipe the middle is further from them and so moves faster.

Worked example. Water, of viscosity 0.001 Pa s, in a tube 2 mm wide and 1 m long under 1 kPa flows at \(\pi\times1000\times0.001^{4}/(8\times0.001\times1)=3.9\times10^{-7}\) m³/s, or 24 ml a minute. The mean speed is 12.5 cm/s, the speed on the axis 25 cm/s, and the Reynolds number 250, safely laminar. A tube 1 mm wide passes 1.5 ml a minute, a sixteenth as much. Olive oil, 81 times as viscous, flows 81 times more slowly.

Watch out. The law holds for steady, laminar flow of a simple liquid in a long, straight, round pipe. Above a Reynolds number of about 2300 the flow turns turbulent and the pressure needed rises roughly as the square of the flow. Near the entrance the profile has not yet formed. Liquids such as ketchup, blood and ballpoint ink, whose viscosity changes with how fast they are sheared, give a blunter profile.

A plaque narrows an artery to 80 per cent of its original width. If the pressure is unchanged, how much flow is left?

\(0.8^{4}=0.41\): about two fifths. A modest narrowing costs more than half the flow, which is why the body must raise the pressure, or widen other vessels, to compensate.

Reference: R. P. Feynman, R. B. Leighton and M. Sands · The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. II, ch. 41: The flow of wet water

2. Through a bed of grains

Sand, soil, ground coffee, a ceramic filter and sandstone are all solids shot through with connected gaps. Following the water through every gap is hopeless, and unnecessary. In 1856 Henry Darcy, designing the fountains of Dijon, ran water through columns of sand and found that the flow was simply proportional to the pressure drop and inversely to the depth of the bed. Everything about the maze of gaps is gathered into one number, the permeability, which has the units of area and is roughly the cross-section of a typical pore. Treating the gaps as a bundle of crooked pipes and applying Poiseuille’s law to each gives an estimate of it, the Kozeny-Carman formula: permeability goes as the square of the grain size, and depends steeply on the porosity, the share of the bed that is empty.

Worked example. Sand of 0.5 mm packed to a porosity of 0.4 has a permeability of \(0.0005^{2}\times0.4^{3}/(180\times0.6^{2})=2.5\times10^{-10}\) m², or 250 µm². A bed 0.5 m deep under 1 m of water, 9.8 kPa, passes \(2.5\times10^{-10}\times9790/(0.001\times0.5)=4.8\) mm/s over its whole area: 17 m³ an hour through each square metre. Between the grains the water moves at 12 mm/s. Loosening the bed from a porosity of 0.35 to 0.45 triples the flow.

Watch out. Kozeny-Carman is an estimate for clean, rounded grains of one size, good to perhaps a factor of two. Mixed sizes pack tighter, with fines filling the gaps between coarse grains, and the smallest tenth of the grains largely sets the permeability. Darcy’s law itself fails when the flow in the pores is fast enough for inertia to matter, and when the bed moves or swells, as coffee does.

Coffee ground twice as fine is packed into the same basket. Roughly how much longer does the same shot take at the same pressure?

About four times, since permeability goes as the square of the grain size. That steepness is why a small turn of a grinder’s adjustment changes a shot from sour and fast to bitter and slow.

Reference: R. P. Feynman, R. B. Leighton and M. Sands · The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. II, ch. 41: The flow of wet water

3. Capillarity: tension at the surface

Molecules inside a liquid are pulled equally in all directions by their neighbours; those at the surface are pulled only inward and sideways. The surface therefore behaves like a stretched skin, with a tension that is the same everywhere on it. Where a liquid meets a solid it settles at a definite contact angle: small if the liquid is attracted to the solid more than to itself, as water is to clean glass, and more than a right angle if it is not, as water on wax. In a narrow tube that wets, the surface is forced into a cup shape, and its tension, acting all round the rim, pulls upward on the column until the weight hanging from it balances the pull. Laid flat, with no weight to stop it, the liquid keeps going, slowed only by its own viscosity in the ever longer wetted length behind the front.

Worked example. Water, with a surface tension of 0.073 N/m, wetting a clean glass tube of radius 0.5 mm, rises \(2\times0.073/(998\times9.81\times0.0005)=30\) mm. In a pore of radius 10 µm it would rise 1.5 m, drawn by a suction of 14.6 kPa. Laid flat, that pore fills 19 mm in the first second and 190 mm in a hundred. A pore ten times finer pulls ten times harder but fills only 6 mm in a second.

Watch out. A straight round tube with one contact angle. Real pores vary in width, and the front stalls at wide places and jumps at narrow ones. Contact angles depend strongly on how clean the surface is and differ for an advancing and a retreating front. The square-root law leaves out the inertia of the first instants and, in an upright tube, the weight that eventually stops the rise. Trees lift water far higher than the formula’s suction of one atmosphere would suggest, because water in fine pores can sustain tension.

A ballpoint pen stops writing when held upside down for long, but a felt-tip pen does not. What is the difference?

The felt tip is a bundle of fine pores, and capillary suction in pores that fine is worth a column of water many centimetres high, so it holds and feeds ink against gravity. A ballpoint’s reservoir is one wide tube, where the capillary pull is small and gravity decides which way the ink goes.

Reference: R. P. Feynman, R. B. Leighton and M. Sands · The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. II, ch. 41: The flow of wet water
