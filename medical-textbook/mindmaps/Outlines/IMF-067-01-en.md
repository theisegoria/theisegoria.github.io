# Ethics, consent, confidentiality, and medical law

## Orientation and ethical reasoning
### Law sets minimum standards, ethics often asks more
### Four principles
#### Autonomy supports informed self-determination
#### Beneficence promotes welfare
#### Non-maleficence avoids disproportionate harm
#### Justice: fair distribution, equal respect
### Principles conflict and give no mechanical answer
### Clarify facts, options, values, capacity, law
### Least restrictive effective option, documented
### Ethics consultation supports, never replaces

## Informed consent
### Voluntary, informed, capacitous, specific
### A process, not a signature
### Explain purpose, risks, alternatives, no treatment
### Material risk
#### What a reasonable person would find significant
#### What this patient is likely to value
### Plain language, interpreters, teach-back
### Consent can be withdrawn at any time
### Emergency necessity
#### Lacks capacity, no valid refusal, delay harms
#### Not for convenient non-urgent care
### Therapeutic privilege is extremely narrow

## Decision-making capacity
### Decision-specific and time-specific
### Understand, retain, use or weigh, communicate
### Diagnosis or unwise choice does not remove it
### Support capacity first
#### Treat pain, delirium, hypoxia, withdrawal, fear
#### Interpreters, supporters, optimal timing
### When absent
#### Advance directives and appointed decision-makers
#### Substituted judgement: what patient would choose
#### Best interests integrate welfare and wishes
#### Family not automatically legal decision-makers

## Children and adolescents
### Parental authority limited by child's welfare
### Seek assent according to development
### Some adolescents can consent independently
### Confidential care encourages disclosure
#### Explain limits for serious risk and safeguarding
### Harmful parental refusal: escalate, do not abandon

## Confidentiality and privacy
### Enables trust and candid disclosure
### Collect and access only what care needs
### De-identification fails with rare details
### Disclosure without consent
#### Serious harm, protection, statutory duties
#### Minimum necessary, tell patient, document
### Continues after death
### Genetic information has familial implications
#### Patient-led sharing and specialist counselling

## Documentation and boundaries
### Record is a care tool and legal document
### Separate observation, allegation, inference
### Dated, traceable amendments
#### Copying inaccurate text propagates harm
### Boundaries prevent exploitation
#### Power persists after care ends
### Chaperones support but do not replace consent

## Safeguarding and violence
### Abuse, neglect, exploitation, coercive control
### Warning signs never prove abuse alone
### Speak privately with a trained interpreter
### No confrontation, no promise of secrecy
### Document patient's words and injuries
### Intimate-partner violence
#### Escalates during separation or pregnancy
#### Strangulation: delayed injury, few marks
#### Abrupt intervention can increase danger

## Error, candour, and negligence
### Stabilise, preserve evidence, disclose facts
### Apology is compatible with analysis
### Just culture: error, shortcuts, recklessness
### Negligence: duty, breach, causation, harm
#### Poor outcome alone is not negligence
### Abandonment when care ends without transition
### Conscientious objection limits

## Resource allocation and public health
### Transparent, consistent clinical criteria
#### Not social worth, wealth, or stereotypes
### Disability is not lower life value
### Restrictive measures need necessity, proportionality
#### Coercion without support damages trust

## Research ethics
### Validity, risk-benefit, fair selection, review
### Equipoise: genuine expert uncertainty
### Consent distinguishes research from therapy
### Placebo must not cause serious avoidable harm
### Genomics, AI, biobanks add governance concerns
