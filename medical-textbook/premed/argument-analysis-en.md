# R01: Public libraries and the meaning of access

Japanese is a comprehension aid; practice the English passage separately before consulting it. All information needed for the questions is in the passage. The writing extension is supplementary, not an MCAT essay requirement.

## Passage

A town council proposes replacing its small public library with a digital lending service. The councillors describe this as an expansion of access: a resident could borrow a book without travelling, and the collection would no longer be limited by shelf space. Their definition of access is understandable, but incomplete. Making a resource available is not the same as making its use possible.

Consider a resident who owns a telephone but shares a crowded room, or a new reader who can operate an application but cannot easily decide which material answers a question. Neither person is simply disconnected. Both can open the digital door. Yet a quiet place and a conversation with a librarian may determine whether they can do anything useful once through it. The physical library supplies conditions of use, not merely objects to be used.

This is not an argument that screens corrupt reading or that a building is valuable merely because it is old. Digital lending can remove real obstacles, especially for someone unable to travel. Nor must every existing service be preserved indefinitely. The mistake is to treat a gain in one dimension as evidence that nothing has been lost in another. A larger catalogue is not a measurement of the availability of quiet space.

The council should therefore ask what residents are trying to accomplish and what prevents them from doing so. Perhaps a smaller reading room combined with digital lending would meet their needs. Perhaps it would not. That uncertainty is a reason to investigate before closing the building, rather than a reason to assume that closure is harmless. The relevant comparison is between workable arrangements for actual readers, not between an imperfect building and an imagined technology without limitations.

## Questions

1. Which claim best expresses the author's central argument? A: Digital lending should be prohibited. B: Access must include practical conditions of use. C: Existing libraries should never change. D: A larger catalogue necessarily improves reading.
2. What function do the two residents in paragraph two serve? A: They show that everyone lacks internet access. B: They illustrate obstacles that can persist despite digital availability. C: They establish how many residents oppose closure. D: They prove digital lending is more expensive.
3. Which proposal is most consistent with the passage? A: Close immediately because all uncertainty favours innovation. B: Preserve every service regardless of use. C: Compare residents' needs under several realistic arrangements. D: Judge access only by catalogue size.
4. Suppose the council funds quiet study rooms and advice sessions elsewhere before closing the library. What is the strongest passage-based conclusion? A: Every objection to closure disappears. B: This addresses some conditions the author says should be evaluated. C: The author must oppose it because it uses technology. D: The collection size becomes irrelevant to every reader.
5. Why does the author acknowledge that digital lending helps people unable to travel? Explain in two sentences without adding outside facts.
6. Identify one piece of evidence that would help choose between keeping the building and the proposed hybrid arrangement. Explain what it would inform, and what it would not by itself prove.

## Answers and distractor analysis

1. B. The distinction between availability and usable access organises the passage. A and C contradict explicit qualifications; D repeats the single-measure error being criticised.
2. B. The examples distinguish digital connection from effective use. A overgeneralises and contradicts their ability to connect. C invents survey evidence. D introduces costs not supplied by the passage.
3. C. The final paragraph calls for investigation of practical alternatives. A reverses the role of uncertainty. B ignores the permission to change services. D ignores the argument about multiple dimensions.
4. B. The proposal addresses quiet space and guidance. A is too absolute: adequacy and other needs still require evaluation. C contradicts the author's refusal to condemn screens. D falsely treats recognition of one need as denial of all others.
5. The concession recognises a genuine benefit and prevents the argument from becoming a blanket rejection of digital lending. It supports comparing gains and losses rather than declaring one format universally superior.
6. One reasonable answer is evidence about when residents need quiet space, compared with the capacity and accessibility of the proposed reading room. This would inform whether that arrangement meets a particular need, but would not alone establish that every service need is met or that the arrangement is affordable. Other well-justified evidence choices are acceptable.

## Writing extension and self-review

Original prompt: “When a service becomes more convenient for most people, what obligations remain toward those it serves less well?” Write a defensible position, a concrete example, a serious objection and a response that changes or qualifies your position where appropriate. Do not present invented statistics as facts. This is not an official scoring rubric: assess whether the claim is clear, reasons support it, examples are explained, the objection is addressed fairly, and the conclusion follows without exaggeration. Fluency alone does not establish a sound argument.
