Microwave ovens: standing waves in a steel box

A microwave oven heats food with radio waves 12 centimetres long, made by a vacuum tube invented for wartime radar and poured into a closed metal box. Three pieces of physics decide how it behaves. Waves trapped in a box settle into fixed patterns with hot and cold places a few centimetres apart, which is why the plate turns. Water absorbs these waves because its molecules try to turn with the field and cannot quite keep up, and the frequency is chosen not to heat water as fast as possible but to let the waves reach a centimetre or two inside. And the tube that makes the waves, the magnetron, works by bending electrons into circles with a magnet so that they brush past a ring of resonant cavities. These experiments map the pattern in the box, follow the waves into water, and steer an electron.

1. The cavity: hot and cold spots

A wave bouncing between two metal walls adds to its own reflection, and the sum stands still: fixed places where the field is always zero, and between them places where it swings hardest. For this to work the distance between the walls must hold a whole number of half-wavelengths. A box has three pairs of walls, so each standing pattern is labelled by three whole numbers, and it can exist only at the one frequency that fits all three. An oven 33 cm square and 21 cm high has several such patterns within a few tens of megahertz of the magnetron’s 2.45 GHz. Food heats in proportion to the square of the field, so each pattern is a grid of hot spots about six centimetres apart with cold lines between. A turntable carries the food through the grid; each point then receives the average of the pattern round a circle.

Worked example. In a box of \(0.33\times0.33\times0.21\) m the pattern with 3, 3 and 2 half-waves has \(f=\tfrac{c}{2}\sqrt{(3/0.33)^2+(3/0.33)^2+(2/0.21)^2}=2.398\) GHz, and its hot spots are 11 cm apart. The pattern 2, 5, 0 lies at 2.446 GHz, and eight patterns fall within 60 MHz of 2.45 GHz. For the 3, 3 pattern with the plate stopped the coldest point gets nothing; turning, the centre still gets full heat while a ring 5.5 cm out gets under 2 per cent.

Watch out. One pattern is shown at a time, in an empty box. A real oven excites several at once, the food itself shifts and damps them, and heat spreads through the food by conduction, so real heating is more even than any single pattern here. The oven is a generic design with representative dimensions.

Why does the plate in a microwave oven turn?

The waves in the box form a fixed pattern of hot and cold places a few centimetres apart. Food left still would cook in spots and stay cold between them. Turning carries each part through hot and cold places in turn, so it receives something nearer the average.

Reference: M. Vollmer · Physics of the microwave oven (Physics Education, 2004)

2. Why water heats

A water molecule is slightly positive at one end and negative at the other, so an electric field twists it into line. In liquid water each molecule is held by its neighbours and takes about eight millionths of a millionth of a second to turn. A field that reverses much more slowly than that is followed easily, and the water just stores and returns the energy. A field that reverses much faster is not followed at all. In between, the molecules turn but lag, the field does work against the drag, and that work becomes heat. The loss is greatest near 20 GHz. An oven runs at an eighth of that, where water absorbs only moderately, so that the waves are not used up in the first millimetres. How far they reach is the penetration depth, and it grows as the water warms and its molecules turn more freely.

Worked example. For water at 25 °C, \(\varepsilon_s=78.3\), \(\varepsilon_\infty=5.2\) and \(\tau=8.3\) ps. At 2.45 GHz that gives \(\varepsilon^{\prime}=77.1\) and \(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}=9.2\): the wavelength inside the water is \(12.2/\sqrt{77.1}=1.4\) cm and the power falls to 37 per cent in 1.9 cm. At 5 °C the depth is 1.1 cm and at 80 °C, 5.8 cm. At the frequency of greatest loss, 19 GHz, \(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}=36.5\) and the depth is half a millimetre. In ice the depth at 2.45 GHz is nearly 12 m. Heating 250 g of water by 60 K at 800 W, with 90 per cent absorbed, takes 87 s.

Watch out. Pure water with a single relaxation time is a good model up to tens of gigahertz. Food also contains salts, whose moving ions add loss at low frequencies, and fats and sugars that absorb less. The widespread statement that 2.45 GHz is a resonance of the water molecule is wrong: it lies far below the peak and was chosen for depth and for an available radio band.

Why do microwave ovens not use the frequency that heats water fastest?

At that frequency, near 20 GHz, water absorbs so strongly that the waves are used up within about a millimetre of the surface, and the inside of the food would be heated only by slow conduction. At 2.45 GHz absorption is weaker, so the waves deposit their energy through the first few centimetres.

Reference: M. Vollmer · Physics of the microwave oven (Physics Education, 2004)

3. The magnetron

A magnetron is a diode with a magnet. Electrons boil off a hot central cathode and are pulled outwards by several thousand volts towards a surrounding copper anode. A magnetic field along the axis pushes sideways on every moving electron, at right angles to its motion, and bends its path. With a weak field the electron still reaches the anode. Above a critical field, the Hull cut-off, it is turned right round before it gets there and falls back towards the cathode, and the electrons form a cloud that circles in the gap. The anode is not smooth: it carries a ring of cavities, each a tiny resonator tuned to 2.45 GHz. As the cloud sweeps past their mouths it is slowed by the field of the waves in them and gives up its energy to those waves, which an antenna then leads out to the oven.

Worked example. With a cathode of radius 2 mm, an anode of 4.5 mm and 4 kV between them, \(\sqrt{8mV/e}=4.27\times10^{-4}\) T m and \(r_b(1-r_a^2/r_b^2)=3.61\) mm, so the cut-off field is 118 mT. At 110 mT the electron reaches the anode; at 125 mT it turns back 4.25 mm from the axis, and at 160 mT, 3.3 mm. An electron crossing the full 4 kV would reach 12 per cent of the speed of light.

Watch out. One electron is followed through the steady fields of a smooth diode. The working tube depends on the electrons acting together: the cloud bunches into spokes that turn in step with the waves, and that interaction, which decides the power and efficiency, is not modelled. Dimensions are representative of a domestic tube.

Why does a magnetron need a magnet?

Without it electrons would fly straight from cathode to anode and deliver their energy as heat. The magnetic field bends them into paths that circle the cathode, so they pass the mouths of the resonant cavities again and again and can give their energy to the microwaves in the cavities instead.

Reference: M. Vollmer · Physics of the microwave oven (Physics Education, 2004)
