Vibration and resonance: driving, ringing, isolating

Anything with mass that is held in place by something springy will vibrate, and nearly everything is. A car body sits on springs; a washing machine’s drum hangs on them; a guitar string, a quartz crystal, a bridge deck and a building are each mass and spring in one. All of them answer a disturbance in the same way, described by two numbers: the frequency at which the thing prefers to move, and how quickly its motion dies away. From those two follow the tall, narrow peak of resonance, the ring of a struck bell, and the odd fact that the way to stop a machine shaking the floor is to mount it on something soft. These experiments take the three in turn.

Before you begin: Newton’s second law, sines and cosines, the exponential function

1. Pushing in time

Push a child on a swing at random and little happens. Push once per swing, however gently, and the motion grows until friction wastes as much energy per swing as the push supplies. That is resonance. For a mass on a spring with some damping, pushed by a force that goes back and forth at a steady rate, the outcome depends on the ratio of that rate to the natural frequency. Far below, the spring simply holds the mass against the force. Far above, the force changes direction before the heavy mass has gone anywhere. In between the spring force and the inertia cancel, and only damping stands against the push. The height of the peak is the quality factor Q, one over twice the damping ratio, and its width as a fraction of the natural frequency is one over Q. A system that rings long responds strongly but only to a narrow band, which is why a quartz crystal keeps time and a radio selects one station.

Worked example. With a damping ratio of 0.1, driving at the natural frequency gives a swing \(1/(2\times0.1)=5\) times the slow deflection, a quarter of a cycle behind the force. The true peak is a shade lower in frequency, at 0.990, and a shade higher, 5.03. At half the natural frequency the swing is 1.32 times the slow deflection and lags by only 8°; at twice, it is 0.33 times and nearly opposite.

Watch out. This is the steady response, reached after the starting transient has died away, which takes about Q cycles. It assumes a linear spring and damping proportional to speed. Real springs stiffen or soften at large swings, which bends the peak over and can make the response jump.

Soldiers are told to break step when crossing a footbridge. What are they avoiding?

Marching in step applies a small push at a steady rate. If that rate is close to a natural frequency of the bridge, a lightly damped structure can build up a swing many times what the same footfalls would cause at random.

Reference: R. P. Feynman, R. B. Leighton and M. Sands · The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. I, ch. 23: Resonance

2. Ringing down

Disturb the same system and leave it alone, and it swings at very nearly its natural frequency while the damper drains its energy. Because the damping force is proportional to speed, each swing loses the same fraction of what is left, so the peaks fall along an exponential curve, not a straight line. The natural logarithm of the ratio of one peak to the next is the logarithmic decrement, about \(2\pi\) times the damping ratio. It is the easiest way to measure damping: strike the thing and count. As damping rises the swings become fewer, until at a damping ratio of 1, called critical, there are none. The system then returns to rest once, in the least time possible without overshooting. More damping than that makes the return slower, like a door on a stiff closer.

Worked example. With a damping ratio of 0.05 the decrement is \(2\pi\times0.05/\sqrt{1-0.0025}=0.315\), so each peak is \(e^{-0.315}=0.73\) of the one before and the swing halves every \(\ln2/0.315=2.2\) cycles. A 2 Hz system then swings at 1.9975 Hz, barely changed. At a ratio of 0.3, typical of a car, it halves in a third of a cycle; at 0.001, it takes 110.

Watch out. Damping proportional to speed is the easy case, true of a piston in oil at low speed. Dry friction takes a fixed amount from each swing and stops the motion dead after a finite number; air drag at high speed takes more from large swings than small. Real structures mix all three, and one damping ratio is a summary.

A wine glass rings for a second or two after being flicked; a plastic cup makes a dull tick. Which has the higher Q, and how does that show when sound of the right pitch is played at each?

The glass, by a large factor. Ringing long and responding strongly at resonance are the same property, so sustained sound at exactly its pitch can build a swing large enough to break it, while the cup, which forgets each push at once, hardly moves.

Reference: R. P. Feynman, R. B. Leighton and M. Sands · The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. I, ch. 23: Resonance

3. Soft mounts

A machine with anything out of balance shakes itself once per turn. Bolt it to the floor and the floor shakes with it. Stand it on springs and the force that reaches the floor is the force in the springs, which depends on how far the machine moves. Above resonance the machine moves less and less as the shaking gets faster, because there is not time for it to go far. So the rule of isolation is to put the bounce frequency of the machine on its mounts well below its running speed. The crossover comes at the square root of two times the bounce frequency, where the mounts pass exactly the force they receive, whatever their damping. Damping is a bargain struck at that point: it tames the resonance the machine must pass through while starting and stopping, at the cost of passing more force at speed.

Worked example. Mounts that sag 5 mm under a machine give a bounce frequency of \(\sqrt{9.81/0.005}/2\pi=7.05\) Hz. At 1200 turns a minute, 20 Hz, the machine runs at 2.84 times that, and with a damping ratio of 0.1 the floor feels 16 per cent of the shaking force. At resonance it would feel 5.1 times the force. To pass only a tenth with undamped mounts, the ratio must be \(\sqrt{11}=3.3\), which needs a sag of 6.8 mm.

Watch out. One mass moving in one direction on a rigid floor. A real machine can also rock and twist, each with its own frequency, and a real floor is itself a spring. The sag rule holds for steel springs; rubber is stiffer when vibrated than when loaded slowly, so it bounces faster than its sag suggests.

A washing machine walks across the floor as its spin starts but settles down at full speed. Why?

As the drum speeds up it passes through the bounce frequency of the tub on its springs, where the swing is largest. At full speed it is far above that frequency, the tub hardly moves, and little force reaches the cabinet.

Reference: R. P. Feynman, R. B. Leighton and M. Sands · The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. I, ch. 23: Resonance
