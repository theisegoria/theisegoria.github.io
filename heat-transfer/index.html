Heat transfer: conduction, convection, radiation

Thermodynamics says which way heat will go and how much there is to move. It does not say how fast, and how fast is usually the question: how long the pan takes to heat, whether the brake will fade, what the house costs to keep warm. Heat has three ways to travel. Through matter it is handed from atom to atom, at a rate set by the temperature gradient. A moving fluid carries it away bodily. And every surface radiates, in proportion to the fourth power of its temperature, across empty space. The three follow different laws and take charge in different circumstances. These experiments take a wall, a cup of tea and a glowing disc to show each one at work.

1. Conduction: resistances in a row

Fourier’s law says that heat flows down a temperature gradient at a rate proportional to it, and the constant, the thermal conductivity, is a property of the material. Copper conducts ten thousand times better than mineral wool. For a flat slab the law turns into something that looks exactly like Ohm’s law: the heat flowing through each square metre is the temperature difference divided by a resistance, the thickness over the conductivity. Layers in a row add their resistances, as resistors in series do, and because the same heat passes through every layer, the temperature drop across each is in proportion to its resistance. So the profile through a wall is a set of straight lines, steep in the insulator and nearly flat in the brick. The air itself adds two more resistances: a thin, sluggish film clings to each face and must be crossed by conduction.

Worked example. A brick wall 215 mm thick, of conductivity 0.7 W/m K, resists with \(0.215/0.7=0.31\) m²K/W, and the air films add \(1/8+1/25=0.165\). With 20 °C inside and 0 °C outside it loses \(20/0.47=42\) W per square metre, and its inner face sits at 14.7 °C. Line it with 100 mm of mineral wool, of conductivity 0.035, and the resistance rises by \(0.1/0.035=2.86\) to 3.33: the loss falls to 6.0 W, a seventh, and the inner face warms to 19.2 °C.

Watch out. Steady flow in one direction through a wall with no windows, gaps or studs, with typical handbook values for the surface films. Real walls leak heat through their weakest paths, and damp insulation conducts far better than dry. Nothing here says how long the wall takes to warm, which depends on its heat capacity as well.

A steel pan and a copper pan of the same thickness sit on the same flame. Why does food scorch in patches in the steel one?

Steel conducts heat poorly sideways as well as through. Heat arriving above the flame cannot spread along the base before it reaches the food, so the surface copies the pattern of the burner. Copper, conducting about twenty-five times better than stainless steel, evens it out.

2. Convection: a cooling cup

Air warmed by a hot surface expands, rises and is replaced by cooler air, and that circulation carries heat away far faster than still air could conduct it. The details of the flow are complicated, but Newton noticed that the result is simple: the rate of loss is roughly proportional to the temperature difference between the surface and the surroundings. The constant of proportionality, the heat transfer coefficient, sums up everything about the flow, from about 10 W per square metre per degree in still air to hundreds in a gale and thousands in boiling water. A body that loses heat this way, and is small or conductive enough to stay at one temperature throughout, cools along an exponential curve. Its time constant is its heat capacity divided by the product of the coefficient and its surface area. Since capacity goes with volume and loss with area, big things cool slowly.

Worked example. A cup holding 250 ml, taken as a cylinder as tall as it is wide, is 68 mm across and has 220 cm² of surface. Shedding 15 W per square metre per degree, tea at 90 °C in a room at 20 °C loses \(15\times0.022\times70=23\) W at first. The time constant is \(1000\times4186\times0.00025/(15\times0.022)=3180\) s, or 53 minutes; the tea reaches 60 °C after \(53\ln(70/40)=30\) minutes and is at 78 °C after ten. A pot of 2 litres, twice as large each way, has twice the time constant.

Watch out. Real tea cools considerably faster than this at first, because evaporation from the open surface carries off more heat than convection does, and evaporation is not proportional to temperature difference. The coefficient also changes as the cup cools. The model treats the cup as having no heat capacity of its own and the tea as perfectly stirred.

You want your coffee as hot as possible in five minutes’ time and take milk. Should you add the milk now or just before drinking?

Now. Heat is lost in proportion to how much hotter the coffee is than the room, so cooling it at once with the milk makes it lose less over the five minutes. The same milk added at the end cools coffee that has already given up more heat.

Reference: J. H. Lienhard IV and J. H. Lienhard V · A Heat Transfer Textbook (free online edition)

3. Radiation: the fourth power

Every surface above absolute zero emits electromagnetic waves, and needs nothing in between to carry them: this is how the Sun warms the Earth. The power radiated by each square metre rises as the fourth power of absolute temperature, the Stefan-Boltzmann law, scaled by the emissivity, a number between zero for a perfect mirror and one for a perfect absorber. A surface also absorbs what its surroundings radiate, so the net loss is the difference of two fourth powers. Near room temperature that difference is modest, about as much as still air carries off, which is why a person loses heat about equally both ways. But doubling the absolute temperature multiplies the radiation by sixteen. The spectrum shifts too. Its peak wavelength is inversely proportional to absolute temperature, Wien’s law, so a body must reach about 500 °C before enough of its radiation reaches the visible for it to glow.

Worked example. A surface of 0.1 m² with emissivity 0.8 at 100 °C, in surroundings at 20 °C, radiates \(0.8\times5.67\times10^{-8}\times0.1\times(373^{4}-293^{4})=54\) W, while still air at 10 W/m²K takes 80 W. At 600 °C the air takes 580 W but radiation 2600 W, four and a half times as much, and the spectrum peaks at \(2898/873=3.3\) µm. The two are equal near 190 °C.

Watch out. The surface is grey: one emissivity at every wavelength. Real surfaces differ. Polished metal has a low emissivity that rises with temperature, and white paint, which reflects sunlight, is nearly black in the infrared. The surroundings are taken as large and at one temperature, and the air as still; a spinning brake disc in an airstream sheds several times more by convection.

A vacuum flask has a gap with no air in it, and the walls facing the gap are silvered. What is each feature for?

The vacuum removes conduction and convection across the gap, since there is nothing to conduct or to flow. Radiation crosses a vacuum freely, so the walls are silvered to give them a very low emissivity, which cuts the one remaining path.
