Guitars: string, fret, truss rod and pickup

A guitar is six stretched wires and a way of choosing their lengths. The pitch of a string is fixed by three things, its length, its tension and its weight, and the instrument is built round each. The frets set the length, at positions worked out so that every step is the same musical interval. The tension, half a hundredweight in all, bends the neck, so a steel rod inside is tightened to bend it back. And because a solid body makes almost no sound, the electric guitar listens to its strings with magnets, which hear a different mixture of harmonics depending on where along the string they sit. These experiments pluck one string, set the bow of a neck, and move a pickup.

1. One string

A wave travels along a stretched string at a speed that depends on how tight and how heavy it is: the square root of the tension divided by the mass of each metre. Fixed at both ends, the string can hold only those waves that fit a whole number of half-wavelengths into its length, so its lowest note has a frequency equal to the wave speed divided by twice the length, and above it lies a ladder of harmonics at two, three, four times that frequency. A plucked string sounds all of them at once. How much of each depends on the shape the string is pulled into before it is let go. Pulled at its middle, the shape resembles the fundamental and the even harmonics are absent altogether. Pulled near one end, the sharp corner needs many high harmonics to describe it, and the note is brighter. Halving the length doubles the frequency, which raises the pitch by an octave.

Worked example. The top string weighs 0.401 g a metre and sounds E at 329.6 Hz over 648 mm, so its tension is \(0.000401\times(2\times0.648\times329.6)^{2}=73\) N and waves run along it at \(\sqrt{73/0.000401}=427\) m/s. Plucked at the middle, the second harmonic is zero and the third is 11 per cent of the first. Plucked a fifth of the way from the bridge, the second is 40 per cent and the third 18 per cent, and the fifth is missing.

Watch out. The string is perfectly flexible and is released from rest in a sharp corner. A real string is slightly stiff, which rounds the corner and makes the upper harmonics a little sharp, and a plectrum or fingertip has width, which removes the highest ones. Damping, which makes the high harmonics die away first, is not shown.

A guitarist touches a string lightly at the twelfth fret and plucks it. A note an octave higher rings out. Why?

The finger holds the middle of the string still. Every harmonic that must move there, the first, third, fifth and so on, is stopped at once, and only those with a still point at the middle survive: the second, fourth, sixth. Together they form a note at twice the frequency.

Reference: N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing · The Physics of Musical Instruments, second edition (Springer, 1998), chapters 2 and 9

2. Frets and the truss rod

Western music divides the octave into twelve equal steps, each a rise in frequency by the twelfth root of two, about 5.9 per cent. Since frequency goes as one over length, each fret must leave a sounding length shorter than the last by that same factor. The frets are therefore not evenly spaced: the first is 36 mm from the nut, the twelfth is exactly half-way, and beyond it the pattern repeats at half the size. The strings that the frets divide also load the neck. They run a centimetre or so above its centre line, so their combined pull acts as a bending moment that bows the neck forward. A slim wooden neck would creep under that load, so a steel rod is let into a curved channel below the centre line and anchored at both ends. Tightening it shortens the back of the neck and bends it the other way. A little forward bow is left on purpose, to give a vibrating string room to swing.

Worked example. On a 648 mm scale the first fret is \(648\times(1-2^{-1/12})=36.4\) mm from the nut and the twelfth 324 mm. A light set of strings pulls with 454 N in total. Acting 12 mm above the centre line it bends the neck with 5.4 N m, and a maple neck with a stiffness of 482 N m² bows by \(5.4\times0.45^{2}/(8\times482)=0.29\) mm over its 450 mm. A rod 6 mm below the line, tightened to 114 N, takes that down to 0.25 mm; at 908 N the neck is straight.

Watch out. The guitar is a generic solid-body instrument with a 648 mm scale, drawn to scale except for the swing of the sounding string. The neck is a uniform beam bent by constant moments, and the rod a straight bar at a fixed depth; real rods lie in a curved slot and real necks taper. The small sharpening of pitch that comes from pressing a string down to a fret, which the saddles are set back to correct, is ignored.

After changing to heavier strings a guitar plays with a higher action in the middle of the neck. Which way should the truss rod nut be turned?

Tighter. Heavier strings need more tension for the same notes, so they bow the neck further forward. More tension in the rod supplies a larger opposing moment and brings the relief back to where it was.

Reference: N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing · The Physics of Musical Instruments, second edition (Springer, 1998), chapters 2 and 9

3. The pickup

A magnetic pickup is a permanent magnet inside a coil of several thousand turns of hair-fine wire. A steel string above it becomes part of the path of the magnet’s field, and when the string moves, the field through the coil changes and a voltage appears across it, in proportion to the speed of the string at that point. Two things follow. First, the pickup is deaf to any harmonic that has a still point directly above it, so its place along the string colours the sound: far from the bridge it loses harmonics low in the series, close to the bridge it keeps nearly all of them. Second, the coil is an electrical component in its own right, with a large inductance, and the cable to the amplifier adds capacitance. Together they resonate at a few kilohertz, lifting the frequencies near that point and cutting off those above it. The tone control is a capacitor that can be switched across the coil to remove the lift.

Worked example. A pickup 22 per cent of the way from the bridge, 143 mm on an open string, has its first deaf spot at harmonic \(1/0.22=4.5\): it hears little of the fourth and fifth, which on the open A string lie near 500 Hz. A coil of 2.5 H with 620 pF of its own and the cable’s capacitance resonates at \(1/(2\pi\sqrt{2.5\times620\times10^{-12}})=4.0\) kHz and boosts that region 1.5 times. With 1500 pF the peak falls to 2.5 kHz; with the tone control at zero it disappears.

Watch out. The pickup is treated as sensing the string at a single point, where a real one senses a few centimetres and so blurs its deaf spots. The string is plucked 18 per cent of the way from the bridge. The coil is 2.5 H and 6 kΩ with 120 pF of its own, loaded by 200 kΩ; humbucking pairs, which sense two points at once, and the amplifier and loudspeaker that follow are not modelled.

Why does the same guitar sound duller through a six-metre cable than through a one-metre one?

Cable has capacitance in proportion to its length. More capacitance across the coil lowers the resonant frequency, so the lift moves down and the cut-off above it removes more of the high harmonics.

Reference: N. H. Fletcher and T. D. Rossing · The Physics of Musical Instruments, second edition (Springer, 1998), chapters 2 and 9
