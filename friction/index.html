Friction: grip, rope and stick-slip

Friction is the force that textbooks apologise for and machines depend on. Without it no wheel could drive, no brake could stop, no knot or nail or screw could hold. It is also unlike the other forces of mechanics. It has no fixed size: it gives whatever is needed to prevent sliding, up to a limit, and then gives way to something weaker. Those two facts, a ceiling proportional to the push between the surfaces and a drop once sliding begins, are enough to explain why a slope has a critical angle, why a rope wound round a post can hold a ship, and why doors creak, brakes squeal and violins sing. These experiments take them in turn.

1. A ceiling, not a force

Put a book on a board and tilt the board. For a while nothing happens, although the pull along the slope is growing all the time. Friction is growing with it, exactly matching it. Static friction is a reaction: it supplies whatever is needed to prevent sliding. What the surfaces set is its ceiling, which Amontons found in 1699 to be proportional to the force pressing them together and, surprisingly, independent of the area in contact. The ratio is the coefficient of static friction. On a slope the pressing force falls as the cosine of the angle while the demand rises as the sine, so the book lets go when the tangent of the angle equals the coefficient, whatever the book weighs. Once it moves, friction falls to a smaller, roughly constant fraction of the pressing force. The reason for both laws is that real surfaces touch only at the tips of their roughness, and the true area of contact grows in proportion to the load.

Worked example. With a coefficient of 0.5 the critical angle is \(\arctan 0.5=26.6^\circ\). A 5 kg block on a 20° slope needs \(5\times9.81\times\sin20^\circ=16.8\) N and friction could give up to 23.0 N, so it stays. At 35° it slides, and with a sliding coefficient of 0.4 it accelerates at \(9.81\,(\sin35^\circ-0.4\cos35^\circ)=2.4\) m/s².

Watch out. Coulomb’s laws are a good summary for dry, hard surfaces, not a law of nature. Rubber, whose friction depends on area and speed, breaks them; so do lubricated surfaces, where the fluid film carries the load. Coefficients vary with cleanliness, humidity and how long the surfaces have rested together, and tabulated values are good to perhaps twenty per cent.

A heavy crate and a light one, of the same material, sit on the same ramp as it is slowly tilted. Which slides first?

Neither: they go together. The pull along the slope and the ceiling on friction are both proportional to the weight, so the weight cancels and only the angle and the coefficient matter.

Reference: R. P. Feynman, R. B. Leighton and M. Sands · The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. I, ch. 12: Characteristics of force

2. A rope round a post

A rope bent round a post presses on it, because the tension pulling along the rope on either side of each small piece has a part directed inward. The tighter the rope, the harder it presses, and so the more friction it can call on. That is a loop: the friction on each piece is proportional to the tension there, and the friction is what changes the tension from piece to piece. A quantity whose rate of change is proportional to itself is an exponential, and so the pull that can be held grows exponentially with the angle of wrap. Three turns with a modest coefficient multiply a hand’s pull several hundred times. The radius of the post cancels: a fat post gives more rope in contact but presses on each piece less. This is how a capstan, a belt drive, a climber’s belay and an elevator’s traction sheave all work, and it is why knots hold.

Worked example. With a coefficient of 0.3, one turn multiplies the pull by \(e^{0.3\times2\pi}=6.6\) and three turns by \(e^{0.3\times6\pi}=286\). A hand pulling with 100 N then holds 28.6 kN, the weight of 2.9 tonnes. To multiply the pull a thousandfold takes \(\ln1000/(2\pi\times0.3)=3.7\) turns.

Watch out. The formula gives the largest ratio friction can hold; with a smaller load the rope simply does not slip. It assumes a flexible rope of no weight and one coefficient all the way round. Stiff rope, a grooved or tapered post and turns that ride over one another all change the number, and wet or oily rope can halve the coefficient.

A sailor wants to let a loaded rope out slowly under control. Should she add turns or take them off?

Take them off, until the ratio the post can hold is only a little more than the ratio between the load and a comfortable pull. Then easing her grip slightly lets the rope slip, and tightening it stops it again.

Reference: R. P. Feynman, R. B. Leighton and M. Sands · The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. I, ch. 12: Characteristics of force

3. Stick and slip

Drag something springy steadily across a surface and it often refuses to move steadily. A door hinge creaks, chalk judders on a board, a wet finger makes a wine glass ring. The cause is the step down from static to sliding friction. While the surfaces are stuck, the drive winds up the spring. When the spring force reaches the static ceiling, the surfaces let go, and at that instant the opposing friction drops. The spring now has force to spare, so the body is flung back, overshoots the point where sliding friction would balance it, slows relative to the surface and is caught again. Energy comes from the steady drive; the timing comes from the spring and the mass. Slow drive and soft spring give long sticking and a brief jump. Fast drive and stiff spring shorten the sticking until the cycle is nearly the natural vibration of the mass on its spring, and that is how a bow sustains a string.

Worked example. A 1 kg block with coefficients 0.5 and 0.3 on a belt moving at 20 mm/s, held by a spring of 200 N/m, lets go when the spring is stretched \(0.5\times9.81/200=24.5\) mm and is caught again at 4.9 mm. Reloading takes \(19.6/20=0.98\) s and the slip 0.24 s, so it jerks 0.82 times a second, though the mass on the spring alone would vibrate at 2.25 Hz. With a spring of 2000 N/m and a belt at 200 mm/s the sticking lasts only 5 ms, and the rate, 7.12 Hz, is the natural frequency.

Watch out. This is the simplest model: two constant coefficients and no damping. With it every drive speed gives stick-slip. Real sliding friction varies with speed, and real systems have damping, so above some speed the motion becomes smooth, which is why a squealing brake falls silent as it is pressed harder or released. The model also treats the body as one rigid mass.

Oiling a creaking hinge silences it. In terms of this model, what has the oil changed?

It has removed the step between sticking and sliding friction. With a film of oil between the surfaces, friction no longer falls when motion starts (it rises gently with speed instead), so there is nothing to fling the surfaces past their balance point and the motion stays steady.

Reference: R. P. Feynman, R. B. Leighton and M. Sands · The Feynman Lectures on Physics, vol. I, ch. 12: Characteristics of force
