Flush toilets: the siphon and the float valve

A flush toilet has no motor, no sensor and no power supply, and yet it carries out a timed sequence: empty the bowl completely, seal the drain against sewer gas, refill itself to the same level every time, and stop. It does this with two old ideas. The bowl empties because the bend behind it turns into a siphon when water arrives quickly enough, and a siphon, once started, pulls with the weight of the water falling down its far side. The cistern refills because a float on a lever closes the supply with the buoyancy of the water it sits in. These experiments pour water into a bowl fast and slow, saw a toilet in half and run it through a flush, and balance the float against the mains.

1. The bowl is a siphon

Behind the bowl the outlet pipe rises over a crest before it falls to the drain. Water stands in the bowl level with that crest, and since the mouth of the pipe is below the surface, the standing water seals the pipe. Add water slowly and it simply spills over the crest at the rate it arrives. Add it quickly and the level rises faster than the crest can shed it, until the pipe runs full from end to end. Now the column of water falling down the far leg is heavier than the short column on the near side, and the difference pulls the contents of the bowl up and over. The flow is set by the height of the fall and the friction in the pipe, not by the supply. It lasts until the bowl is drawn down to the mouth of the pipe, where air is sucked in with a gurgle and the siphon breaks.

Worked example. The pipe is 55 mm across, an area of 23.8 cm², with a fall of 25 cm beyond the crest and losses of \(K=3\). With the bowl at crest level the siphon runs at \(\sqrt{2\times9.81\times0.25/4}=1.11\) m/s, which is 2.63 L/s. It starts when the bowl is 4.9 cm above the crest. Six litres poured in over 3 s, at 2 L/s, starts it and 7.5 L leave: the pour plus 1.5 L of the bowl’s own water. The same six litres over 30 s raise the level by only 1.7 cm and 5.9 L spill over, leaving the bowl as it was.

Watch out. The bowl is a tank of constant surface area and the pipe starts to siphon at a fixed flow over the crest; in a real bowl the shape of the passage and jets that aim water into it decide the moment. Washdown bowls, common in Europe, do not siphon at all and rely on the rush of water alone.

Why does the flush stop on its own, instead of the siphon going on to drain the cistern’s refill as well?

A siphon needs an unbroken column of water. When the bowl has been drawn down to the mouth of the pipe, air enters, the column breaks and the pull is gone. The slow refill that follows only raises the level back to the crest, far too gently to fill the pipe again.

Reference: F. M. White · Fluid Mechanics (McGraw-Hill), chapters on the Bernoulli equation and flow from tanks

2. The whole toilet through a flush

The cistern exists to deliver water faster than a household pipe can. It holds a few litres above a wide outlet closed by a rubber flapper. Pressing the handle lifts the flapper, which floats open until the level has fallen, and the water drops into the bowl in about three seconds. Its speed through the outlet follows Torricelli’s law: the same as that of something that had fallen freely from the surface. As the head of water falls, so does the flow. The bowl then goes through the siphon cycle, and the float valve, which opened as soon as the level began to fall, refills the cistern slowly. A small tube sends part of the incoming water down the overflow pipe into the bowl, to put back the seal that the siphon carried away.

Worked example. The cistern has a surface of 600 cm² and stands 20 cm deep over a 50 mm outlet with a discharge coefficient of 0.6. At the start the flow is \(0.6\times19.6\times10^{-4}\times\sqrt{2\times9.81\times0.2}=2.33\) L/s. A 6 litre flush lowers the level by 10 cm, which by the formula takes 3.0 s; with the float valve already adding water it takes 3.4 s. The siphon breaks after 3.9 s and the cistern is full again after about a minute.

Watch out. The toilet is a generic close-coupled design drawn to scale and cut on its centre line; the passages are shown with a constant width across the bowl, where a real casting is rounded. The rim channel that spreads the water round the bowl is not modelled, and the refill is played six times faster than life.

A dual-flush toilet offers 3 litres or 6. How does one cistern give both?

By closing the outlet at different levels. The small flush lets the flapper or valve drop back after the level has fallen about 5 cm, the large one after 10 cm. The first part of either flush is identical, because the head of water is the same.

Reference: F. M. White · Fluid Mechanics (McGraw-Hill), chapters on the Bernoulli equation and flow from tanks

3. The float valve

The supply arrives at several times the pressure of the air, and something must shut it off at the right level without being told. A float valve does it with a lever. The water of the mains pushes on a small washer with a force equal to the pressure times the area of the opening. A float on the long arm of a lever pushes back on the washer, and its push is the weight of the water it displaces, multiplied by the ratio of the lever’s arms. As the cistern fills, the float sinks deeper, displaces more and presses harder, until it matches the mains and the flow stops. A higher supply pressure simply makes the float settle a little deeper. Modern fill valves replace the ball and arm with a cup sliding on a column, but the balance of forces is the same.

Worked example. At 3 bar a 4 mm opening is pushed with \(3\times10^{5}\times12.6\times10^{-6}=3.77\) N. With a lever of 8 to 1 the float must supply 0.47 N, the weight of 48 mL of water. A 100 mm ball does that when it is 19 mm under, and fully sunk it could hold 33 bar. With the valve wide open the flow is \(0.7\times12.6\times10^{-6}\times\sqrt{600}=0.215\) L/s, 12.9 L/min, so 6 litres take 28 s; at 6 bar they take 20 s.

Watch out. The float is a sphere with no weight of its own and the lever has no friction, so the real float sits a little deeper than shown. The flow is that of a sharp-edged opening with a coefficient of 0.7, with no allowance for the pipe leading to it.

A cistern keeps running into its overflow. Bending the float arm downwards cures it. Why?

The valve closes when the float is sunk to a fixed depth. Bending the arm down puts the float lower for the same position of the valve, so that depth is reached at a lower water level, below the top of the overflow tube.

Reference: F. M. White · Fluid Mechanics (McGraw-Hill), chapters on the Bernoulli equation and flow from tanks
