Electric toothbrushes: resonance in the hand

A sonic toothbrush moves its bristles five hundred times a second, a hundred times faster than a hand can, and it does so with a motor that has no gears, no brushes and no bearings to wear, running for weeks on a cell the size of a finger. The trick is resonance. The head and its shaft are mounted on a spring and have a natural frequency, like a tuning fork, and the handle pushes them in time with it, so that each push adds to the last. It is also sealed against water, so the energy has to get in without a wire, by a magnetic field passed from a coil in the stand to a coil in the handle. These experiments tune the drive to the brush, charge it through its case, and open the handle.

1. Resonance: why it hums at one note

Twist the head of a sonic brush and let go, and it would ring back and forth at its own frequency, set by the stiffness of its spring bar and the inertia of the head, dying away over a dozen swings. The drive coils push it with a torque that alternates at a frequency the electronics choose. If that frequency is well below the natural one, the head just follows the push and barely moves. As the two approach, the head lags further behind until, when they match, every push comes exactly when the head is moving fastest in that direction. The swing then grows until friction removes as much energy each cycle as the push supplies, which is the natural swing multiplied by the quality factor. Bristles pressed against teeth add friction and a little stiffness, so the peak drops and shifts, and the electronics can use that to sense how hard the brush is pressed.

Worked example. The free brush has \(f_0=260\) Hz and \(Q_0=12\). Driven at 260 Hz it swings 6° each way: the bristle tips, 11 mm from the axis, sweep 2.3 mm and reach 1.9 m/s, 31 200 strokes a minute, and 0.40 W is lost in the head. The same torque applied slowly would give 0.5°. Detuned to 230 Hz the swing is 2.2°. Pressed with 1 N, \(Q\) falls to 7.2 and \(f_0\) rises to 265 Hz: the swing is 3.4° and the tips sweep 1.3 mm. At 3 N the swing is 1.7°.

Watch out. The brush is treated as a single linear oscillator with representative values. A real head also bends, the bristles add their own resonances, and the toothpaste and water between bristles and teeth carry some of the motion. Makers tune each handle to its head at the factory, and some track the resonance as the head wears.

Why does a sonic toothbrush run at one particular frequency?

Its head is a spring-mounted mass with a natural frequency. Driven at that frequency, each small magnetic push adds to the motion already there, and the swing builds up to many times what the same push would give at any other frequency. That lets a small, efficient actuator produce a large, fast motion.

Reference: A. Kurs et al. · Wireless power transfer via strongly coupled magnetic resonances (Science, 2007)

2. Charging through a sealed case

A changing current in one coil makes a changing magnetic field, and a changing field through a second coil drives a current in it: this is a transformer. In a toothbrush the two halves are in separate plastic shells, so there is a gap of several millimetres where a transformer would have iron, and most of the field made by the stand’s coil never passes through the handle’s. The fraction that does is the coupling \(k\). Each coil also wastes power in its own resistance, measured by its quality factor \(Q\), the ratio of the energy it stores to the energy it loses per cycle. However the circuit is arranged, the efficiency cannot exceed a limit that depends only on the product \(k^2Q_1Q_2\). A small gap or good coils make that product large and the limit close to one.

Worked example. With 3 mm between the coils the coupling is 0.41. For two coils of \(Q=8\) the figure of merit is \(0.41^2\times64=10.8\) and the best efficiency is 55 per cent, so putting 1 W into the battery draws 1.8 W and wastes 0.8 W as heat; a 2.6 Wh cell fills in 2.6 hours. At 8 mm the coupling is 0.15 and the limit 22 per cent. Coils of \(Q=24\) at 3 mm reach 82 per cent.

Watch out. The fall of coupling with gap is a representative exponential, not a field calculation, and the efficiency shown is the upper limit for an ideally matched load. Real toothbrush chargers are simple circuits that fall well short of it, and they also lose power in the electronics on each side and trickle a little when the brush is full.

Why is wireless charging less efficient than plugging in a cable?

Only part of the magnetic field made by the sending coil passes through the receiving coil, so the sender must build a larger field than is used, and the current that maintains it wastes power in the coil’s resistance. The wider the gap, the smaller the useful part and the larger the waste.

Reference: A. Kurs et al. · Wireless power transfer via strongly coupled magnetic resonances (Science, 2007)

3. Inside the handle

Everything in the handle is arranged along one line. At the bottom a coil sits round a hollow that fits over a peg on the stand, where it picks up the stand’s field. Above it is a single lithium-ion cell and a circuit board that turns the cell’s steady voltage into an alternating current at the head’s frequency. That current flows in two coils wound on an iron frame; between them, on the shaft, is a small block carrying permanent magnets, which the coils’ field rocks one way and then the other. Nothing rotates continuously and nothing rubs: the shaft is held by a thin steel bar that twists, and that bar is the spring of the resonance. The shaft passes out through a rubber seal to the brush head. What matters for cleaning is the speed of the bristle tips across the tooth, which is the number of strokes a second times the length of a stroke.

Worked example. A hand making 300 strokes a minute of 20 mm moves the bristles at an average of 0.10 m/s. A round head turning 25° each way 8800 times a minute, with tips at 6.5 mm, gives 5.7 mm strokes and 0.83 m/s. The sonic brush in air makes 31 200 strokes a minute of 2.3 mm: 1.20 m/s, twelve times the hand, and 62 400 strokes in two minutes. Pressed with 1 N its strokes shrink to 1.3 mm. A 2.6 Wh cell at 1.5 W lasts 52 two-minute sessions.

Watch out. The brush is a generic sonic design with representative dimensions, not a particular product, and its swing in the scene is enlarged four times and slowed about two hundred times so it can be seen. Tip speed is a simple measure; real cleaning also depends on bristle stiffness, on the fluid driven between the teeth, and above all on reaching every surface.

Why does pressing a sonic toothbrush harder against the teeth clean no better?

The brush cleans by the fast motion of its bristle tips, and that motion is a resonance. Pressing adds friction, which lowers the swing in proportion, so the tips move less far and more slowly. The handle senses the smaller swing and warns that the pressure is too high.
