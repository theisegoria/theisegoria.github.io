Noise-cancelling earbuds

Sound is a pressure wave, and two pressure waves in the same place simply add. An earbud that plays an exact upside-down copy of the noise arriving at the ear therefore leaves silence. Everything difficult about noise cancelling is in the word exact: the copy has to match in size to a few per cent and in timing to a few millionths of a second, and it has to be worked out and played in the time sound takes to travel about a centimetre. That is why cancelling works on the low drone of an engine and not on a voice or a clatter of dishes, why the microphones sit where they do, and why a poor seal in the ear ruins it. These experiments add a wave to its copy, run the feedback loop up to the point where it howls, and put the ear tip and the electronics together inside an earbud.

1. Anti-noise: how exact the copy must be

Take a pure tone as the noise and add to it a copy that has been inverted. If the copy is exactly as large and exactly in step, the sum is zero. If its size is wrong by a factor \(g\) and it is late by a time \(\tau\), so that its phase is off by \(\varphi=2\pi f\tau\), a remainder is left whose size follows from adding the two as arrows: \(\sqrt{1+g^2-2g\cos\varphi}\) times the noise. Two things follow. Small errors matter a great deal: a 1 dB error in size alone leaves 12 per cent of the noise. And because the same delay is a larger fraction of a cycle at a higher frequency, a fixed timing error that is harmless for a low hum makes cancellation useless, and then harmful, as the pitch rises.

Worked example. At 200 Hz, with the copy 0.5 dB too strong (\(g=1.059\)) and 40 µs late, the phase error is \(360^\circ\times200\times40\times10^{-6}=2.9^\circ\) and 7.9 per cent of the noise is left: 22 dB removed. With the size exact, the same 40 µs leaves 25 per cent at 1 kHz, only 12 dB, and cancellation of 20 dB is possible only below about 400 Hz. In 40 µs sound travels 13.7 mm, roughly the distance from a microphone on the outside of an earbud to its speaker.

Watch out. A single steady tone is the easiest case. Real noise contains many frequencies at once and changes from moment to moment, so the earbud needs a filter that gets size and timing right at every frequency together, and it must keep adapting that filter as the fit of the earbud changes.

Why can noise cancelling remove an engine drone but not a nearby conversation?

The anti-noise is always a little late, and a fixed delay is a small fraction of a cycle for a low sound but a large fraction for a high one. A 40 µs delay is under 2 degrees at 100 Hz and over 40 degrees at 3 kHz, where it cancels almost nothing.

Reference: S. M. Kuo and D. R. Morgan · Active noise control: a tutorial review (Proceedings of the IEEE, 1999)

2. The feedback loop and its limit

One way to make the copy is not to predict the noise at all. Put a microphone inside the ear tip, next to the speaker, and connect it so that whatever pressure it hears, the speaker pushes the opposite way, with gain \(K\). The pressure at the ear then settles at the noise divided by \(1+L\), where \(L\) is the gain once round the loop from speaker to microphone and back. A large gain gives deep quiet. But the trip round the loop takes time, and the speaker responds more weakly and later at high frequencies, so \(L\) turns in phase as frequency rises. At the frequency where it has turned by half a cycle the loop is no longer opposing the sound but reinforcing it. If the gain there is still above one, the earbud squeals. Short of that, there is always a band just above the quiet one where noise is made a little louder.

Worked example. With \(f_1=100\) Hz, a gain of 20 dB (\(K=10\)) and a delay of 60 µs, the ear hears the noise reduced by \(20\log_{10}11=20.8\) dB at low frequency, and the cancelling fades out near \(Kf_1=1\) kHz. The phase reaches \(-180^\circ\) at 4.2 kHz, where the gain could be 42 (32.5 dB) before the loop becomes unstable, a margin of 12.5 dB; the worst amplification is 2.9 dB near 3 kHz. Push the gain to 30 dB and the margin is 2.5 dB, with noise near 3.9 kHz made 13 dB louder. With a 100 µs delay the limit falls to 28 dB.

Watch out. The speaker and ear are modelled as a single roll-off and a pure delay. A real earbud has resonances of the driver and the ear canal, and its designers shape the loop with filters to trade depth against bandwidth, but the limit set by delay is the same in kind. The compensation that keeps the wanted music from being cancelled along with the noise is not shown.

Why can the gain of a feedback noise canceller not simply be turned up for more quiet?

Because the loop has delay, at some frequency its output arrives half a cycle late and adds to the sound instead of opposing it. If the gain round the loop is one or more at that frequency the system oscillates. The gain must stay below that limit, and the shorter the delay the higher the limit.

Reference: S. M. Kuo and D. R. Morgan · Active noise control: a tutorial review (Proceedings of the IEEE, 1999)

3. Inside the earbud: tip and electronics together

A noise-cancelling earbud blocks sound three ways at once. The silicone tip seals the ear canal, which stops high-pitched sound well and low-pitched sound poorly. A microphone on the outside hears the noise a moment before it reaches the eardrum, and a filter shapes that signal into anti-noise: this is feed-forward cancelling. A second microphone inside the tip hears what is left and drives the feedback loop of the previous experiment. The two electronic systems work only at low frequencies, exactly where the tip is weak. Their sum is strong at both ends of the range and weakest in the middle, around 1 kHz. Everything depends on the seal: a tip that leaks lets low-frequency noise straight in, and also lets the pressure made by the speaker escape, so the anti-noise no longer matches.

Worked example. With a good seal the model removes 34 dB at 100 Hz (6 dB from the tip, 28 dB from the electronics), 18 dB at 1 kHz and 21 dB at 5 kHz, where the electronics contribute nothing and slightly hurt. In aircraft-cabin noise of 84 dBA the tip alone brings the level at the eardrum to 76 dBA and the electronics take it to 63 dBA. For office voices at 69 dBA the tip gives 11 dB and the electronics another 8 dB. With the tip half unsealed the aircraft noise is reduced to 72 dBA only.

Watch out. The earbud is a generic in-ear design with a 9 mm driver, not a particular product, and the ear is simplified to a straight canal. The attenuation curves and noise spectra are representative shapes chosen to match typical published measurements, not measurements of a device. The waves in the scene are illustrative and are not drawn to the scale of the wavelength, which at 200 Hz is 1.7 m.

Why does a noise-cancelling earbud perform so much worse when it does not fit the ear well?

A gap lets low-frequency noise pass the tip unhindered, and lets the pressure the speaker makes leak out, which is worst at low frequencies. The anti-noise then arrives weaker than the filter expects, so the size no longer matches and the cancellation collapses just where it was doing the work.

Reference: S. M. Kuo and D. R. Morgan · Active noise control: a tutorial review (Proceedings of the IEEE, 1999)
