Nonlinear waves: solitons, shocks and the return of energy

In a linear wave every component travels independently, and two waves pass through each other by simple addition. Real waves are nonlinear: a taller part of a water wave moves faster than a lower one, so waves steepen. What happens next depends on what opposes the steepening. Viscosity turns it into a shock, a thin front across which the wave jumps. Dispersion, which spreads short wavelengths apart, can balance it exactly and produce a solitary wave that keeps its shape forever, as John Scott Russell saw in 1834 when a wave from a stopped canal boat ran ahead of him for two miles. In 1965 Zabusky and Kruskal found that such waves collide and emerge intact, like particles, and named them solitons; in doing so they explained a puzzle Fermi, Pasta, Ulam and Tsingou had found ten years earlier, a chain of oscillators that refused to share its energy. These experiments follow solitons through a collision in a wave flume, watch a hump of fluid break into a shock, and see that energy come back.

Before you begin: Partial differential equations (the wave and heat equations), Fourier modes, ordinary differential equations

1. Solitons: waves that collide like particles

Shallow water waves of small amplitude obey the Korteweg–de Vries equation \(u_t+6uu_x+u_{xxx}=0\). The term \(6uu_x\) steepens the wave; \(u_{xxx}\) disperses it. A single soliton \(u=\frac{k^2}{2}\operatorname{sech}^2\frac{k(x-k^2t)}{2}\) balances them: height \(k^2/2\), speed \(k^2\), width \(\sim1/k\), so taller solitons are narrower and faster. Hirota’s exact two-soliton solution shows what happens when the fast one catches the slow one. The two merge, exchange their identities, and separate again with their original shapes and speeds. The only trace of the collision is a jump in position: the fast soliton ends up ahead of where it would have been, the slow one behind.

Worked example. With \(k_1=1.4\) and \(k_2=0.8\) the solitons have speeds 1.96 and 0.64 and heights 0.98 and 0.32. The interaction coefficient is \(A=(0.6/2.2)^2=0.0744\) and \(\ln(2.2/0.6)=1.299\), so the fast soliton is pushed forward by \(\frac{2}{1.4}\times1.299=1.856\) and the slow one back by \(\frac{2}{0.8}\times1.299=3.248\). The slow soliton, being broader, is displaced further. At the moment of overlap the peak is lower than the taller soliton alone, not the sum of the two.

Watch out. The equation is written in scaled units; for water of depth \(h\) the physical soliton of height \(a\) travels at \(\sqrt{g(h+a)}\) and has width of order \(h\sqrt{h/a}\). KdV holds for long waves of small amplitude moving in one direction, and the heights in the 3D flume are exaggerated for visibility. The dashed curve adds the two solitons at their shifted positions, which is the solution far from the collision but not during it.

Why are taller solitons faster?

In \(u_t+6uu_x+u_{xxx}=0\) the local speed of the nonlinear term is \(6u\), so a taller wave is carried faster. Balancing this against dispersion fixes the shape \(\frac{k^2}{2}\operatorname{sech}^2\) and ties the speed to the height: \(c=k^2=2\times\text{height}\).

Reference: N. J. Zabusky and M. D. Kruskal · Interaction of “solitons” in a collisionless plasma and the recurrence of initial states, Physical Review Letters 15, 240–243 (1965)

2. Burgers’ equation: how a wave breaks

Drop the dispersion and keep the steepening: \(u_t+uu_x=0\). Each value of \(u\) is carried at speed \(u\) along a straight characteristic, so the top of a hump overtakes its front. The profile steepens until, at the breaking time \(t^*=1/\max(-u_0^{\prime})\), its slope becomes infinite and the characteristics cross; past that the inviscid solution would be three-valued. A little viscosity, \(u_t+uu_x=\nu u_{xx}\), resolves the crossing into a shock. The Cole–Hopf transformation \(u=-2\nu\,\partial_x\ln\varphi\) turns Burgers’ equation into the heat equation for \(\varphi\), so the viscous solution is known exactly for any initial hump.

Worked example. For \(u_0=a\,e^{-x^2}\) the steepest downslope is at \(x=1/\sqrt2\), where \(-u_0^{\prime}=\sqrt2\,a\,e^{-1/2}\). With \(a=1\) the wave breaks at \(t^*=\sqrt e/\sqrt2=1.166\). A shock with \(u_L=0.8\) behind and \(u_R=0\) ahead moves at the Rankine–Hugoniot speed \((0.8+0)/2=0.4\), half the speed of the fluid behind it, whatever the viscosity; viscosity only sets its thickness, roughly \(4\nu/(u_L-u_R)\).

Watch out. Burgers’ equation is a model, not the equations of gas dynamics, but it captures their essential balance of steepening and diffusion. The Rankine–Hugoniot speed follows from conserving \(\int u\,dx\); the steepest slope and the jump are read from the computed profile, so they depend slightly on the plotting resolution. The viscous solution is computed exactly by quadrature of the Cole–Hopf integral.

Why does the shock move at the average of the two speeds?

Burgers’ equation conserves \(\int u\,dx\) with flux \(u^2/2\). A jump moving at speed \(s\) must take in exactly what the fluxes deliver: \(s(u_L-u_R)=\frac{u_L^2}{2}-\frac{u_R^2}{2}\), so \(s=\frac{u_L+u_R}{2}\).

Reference: N. J. Zabusky and M. D. Kruskal · Interaction of “solitons” in a collisionless plasma and the recurrence of initial states, Physical Review Letters 15, 240–243 (1965)

3. Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou: energy that comes back

In 1955, on one of the first computers, Fermi, Pasta, Ulam and Tsingou simulated a chain of masses joined by springs with a small quadratic nonlinearity, expecting it to thermalize: energy put into the lowest mode should spread evenly over all the modes. It did not. The energy moved into the next few modes and then, after about 157 periods of the lowest mode, almost all of it returned. Zabusky and Kruskal later showed that the long-wave limit of the chain is the KdV equation: the initial sine wave breaks up into solitons, which travel around the chain, pass through each other, and briefly reassemble the original shape.

Worked example. With \(N=32\), \(\alpha=0.25\) and an initial displacement \(x_j=\sin(j\pi/33)\), the chain holds energy 0.0747. The lowest mode has \(\omega_1=2\sin(\pi/66)=0.0952\), a period of 66.0. At its lowest, near \(t\approx6900\), mode 1 keeps only about 8% of the energy; at \(t\approx10\,200\), about 154 periods, it has 98% of the energy back. With \(\alpha=0\) the modes are exactly independent and nothing moves at all.

Watch out. The recurrence is not exact and is not the end of the story: over much longer times, or at higher energies, the chain does slowly approach equipartition, and super-recurrences occur on longer scales. The motion is integrated with the velocity Verlet method, step 0.1, which conserves energy to high accuracy over these times. The displacements of the chain are drawn greatly magnified.

Why does the energy return instead of spreading out?

Because the chain is close to an integrable system. Its long-wave dynamics is the KdV equation, whose solitons keep their identities through collisions, so the motion is quasi-periodic rather than chaotic and the initial state is approximately revisited. Chaos and thermalization set in only at higher energies or much longer times.

Reference: N. J. Zabusky and M. D. Kruskal · Interaction of “solitons” in a collisionless plasma and the recurrence of initial states, Physical Review Letters 15, 240–243 (1965)
