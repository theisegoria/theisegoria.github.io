Lithium-ion cells and chargers

A lithium-ion cell stores energy by moving lithium between two solids. Discharged, the lithium sits in a metal oxide; charging pulls it out and packs it between the layers of graphite, where it would rather not be, and the cell gives the energy back as the lithium returns. The voltage between the two tells how full the cell is, but only when no current is flowing: under load the voltage sags by the current times the cell’s internal resistance, and that one fact explains most of what batteries do in daily life. It is why a cold phone dies with charge left, why an old battery feels weaker long before it holds less, why fast charging slows down near the top, and why a cell built for a power tool differs from one built for a laptop. These experiments drain a cell, charge it, and open it up.

1. Discharge: voltage under load

At rest, the voltage of a cell depends only on how much lithium is in each electrode, falling from 4.2 V full to about 3.0 V empty with a long, gently sloping middle near 3.7 V. Draw a current and the terminals show less than that, because the ions must be pushed through the electrolyte and into the solid particles, and that costs voltage in proportion to the current: the cell behaves like an ideal source in series with a small resistance. Equipment switches off when the terminal voltage reaches a cut-off, not when the lithium runs out. A larger current reaches the cut-off earlier, and so does a larger resistance, which is what cold and age both produce: ions move more slowly in a cold electrolyte, and an old cell has grown a thicker film on its graphite.

Worked example. A new 3.0 Ah cell with 35 mΩ at 25 °C delivers 2.97 Ah and 10.7 Wh at 3 A (a rate of 1C). At 15 A the terminals start 0.5 V lower, the cell reaches 2.8 V after only 2.18 Ah and 7.1 Wh, and 7.9 W of heat is made inside it. At 0 °C the resistance is 73 mΩ and a 6 A load gets 2.40 Ah. After 500 cycles the capacity is 2.53 Ah and the resistance 46 mΩ.

Watch out. The open-circuit curve is a smooth fit of the right general shape for a nickel-manganese-cobalt cell, and the resistance is a single number that grows as the cell empties. A real cell also responds slowly: its voltage keeps sagging for minutes under load and recovers afterwards, and it warms itself up as it works, which lowers the resistance again. Ageing depends on temperature, voltage and current, not on cycle count alone.

Why does a phone in the cold switch off while still showing charge?

Cold raises the cell’s internal resistance two or three times. The phone’s current then causes a larger voltage drop inside the cell, the terminal voltage touches the cut-off, and the phone shuts down. The lithium is still there, and the phone works again once the cell warms.

2. Charging: constant current, then constant voltage

Charging runs the same resistance the other way: the charger must apply the cell’s own voltage plus the drop across its resistance. Two limits apply. The current must not be so large that lithium arrives at the graphite faster than it can move in, or it plates the surface as metal. And the terminal voltage must never exceed about 4.2 V, above which the electrolyte starts to break down. A charger therefore works in two stages. It holds the current until the terminals reach the limit; because of the resistive drop this happens before the cell is full. Then it holds the voltage and lets the current fall away, finishing when it has dropped to a small fraction of the cell’s capacity per hour. The first stage is fast and fills most of the cell; the second is slow, and the faster the first, the more is left for the second.

Worked example. At 3 A (1C) the terminals reach 4.2 V after 56 minutes with the cell 94 per cent full, and the taper takes another 11 minutes: 67 minutes in all, with 80 per cent reached at 48 minutes. At 6 A the constant-current stage ends at 84 per cent after 25 minutes, 80 per cent comes at 24 minutes, and the whole charge takes 40 minutes. At 9 A the total is still 32 minutes. Limiting the voltage to 4.10 V stores 94 per cent of the full charge.

Watch out. The cell is treated as a voltage source and a fixed resistance. Real fast charging is limited by lithium plating and by heat more than by this simple voltage limit, so chargers step the current down in stages as the cell fills and slow down when it is cold; the model has none of that and would let a cold cell charge at full current, which a real charger refuses to do.

Why does the last fifth of a charge take so long?

Near full, the cell’s own voltage is close to the 4.2 V limit, so only a small voltage is left to drive current through the internal resistance. The charger has to hold the voltage and accept whatever current that small difference pushes, which shrinks towards zero as the cell fills.

3. Inside the cell: the jelly roll

The working part of a cell is a sandwich a third of a millimetre thick: copper foil coated on both sides with graphite, a porous plastic separator, aluminium foil coated on both sides with a lithium metal oxide, and another separator, all soaked in a liquid electrolyte that carries lithium ions but not electrons. Capacity is set by how much coating there is, so to get a useful amount into a small can the sandwich is made as a long strip and wound into a spiral. For a given can, the thickness of the coating is the main choice. A thick coating stores more charge in each turn, but leaves room for fewer turns and so less foil area, and the ions must travel further through it; resistance rises steeply. Cells for laptops choose thick coatings; cells for power tools choose thin ones.

Worked example. With a 70 µm cathode coating the stack is \(140+15+154+10+32=351\) µm thick, so between radii of 2.0 mm and 8.6 mm there are 18.8 turns and the strip is \(\pi(8.6^2-2.0^2)/0.351=626\) mm long. Coated on both sides and 58 mm wide, that is 726 cm² of electrode; at 587 mAh per cm³ of coating it holds 2.98 Ah, about 10.9 Wh, in a cell of 52 g: 210 Wh/kg. A 40 µm coating gives 29 turns, 2.66 Ah and 20 mΩ; a 100 µm coating gives 14 turns, 3.14 Ah and 57 mΩ.

Watch out. The cell is a generic 18650 with representative dimensions and materials, not a particular product; the windings are drawn at their true pitch, so they are fine lines until you zoom in. The resistance model is a simple scaling. The colour change of the graphite is real (fully lithiated graphite is golden) but is shown here on the cut edge for clarity.

Why can a power-tool cell deliver far more current than a laptop cell of the same size?

Its electrode coatings are thinner. That fits more turns, and so more foil area, into the can, and gives the ions a shorter path, so the internal resistance is much lower and less voltage and heat are lost at high current. The price is less active material and a lower capacity.
