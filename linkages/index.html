Linkages and cams: motion from geometry

A shaft that turns is the cheapest motion there is. Almost every other motion a machine needs, a needle that stabs, a rib that unfolds, a valve that lifts and waits, a wiper that sweeps, is made from it by geometry alone: rigid bars joined by pins, or a shaped plate pushing a follower. No part stretches and nothing is computed, yet the output can pause, reverse, trace a figure of eight or run very nearly along a straight line. The questions are geometric ones. When can a bar make a full turn? What curve does a point on a moving bar draw? How should a profile be shaped so that the follower starts without a shock? These experiments answer them with circles and triangles.

1. Four bars

Three bars pinned in a triangle cannot move at all, and five have too much freedom. Four is the smallest closed chain that moves, and it moves in just one way: fix one bar, set the angle of a second, and the positions of the other two follow, because their free ends must meet. Finding where is a matter of intersecting two circles. What kind of motion results depends only on the four lengths. Grashof’s rule of 1883 says that some bar can turn completely if, and only if, the shortest and longest together are no longer than the other two together. If the shortest is the driven bar, it cranks round while the output rocks, as in a windscreen wiper or a treadle. If the shortest is the fixed bar, both neighbours rotate. Otherwise the linkage only rocks. Any point fixed to the coupler traces a closed curve, and the variety of those curves is what makes the device useful.

Worked example. Bars of 40, 120, 80 and 110 mm, with the 110 mm bar fixed: \(40+120=160\) is less than \(80+110=190\), and the shortest bar is the driven one, so it is a crank-rocker. As the crank goes round once, the 80 mm bar swings between 66° and 133°, through 67°, and the transmission angle stays between 34° and 95°. Make the fixed bar 150 mm with three bars of 100 mm, and \(100+150=250\) exceeds 200: nothing can turn fully.

Watch out. Rigid bars and perfect pins in one plane. Real pins have clearance, so the output position is slightly uncertain, most of all where the transmission angle is poor. The geometry says nothing of forces or inertia: at speed, the shaking of the moving bars has to be balanced. The linkage can also be assembled the other way up, with the coupler below the line of centres, giving a mirror set of motions.

A crank-rocker is used the other way round, the rocker driving the crank, in a treadle sewing machine. Why does it sometimes refuse to start?

Twice per turn the coupler lines up with the crank. There a push along the coupler has no leverage to turn the crank either way: a dead point. A flywheel carries the machine through, but from rest at a dead point it must be nudged by hand.

Reference: Y. Zhang, S. Finger and S. Behrens · Introduction to Mechanisms (Carnegie Mellon University)

2. A straight line from circles

A pin joint makes a circle, easily and exactly. A straight line is much harder. In 1784, when James Watt needed the piston rod of his engine to move in a line while the end of the beam it drove moved on an arc, no machine could plane a guide flat enough. His answer was a linkage: two long bars swinging on fixed pivots, joined by a short one whose midpoint moves, over part of its path, almost exactly straight. The whole path is a figure of eight; the straight part is where the two long bars’ opposite curvatures cancel. The search for better and then for exact straight-line linkages occupied mathematicians for a century. Chebyshev built a theory of approximation on it, the same theory now used to design filters. An exact solution, using inversion in a circle, was found by Peaucellier in 1864.

Worked example. Watt’s linkage with long bars of 2, a coupler of 1 and pivots offset by 4 and 1: over the straightest third of its travel, a stroke of 1.00, the midpoint strays 0.0006 from the line, one part in 1750. Chebyshev’s, with bars of 2.5, a coupler of 1 and pivots 2 apart, is straight to one part in 1040 over its best third. Hoeken’s, with a crank of 1, bars of 2.5 and pivots 2 apart, gives a stroke of 2.6 straight to one part in 470 from a crank that turns continuously.

Watch out. The deviation is measured over the straightest third of the point’s travel, as a share of the length of that stretch; a shorter stretch is straighter and a longer one less so. The proportions are the classical ones, not optimised for this measure. Play in the pins and bending of the bars add errors larger than these in any real mechanism.

Watt’s linkage is fitted across the rear axle of some cars, with its long bars running to the body on either side. What does it do there?

The centre of the short bar is pinned to the axle. As the suspension moves up and down that point travels in a nearly straight vertical line, so the axle cannot shift sideways under the body in a corner, yet is free to rise and fall.

Reference: Y. Zhang, S. Finger and S. Behrens · Introduction to Mechanisms (Carnegie Mellon University)

3. Cams: any motion you can draw

A linkage gives a limited family of motions. A cam gives any. It is a plate whose radius varies round its edge, turning on a shaft, with a follower pressed against it by a spring: the follower’s lift at each instant is simply the radius there less the smallest radius. So the designer draws the lift wanted against shaft angle, rise, dwell, fall and rest, and wraps the graph round a circle. The subtlety is in how the rise is shaped. The follower has mass, so the force on it is set by its acceleration, the second derivative of the lift curve. A curve that looks perfectly smooth can have an acceleration that jumps, and each jump is a blow that excites vibration and wears the surfaces. Good cam laws are chosen for their second and third derivatives. The cycloidal law, a ramp minus one cycle of a sine, starts and ends with no acceleration at all.

Worked example. A lift of 8 mm in 60° of cam rotation at 3000 turns a minute, 314 rad/s. With the cycloidal law the follower peaks at \(2\pi\times0.008/1.047^{2}\times314^{2}=4520\) m/s², 460 times gravity, and at 4.8 m/s. A 100 g follower then needs 450 N. The harmonic law peaks lower, at 3550 m/s², and constant acceleration lower still, at 2880, but both apply it instantly. Doubling the speed quadruples every figure.

Watch out. The follower is a flat face moving along a line through the shaft, and is treated as rigid. A roller follower needs the profile offset by the roller’s radius, and a rise that is too quick for the base circle makes the profile undercut or the follower jam sideways. Real valve trains flex, so the valve’s motion differs from the cam’s, and modern laws are polynomials chosen to keep the third derivative finite as well.

An engine’s valve springs are just strong enough at 6000 turns a minute of the camshaft. Roughly how much stronger must they be at 7000?

\((7000/6000)^{2}=1.36\): over a third stronger. The acceleration the spring must supply to keep the follower on the closing side of the cam grows as the square of the speed.

Reference: Y. Zhang, S. Finger and S. Behrens · Introduction to Mechanisms (Carnegie Mellon University)
