Particle physics: the Standard Model at a glance

Everything that has been seen in a particle collision is described by one theory: six quarks and six leptons, the force carriers that act between them, and the Higgs boson. Most of these particles live for far less than a billionth of a second, so nobody has ever watched one directly. What is measured are the tracks and energy deposits of their decay products, and what is reconstructed from them is a quantity that does not depend on how the parent moved: its mass. The same habit of looking for what stays fixed organises the whole subject. Hadrons fall into patterns fixed by a symmetry among quarks, and the strengths of the forces change with energy in a way fixed by the particles that exist. These experiments rebuild a particle from its tracks in a detector, assemble hadrons from quarks, and follow the three gauge couplings from laboratory energies towards the scale where they nearly meet.

Before you begin: Special relativity (energy and momentum), basic quantum mechanics, the Lorentz force

1. Invariant mass: rebuilding a particle from its tracks

A short-lived particle decays into two charged particles at the centre of a detector. A solenoid fills the tracker with a magnetic field along the beam, so each track is an arc of a circle whose radius gives its momentum across the field, \(p_T=0.3\,BR\) in GeV, tesla and metres. From the two momenta and the known masses of the daughters we form \(m^2=(E_1+E_2)^2-|\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2|^2\). Energy and momentum are conserved in the decay and this combination is the same in every frame, so it equals the squared mass of the parent, however fast it moved and whichever way it broke up. Over many events the values pile into a peak at the parent’s mass, whose width is the natural width \(\Gamma=\hbar/\tau\) smeared by the precision of the tracker.

Worked example. A \(\rho^0\) of momentum 4 GeV decaying at \(\cos\theta^{*}=0.3\) gives pions of 2.597 and 1.478 GeV; their sum is \(\sqrt{4^2+0.775^2}=4.074\) GeV and the invariant mass is 0.775 GeV whatever the angle. A \(Z\) at rest gives two muons of 45.6 GeV: in a 2 T field their radius is 76 m, so across a 1.1 m tracker the track departs from a straight line by only \(0.3\times2\times1.1^2/(8\times45.6)=2.0\) mm. A \(K^0_S\) of 3 GeV flies \(\beta\gamma c\tau=(3/0.4976)\times2.68=16.2\) cm on average before its pions appear.

Watch out. The detector is a generic design, not a particular experiment, drawn with half of it removed; the decay is shown in the plane across the beam. The field is taken as uniform inside the solenoid and zero outside, so tracks are straight beyond it. Pions stop in the hadron calorimeter and muons pass through to the muon stations. The histogram uses a simple resolution model (a curvature term proportional to \(p_T/B\) and a constant scattering term), and backgrounds are left out.

Why is the invariant mass, rather than the energy, used to identify a decaying particle?

The energies of the decay products depend on the parent’s speed and on the direction of the decay, so they differ from event to event. The invariant mass of the pair is the same in every frame and equals the parent’s mass, so all decays of the same particle land on the same value.

Reference: S. Navas et al. (Particle Data Group) · Review of Particle Physics, Physical Review D 110, 030001 (2024)

2. The quark model: weights that add

By 1960 dozens of strongly interacting particles were known. Plotted by two conserved labels, isospin \(I_3\) and hypercharge \(Y\), they fell into hexagons and triangles of eight and ten: the weight diagrams of the symmetry group SU(3). Gell-Mann and Zweig explained the patterns in 1964 with three constituents, the up, down and strange quarks, which form the smallest triangle. A baryon is three quarks and a meson is a quark with an antiquark, and the position of a hadron is simply the sum of its quarks’ positions. The electric charge follows from the position, \(Q=I_3+\tfrac12Y\). Within a pattern the masses are split almost entirely by the number of strange quarks, which is what allowed the tenth member of the decuplet to be predicted before it was seen.

Worked example. The proton is \(uud\): \(I_3=\tfrac12+\tfrac12-\tfrac12=\tfrac12\), \(Y=3\times\tfrac13=1\), so \(Q=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1\). In the decuplet the steps are \(1384.6-1232=152.6\) and \(1533.4-1384.6=148.8\) MeV, so equal spacing puts the \(sss\) state at \(2\times1533.4-1384.6=1682\) MeV; the \(\Omega^-\) was found at 1672 MeV. In the octet \(2(m_N+m_\Xi)=4514\) MeV and \(3m_\Lambda+m_\Sigma=4540\) MeV, agreeing to 0.6%.

Watch out. Only the three light flavours are shown; charm, bottom and top extend the scheme but the symmetry among them is badly broken by their masses. The neutral mesons at the centre are mixtures of \(u\bar u\), \(d\bar d\) and \(s\bar s\), not single quark pairs. Isospin multiplets are averaged in the mass plots, and the \(\Delta\) mass is the nominal 1232 MeV.

Why is there no baryon made of three up quarks in the octet that contains the proton?

Three identical quarks can only be in a flavour state that is symmetric under exchange. The symmetric states of three quarks form the decuplet, with spin three-halves; the octet’s states have mixed symmetry and need at least two different flavours. So \(uuu\) appears only as the \(\Delta^{++}\).

Reference: S. Navas et al. (Particle Data Group) · Review of Particle Physics, Physical Review D 110, 030001 (2024)

3. Running couplings: forces that change with energy

The strength of a force is not a fixed number. A charge is surrounded by virtual particles that screen or enhance it, so the coupling measured depends on the energy of the probe. At one loop the inverse coupling changes linearly with the logarithm of the energy, with a slope set by counting the particles that carry the charge. For electromagnetism the slope makes the force slowly stronger at high energy. For the strong force the gluons, which carry colour themselves, outweigh the quarks and reverse the sign: the coupling falls at high energy, asymptotic freedom, and grows at low energy until quarks are confined. Extended over fourteen powers of ten, the three gauge couplings of the Standard Model come close to a single value.

Worked example. With \(\alpha_s(M_Z)=0.118\) and five flavours, \(b_0=23/3\) and \(1/\alpha_s(10\ \mathrm{GeV})=8.475+\tfrac{23}{6\pi}\ln(10/91.19)=5.78\), so \(\alpha_s=0.173\); at 1 TeV it has fallen to 0.088, and the one-loop formula diverges at \(\Lambda=88\) MeV. At \(M_Z\) the inverse couplings are 59.0, 29.6 and 8.5. In the Standard Model the pairs meet at \(10^{13.0}\), \(10^{14.4}\) and \(10^{17.0}\) GeV; with supersymmetric partners at 1 TeV all three meet between \(10^{16.0}\) and \(10^{16.4}\) GeV, at \(1/\alpha\approx26\).

Watch out. These are one-loop formulas with a fixed number of flavours and no threshold effects, so they are only a guide: precise work uses four or five loops and changes \(n_f\) at each quark mass, which raises the low-energy values. Supersymmetric partners have not been observed; the second curve shows what they would do, not what is known to happen. Whether the couplings unify at all is an open question.

Why does the strong force get weaker at high energies when electromagnetism gets stronger?

Virtual pairs of charged particles screen an electric charge, so probing closer sees more of it. Quark pairs screen colour in the same way, but gluons carry colour too and their effect is the opposite and larger: with fewer than seventeen flavours the net result is anti-screening, and the coupling shrinks at short distances.

Reference: S. Navas et al. (Particle Data Group) · Review of Particle Physics, Physical Review D 110, 030001 (2024)
