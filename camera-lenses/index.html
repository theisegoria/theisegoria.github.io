Camera lenses and autofocus

A camera lens has one job: to bring the light from each point of a scene back together at one point of the sensor. It can do that exactly for only one distance at a time, so everything nearer or farther is drawn as a small disc instead of a point, and the photographer chooses, with the aperture, how fast those discs grow. The glass itself is a compromise too: spherical surfaces are easy to polish but do not focus perfectly, which is why a good lens has six or more elements where one would seem to do, and why every lens is sharpest a few stops down from wide open. Finding focus quickly is a separate problem, solved by letting the sensor compare the views through the two halves of the lens. These experiments set the depth of field, open a lens to see the glass, the iris and the focusing movement, and focus a camera both ways.

1. Depth of field

A lens of focal length \(f\) focused at distance \(s\) forms a sharp image only of things at that distance. A point at any other distance \(d\) comes to a focus in front of or behind the sensor, and on the sensor its light covers a disc. Similar triangles give the size of that disc: it is proportional to the diameter of the aperture, \(f/N\), and to how far the point is from the focused distance measured in the way a lens sees distance, \(|1/d-1/s|\). If the disc is smaller than the eye can tell from a point in the final picture, about 0.03 mm on a full-frame sensor, the point looks sharp. The range of distances that pass this test is the depth of field. It grows as the aperture closes, shrinks rapidly with focal length, and reaches all the way to infinity once the lens is focused at the hyperfocal distance.

Worked example. A 50 mm lens at \(f/2.8\) with \(c=0.03\) mm has \(H=50^2/(2.8\times0.03)+50=29.8\) m. Focused at 3 m it is sharp from 2.73 m to 3.33 m, a zone 0.60 m deep. At \(f/8\) the hyperfocal distance falls to 10.5 m and the zone widens to 2.34 m to 4.19 m. An 85 mm lens at \(f/1.8\) focused at 2 m holds only 1.97 m to 2.03 m, six centimetres, while a 24 mm lens at \(f/8\) focused at 3 m is sharp from 1.34 m to infinity.

Watch out. This is the thin-lens model, which treats the lens as a single surface; it is accurate at ordinary distances and less so for close-up work, where the pupils of a real lens matter. The 0.03 mm criterion assumes a full-frame sensor and a moderate print; a smaller sensor or a larger print needs a stricter one. Diffraction, treated in the next experiment, is left out here.

Why does a portrait lens blur the background so much more than a wide-angle lens at the same f-number?

The blur disc is proportional to \(f^2/N\). At the same f-number an 85 mm lens has an opening three and a half times wider than a 24 mm lens and also magnifies the blur more, so its discs are about twelve times larger.

Reference: R. Kingslake · Optics in Photography (SPIE Press)

2. Inside the lens: glass, iris and the sharpest aperture

A single piece of glass with spherical faces does not bring parallel light to one point: rays through its edge cross the axis nearer the lens than rays through its middle. The resulting blur, spherical aberration, shrinks with the cube of the f-number, so stopping down cures it quickly. A photographic lens instead shares the bending among several elements, positive and negative, arranged almost symmetrically about the iris so that the errors of one group cancel those of the other. The iris is a ring of thin curved blades that swing together to leave a nearly circular opening. Closing it also runs into a limit that no design can remove: light passing through a small hole spreads, and the smallest spot it can form, the Airy disc, grows in proportion to the f-number. Between the two limits lies the sharpest aperture.

Worked example. For a single 50 mm element at \(f/2.8\) the aberration blur is \(0.067\times50/2.8^3=0.153\) mm, five times the 0.03 mm that counts as sharp; it falls to 6.5 µm at \(f/8\), where the Airy disc for green light is \(2.44\times0.55\ \mu\mathrm{m}\times8=10.7\) µm, so a single element is at its best there, about 12.6 µm. A six-element design that cuts the aberration forty-fold reaches 5.0 µm at \(f/3.2\). To focus from infinity down to 3 m the whole optical group moves forward \(50^2/(3000-50)=0.85\) mm; to reach 0.45 m it moves 6.25 mm.

Watch out. The glass is drawn to the proportions of a double-Gauss lens but is not a ray-traced prescription, and the gold rays are a sketch of the cone of light, not exact paths. The forty-fold correction is a representative figure for the centre of the picture; real lenses are also limited by other aberrations that grow towards the corners, and many modern lenses focus by moving a small inner group, not the whole cell.

Why is a lens not at its sharpest either wide open or fully stopped down?

Wide open, the outer parts of the glass are in use and their aberrations blur the image; that blur falls as the cube of the f-number. Stopped far down, the small opening spreads the light by diffraction, a blur that rises in proportion to the f-number. The sharpest setting is where the two are about equal.

Reference: R. Kingslake · Optics in Photography (SPIE Press)

3. Autofocus: phase detection and contrast

There are two ways for a camera to find focus by looking through its own lens. Contrast detection uses the fact that a focused image has the strongest fine detail: the camera moves the lens a little, measures the contrast, and keeps going while it rises. It is accurate but slow, because a single measurement says neither which way to move nor how far, and the peak is recognised only after passing it. Phase detection compares two views. Light from the left half of the lens and light from the right half arrive at the same place on the sensor only when the image is in focus; otherwise the two half-images are displaced sideways, in one direction if the lens is too far forward and the other if it is too far back. Pixels masked to see one half each measure that displacement, and one reading gives the full correction.

Worked example. With the sensor 0.6 mm from focus at \(f/2.8\) the blur disc is \(0.6/2.8=0.214\) mm across and the two half-aperture images are \(0.424\times0.214=0.091\) mm apart, about 23 pixels of 4 µm, an easy thing to measure. Wide open at \(f/1.4\) the same error gives 0.182 mm. The contrast of a pattern of 10 line pairs per mm, by comparison, is under three per cent at that defocus, flat and useless as a guide, and climbs back only within about 0.3 mm of focus.

Watch out. The two half-images are drawn for a simple edge and stripe, with the blur modelled as a uniform disc and each half as a half disc; real phase-detection pixels see overlapping parts of the pupil and need a calibration. The contrast search shown is a plain halving search, simpler than the predictive routines in a real camera, and noise and subject movement are left out.

Why can phase detection focus in one movement when contrast detection has to hunt?

The displacement between the two half-aperture images is proportional to the focus error and changes sign with its direction, so one measurement gives both the distance and the way to go. Contrast is the same on both sides of focus and says only how good the present position is, so the camera must move to learn anything.

Reference: R. Kingslake · Optics in Photography (SPIE Press)
