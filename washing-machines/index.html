Washing machines: tumbling, spinning and staying put

A front-loading washing machine uses one drum at two very different speeds. Slowly, it lifts the washing and drops it into the water, and the trick is to turn just slowly enough that gravity can still pull the clothes off the wall. Fast, it pins everything to the wall with hundreds of times the force of gravity and flings the water out through the holes. Between the two it has to pass through a speed at which the whole tub wants to shake itself to pieces, and it must then spin smoothly with a lopsided load that nobody arranged. These experiments find the speed at which washing stops falling, measure what a faster spin buys, and hang the tub on its springs with a lump of wet towels inside.

1. Tumbling: the speed at which washing falls

A garment resting against the wall of a turning drum is moving in a circle, and something must supply the inward force that a circle needs. Near the bottom the wall supplies it easily. As the garment climbs past the level of the axle, gravity begins to point partly inward and does some of the work, so the wall has to push less. At the height where the inward part of gravity alone is enough, the wall pushes with nothing, and above that the garment would have to be pulled outward to stay on, which a wall cannot do. It lets go and falls as a thrown object would. If the drum turns fast enough that even at the very top gravity is not enough, the garment never lets go. That critical speed depends only on the radius of the drum.

Worked example. A drum 48 cm across has \(r=0.24\) m, so \(\omega_{\rm crit}=\sqrt{9.81/0.24}=6.39\) rad/s, which is 61 rpm. At a washing speed of 48 rpm, \(\omega^{2}r/g=0.62\): the washing lets go 38° above the horizontal, rises a little further, then falls 41 cm and lands at 2.9 m/s. At 60 rpm it lets go almost at the top and falls only 7 cm.

Watch out. The garment is a point that does not slide on the wall, which the lifters make roughly true, and it flies without air resistance or collisions with other washing. A real load is a tangled mass that fills a third of the drum, so machines are tuned by experiment around the speed this gives.

A larger drum must turn more slowly to wash. Why, and what does the washing gain from it?

The critical speed is \(\sqrt{g/r}\), which falls as the radius grows, so the washing speed must fall with it. The gain is a longer drop: the fall is a fixed fraction of the diameter, and a higher fall means a harder landing and more flexing of the cloth.

Reference: J. P. Den Hartog · Mechanical Vibrations (Dover), chapter 2 on rotating unbalance and vibration isolation

2. Spinning the water out

Well above the critical speed the washing is pressed against the wall with a force many times its weight. It is convenient to count that force in units of gravity: the ratio of the inward acceleration \(\omega^{2}r\) to \(g\). Water in the cloth feels the same pull and is driven outward through the fabric and the holes in the drum. Because the pull goes as the square of the speed, a modest increase in rpm is a large increase in force. But the water does not all come out. Some sits in the large gaps between threads and leaves readily; the rest is held in the fine spaces inside each yarn by surface tension, and the finer the space the harder it is to shift. So the water left falls quickly at first and then flattens toward a floor that only heat can remove.

Worked example. At 1400 rpm, \(\omega=146.6\) rad/s. With \(r=0.24\) m the pull is \(146.6^{2}\times0.24/9.81=526\,g\) and the wall moves at 127 km/h. Cotton is left holding 48 per cent of its dry weight, so a 7 kg load carries 3.3 kg of water, which takes 2.4 kWh to evaporate. At 1000 rpm the pull is \(268\,g\), 60 per cent is left, and the load carries 0.9 kg more water.

Watch out. The curve for water left is a fit through the spin-drying classes printed on European energy labels for cotton, not a physical law. Synthetics hold far less. Evaporation is costed at 2.6 MJ per kilogram of water, which covers warming it and boiling it off but none of the losses of a real dryer.

Two machines both spin at 1400 rpm, but one has a drum 10 cm wider. Which leaves the washing drier?

The wider one. The pull is \(\omega^{2}r\), so at the same speed it is proportional to the radius: a 58 cm drum gives about a fifth more than a 48 cm one. Rpm alone does not say how hard a machine spins.

Reference: J. P. Den Hartog · Mechanical Vibrations (Dover), chapter 2 on rotating unbalance and vibration isolation

3. Balance: springs, dampers and concrete

Wet washing never spreads itself evenly, so the spinning drum carries a lump on one side, and that lump pulls on the axle with a force that grows as the square of the speed. Bolted rigidly to the floor, the machine would hammer the house. So the tub is hung on springs. A mass on springs has its own rate of bouncing, and what happens depends on how the drum speed compares with it. Well below, the tub simply follows the slow pull. Close to it, each turn adds to the last and the swing grows until friction in the dampers limits it. Well above, the tub has no time to respond: it stays almost still while the drum turns about the centre of mass of drum and lump together, and the springs, hardly stretched, pass very little force to the cabinet. The swing that remains is the lump’s mass times its radius, divided by the mass of the tub, so the cure for a small remaining swing is to make the tub heavy.

Worked example. A 60 kg tub on springs of 14.8 kN/m bounces at 2.5 Hz, or 150 rpm. A 0.5 kg lump at 0.24 m gives \(me/M=2\) mm. At 150 rpm with \(\zeta=0.2\) the swing is \(2/(2\times0.2)=5\) mm. At 1400 rpm, \(r=9.3\), the swing is 2.0 mm and the lump sits on the inside of the orbit. The lump pulls on the drum with \(0.5\times0.24\times146.6^{2}=2580\) N, but only 116 N reaches the floor. Halve the tub to 30 kg and the swing at speed doubles to 4.1 mm.

Watch out. The tub is treated as a single mass moving in a circle, with the same stiffness and damping in every direction. A real tub also rocks about two axes, and its dampers are friction struts that grip harder at low speed. The machine in the picture is a generic front-loader drawn to scale; the shaking is enlarged ten times and the rotation slowed so that the phase can be seen.

Why does a machine pause, turn slowly back and forth, and try again before a fast spin?

It has measured too large a lump, from the ripple in the motor’s speed or current as the lump is lifted on each turn. The swing at speed is proportional to the lump, and the resonance must be crossed on the way up, so it loosens the load and lets it settle more evenly before trying again.

Reference: J. P. Den Hartog · Mechanical Vibrations (Dover), chapter 2 on rotating unbalance and vibration isolation
