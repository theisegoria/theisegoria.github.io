Zippers: a wedge that locks hooks

A zipper is two rows of identical hooks and a wedge. Each tooth has a bump on one face and a hollow on the other, and when the rows are meshed every bump sits in the hollow of the tooth above it on the other side. Once in, a tooth cannot be pulled straight out sideways, because its neighbours are in the way; it can only leave by tilting, and it can only tilt if the tape it is fixed to is bent. The slider is the one place where the tapes are bent, and it bends them just enough, one tooth at a time. These experiments run a slider along a chain, treat the slider as the wedge it is, and find how sharply a tape must curve before its teeth let go.

1. Teeth and slider

The teeth on the two tapes are identical and equally spaced, but one row is shifted by half a space, so that along the closed chain they alternate: left, right, left, right. The slider is a flat box with one opening at the bottom and two at the top, divided by a wedge. As it is pulled upward, the separate rows enter the two upper openings and are guided along curved walls toward the single lower one. Because the rows arrive at an angle, the teeth of each are fanned apart at their tips. Into each widened gap the head of a tooth from the other row is pushed, and as the channel straightens, the gap closes round it. Pulled downward, the wedge at the top drives between the rows and the same motion runs in reverse. Nothing about the teeth is asymmetric between closing and opening; only the direction of travel differs.

Worked example. In this zipper the teeth are 4 mm apart on each tape, so along the closed chain a tooth comes every 2 mm. Each row turns through 27° inside the slider. Three units of length beyond the throat, that is 15 mm along a row, the row has moved 5.2 mm sideways and 13.8 mm upward, and the two rows are 10.5 mm apart.

Watch out. The zipper is a chunky metal one drawn larger than life, with the front plate of the slider made transparent. The teeth are simplified to a block, a clamp and a rounded head; real ones are stamped with a precise bump and socket. Coil zippers, where each row is one continuous spiral of nylon, work on the same principle.

Why can the two rows of a zipper be made from the same teeth, with no left and right?

Each tooth has its bump on top and its hollow underneath, so a tooth on either side presents a bump to the tooth above it and a hollow to the one below. Turning a row over to face the other way changes nothing about that, and the half-space shift places every bump under a hollow.

Reference: G. Sundback · Separable fastener, US Patent 1,219,881 (1917)

2. The slider is a wedge

Where the two rows meet, the cloth is trying to pull them apart, and the walls of the slider have to push them together. Those walls are inclined to the direction of travel, so the slider is a wedge: a pull along the zipper becomes a push across it. If the walls make a half-angle \(\alpha\) with the chain and the coefficient of friction is \(\mu\), then to close two rows that each resist with a sideways force \(N\) takes a pull of \(2N\tan(\alpha+\phi)\), where \(\tan\phi=\mu\). A narrow Y gives a large sideways force for a small pull. The same reasoning tells what happens when the slider is let go. The tapes push back on the walls, and that push has a component down the zipper of \(2N\tan(\alpha-\phi)\). If the half-angle is less than the angle of friction this is not positive, and no sideways pull can move the slider.

Worked example. With \(\mu=0.2\) the angle of friction is 11.3°. For a half-angle of 25° and 20 N of sideways pull on each tape, the pull to close is \(2\times20\times\tan36.3^\circ=29.4\) N and the push back on a free slider is \(2\times20\times\tan13.7^\circ=9.7\) N. Bringing the rows together through 3 mm takes arms \(3/\tan25^\circ=6.4\) mm long. At 10° the pull falls to 15.6 N and the slider locks itself, but the arms must be 17 mm long.

Watch out. The slider is treated as a plain wedge with flat walls and one coefficient of friction. The sideways force is that of the tapes over the short length inside the slider, not the tension in the whole garment, and the small effort of tilting each tooth into place is left out.

Jeans zippers have a small pin inside the slider that drops between the teeth when the tab hangs down. Why is it needed?

A practical slider is short, so its Y is wider than the angle of friction, and tension across the fly would push it slowly downward. The pin blocks the slider against the teeth until the tab is lifted to pull it out.

Reference: G. Sundback · Separable fastener, US Patent 1,219,881 (1917)

3. Why closed teeth hold

In the closed chain each head lies in the slot between two teeth of the other row, with its bump in the hollow of the tooth above. To come out sideways the head would have to push those two teeth apart by the height of the bump, and it cannot, because they are clamped to a tape that does not stretch. But the teeth stand out from the tape like spokes. If the tape is bent into an arc with the teeth on the outside, their tips move apart while their roots stay the same distance along the tape. For teeth of length \(L\) spaced \(P\) apart on a bend of radius \(R\), each slot widens at the tip by \(PL/R\). When that reaches the height of the bump the head can pass. A zipper is designed so that this radius is far tighter than any curve the garment takes, and is met only inside the slider.

Worked example. Teeth 3 mm long and 2.6 mm apart with a bump of 0.35 mm let go when the tape is bent tighter than \(2.6\times3/0.35=22\) mm. At a radius of 30 mm the slots widen by only 0.26 mm and the chain stays locked. Ten centimetres of closed chain holds \(2\times100/2.6=77\) teeth, so a pull of 200 N across it puts 2.6 N on each.

Watch out. The teeth are rigid rectangles on a tape that bends but does not stretch, and the load is shared equally between them. In a real zipper the tape and its stitching give a little, the load concentrates near the ends, and failure usually begins where one tooth is missing or bent.

A closed zipper can be forced open by folding it sharply back on itself at one point. Why does that work?

Folding bends the tape to a radius of a few millimetres with the teeth on the outside, far tighter than the critical radius. The slots at the fold open wider than the bumps, the teeth there fall apart, and the rest can then be peeled open from that point.

Reference: G. Sundback · Separable fastener, US Patent 1,219,881 (1917)
