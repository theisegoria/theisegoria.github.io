Helicopter rotors: lift, control and a safe way down

A helicopter hangs from a spinning wing. Each blade is an airfoil swept round at about 200 m/s at its tip, and the whole rotor is steered by twisting the blades as they turn. The mechanism that does it is the swashplate: a pair of rings around the mast, one held still and tilted by the pilot, the other spinning with the blades and pushing their pitch horns up and down once per revolution. In forward flight the rotor faces a deep asymmetry, because the advancing blade meets the airflow at the sum of its own speed and the aircraft’s while the retreating blade meets their difference; hinged blades flap up and down to balance it. And if the engine fails, the rotor can keep turning on the air rising through it and let the aircraft down at a survivable speed. These experiments drive a swashplate, map the angle of attack over a rotor disc in forward flight, and apply momentum theory from hover to autorotation.

1. The swashplate: steering by twisting the blades

Each blade sits in a grip that can turn about the blade’s own axis, and a short pitch horn sticks out from the grip. A pitch link joins the horn to the rotating ring of the swashplate. Raise the whole plate and every horn rises together: collective pitch \(\theta_0\), more thrust. Tilt the plate and each horn rises on one side of the revolution and falls on the other, so the pitch varies once per turn as \(\theta_{1c}\cos\psi+\theta_{1s}\sin\psi\). Because the rotor responds to a once-per-revolution push like a resonant oscillator, about a quarter turn later, the rotor disc tilts in the direction set by the stick and the thrust tilts with it.

Worked example. With \(\theta_0=6^\circ\) and forward cyclic \(\theta_{1s}=-4^\circ\), the advancing blade at \(\psi=90^\circ\) has \(6-4=2^\circ\) of pitch and the retreating blade at \(\psi=270^\circ\) has \(6+4=10^\circ\). With a pitch horn of \(r_h=0.27\) and a link circle of radius \(\sqrt{0.42^2+0.27^2}=0.50\), a pitch change of \(4^\circ\) needs the link to move \(0.27\times4^\circ\times\pi/180=0.019\), and the plate tilts by \(\arctan(0.019/0.50)=2.2^\circ\).

Watch out. The linkage is drawn with the small-angle relation between horn travel and pitch, and with the pitch links at a fixed lead angle \(\delta\); real heads add a phase offset so that the stick and the disc tilt agree, and some have more or fewer blades. The blades are shortened and the head is drawn generically, not as any particular aircraft.

Why must the pitch change once per revolution, rather than the whole rotor simply tilting on the mast?

The rotor is a large spinning mass, and tilting it directly would need enormous gyroscopic moments. Changing each blade’s pitch as it goes round makes the aerodynamic forces themselves tilt the disc, with only small control forces; the swashplate converts the pilot’s steady tilt into that once-per-revolution change.

Reference: J. Gordon Leishman · Principles of Helicopter Aerodynamics, 2nd edition, Cambridge University Press (2006)

2. Forward flight: asymmetry, flapping and reverse flow

At advance ratio \(\mu=V/\Omega R\) a blade section at radius \(r\) meets air at \(U_T=\Omega R(r+\mu\sin\psi)\): faster on the advancing side, slower on the retreating side, and backwards inside a circle of diameter \(\mu R\) on the retreating side. A blade hinged at the root is free to flap. Lift, inertia and centrifugal stiffening make it a resonant oscillator at exactly once per revolution, \(\beta^{\prime\prime}+\beta=M_{\rm aero}\), damped by the change of angle of attack that flapping itself causes. The solution here is found by integrating that equation until it repeats. The advancing blade rises, the retreating blade falls, and the angle of attack evens out across the disc, until at high speed the retreating blade runs out of angle and stalls.

Worked example. In hover with Lock number \(\gamma=8\), \(\theta_0=8^\circ\) and inflow \(\lambda=0.05\), the coning angle is \(8\,(0.1396/8-0.05/6)=0.0730\) rad \(=4.2^\circ\). In forward flight at \(\mu=0.25\) with \(8^\circ\) of twist and no cyclic, the computed flapping is \(\beta_0=4.3^\circ\), \(\beta_{1c}=4.0^\circ\) and \(\beta_{1s}=1.4^\circ\): the disc tilts \(4^\circ\) back and the blade is highest at \(\psi\approx205^\circ\), over the nose, a quarter turn after the advancing side. Forward cyclic \(\theta_{1s}=-4^\circ\) cancels almost all of the tilt, leaving \(\beta_{1c}=-0.5^\circ\). The reverse-flow circle reaches a quarter of the way out along the retreating blade.

Watch out. The model assumes a centrally hinged rigid blade, uniform inflow \(\lambda\), a lift slope independent of Mach number and no stall in the forces; the stall boundary at \(12^\circ\) is drawn from the computed angle of attack only. Inflow in forward flight is in fact far from uniform, which shifts the lateral flapping. The disc map shows angles of attack from 0 to \(14^\circ\).

Why does the maximum flap come a quarter of a revolution after the maximum lift?

A centrally hinged blade has a natural flapping frequency of exactly once per revolution, so the once-per-revolution lift drives it at resonance. A resonantly driven, damped oscillator lags its forcing by \(90^\circ\): the blade is still rising while the extra lift acts and reaches its highest point a quarter turn later.

Reference: J. Gordon Leishman · Principles of Helicopter Aerodynamics, 2nd edition, Cambridge University Press (2006)

3. Momentum theory: hover power and autorotation

Treat the rotor as a disc that pushes air downward. In hover the air falls through the disc at the induced velocity \(v_h\) and leaves the wake at \(2v_h\), so the thrust is \(T=2\rho Av_h^2\) and the ideal power is \(Tv_h=T^{3/2}/\sqrt{2\rho A}\). Real rotors need about a third more, the figure of merit FM being the ratio of ideal to actual power. Climbing lowers the induced velocity; descending raises it until, between \(V_c=0\) and \(V_c=-2v_h\), the rotor sinks into its own wake: the vortex ring state, rough and dangerous, where momentum theory breaks down. Below it, the air rises through the disc and can drive the rotor: at ideal autorotation \(V_c+v_i=0\) and no power is needed at all.

Worked example. A 2000 kg helicopter with a rotor of radius 5 m at sea level has disc loading \(19\,620/78.5=250\) N/m² and \(v_h=\sqrt{19\,620/(2\times1.225\times78.5)}=10.1\) m/s. Its ideal hover power is \(19\,620\times10.1=198\) kW, and with FM = 0.75 it needs 264 kW. With the empirical fit (\(\kappa=1\)), ideal vertical autorotation occurs at \(V_c=-1.78\,v_h=-17.9\) m/s, about 3500 ft/min; in practice pilots autorotate with forward speed, which cuts the descent rate to about half.

Watch out. Momentum theory assumes a uniform disc, steady flow and no swirl. In the vortex ring state its assumptions fail, and the dashed curve is Leishman’s empirical fit to flight-test data, used here with \(\kappa=1\) so that it meets momentum theory in hover. Air density falls with altitude in the model as \(e^{-h/8500\,\mathrm{m}}\).

Why does doubling the rotor radius cut the ideal hover power by half, not by a quarter?

Ideal power is \(T^{3/2}/\sqrt{2\rho A}\), and the disc area \(A=\pi R^2\) enters under a square root. Doubling \(R\) quadruples \(A\) and halves \(1/\sqrt A\): the rotor moves twice as much air at half the speed, and the power, thrust times induced velocity, halves.

Reference: J. Gordon Leishman · Principles of Helicopter Aerodynamics, 2nd edition, Cambridge University Press (2006)
