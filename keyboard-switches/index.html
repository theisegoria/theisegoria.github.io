Keyboard switches: springs, contacts and the key matrix

A key on a mechanical keyboard is a small machine with one job: to close an electrical contact at a known point in its travel and to tell your finger that it has done so. A spring pushes back, a plastic stem slides in a housing, and a leg on the stem releases a strip of sprung metal that snaps against a contact. Everything a typist feels, from the weight of the key to the bump or click halfway down, is the shape of one curve of force against distance. Behind the switch are two problems in electronics. Metal contacts do not close cleanly; they bounce for a few thousandths of a second, and the keyboard must not mistake the bounces for extra presses. And a hundred keys must be read through twenty wires, which works until three keys held together fool the keyboard into seeing a fourth. These experiments press a switch, clean up its signal, and wire the matrix.

1. The switch and its force curve

The spring inside a switch is already squeezed a little when the key is at rest, so the key resists from the first touch, and its force then rises steadily with travel: that straight line is a linear switch. A tactile switch adds a bump. A sloped leg on the stem has to push a bent metal leaf aside to get past, which adds force just before the contacts close and removes it just after, so the finger feels the key give way at the moment it registers. A clicky switch stores that energy in a separate part that is released all at once and strikes the housing. Friction between stem and housing opposes motion both ways, so the curve on the way back up lies below the curve on the way down. The area under the downward curve is the work the finger does; the area between the two is what is lost.

Worked example. A linear switch rated 45 cN has a spring that gives 30 cN at rest and 7.5 cN more per millimetre, so with 3 cN of friction the finger feels 33 cN at the top, 48 cN at the 2 mm actuation point and 63 cN at the bottom; a full press takes 1.92 mJ, of which 1.68 mJ comes back and 0.24 mJ is lost. The tactile version peaks at 60 cN at 1.3 mm and has dropped to 43 cN by the actuation point; the clicky one peaks at 66 cN at 1.5 mm. Ten thousand keystrokes, a long day of typing, cost about 20 joules.

Watch out. The switch is a generic design of the common cross-stem pattern, with representative dimensions, and the bump is a smooth curve fitted to the general shape of published force curves, not a model of the leaf itself. Real curves vary between makers and between individual switches, and the sound of a switch, which matters as much to many typists, is not modelled.

Why does a tactile switch let you type without pressing each key to the bottom?

The contacts close about halfway down, and the bump is placed just before that point. The finger feels the force rise and then fall away as the key registers, and can stop there. A linear switch registers at the same depth but gives no signal, so most people press on until the stem hits the bottom.

Reference: J. Ganssle · A Guide to Debouncing

2. Contact bounce and debouncing

When the leaf snaps onto the fixed contact it arrives with some speed, and like a dropped ball it rebounds. For a few milliseconds the circuit makes and breaks several times, each rebound shorter than the last, before the leaf comes to rest. A controller that samples the key a thousand times a second sees every one of these as a press and a release. Debouncing is the rule it uses to ignore them. An eager debouncer reports the key on the first closure and then refuses to look again for a set time; it adds no delay, but a single spike of interference counts as a press. A deferred debouncer reports a change only after the signal has held steady for a set time; it cannot be fooled by a spike, and it is always late by that time. Either way the time must outlast the longest bounce the switch will ever produce.

Worked example. With a first rebound of 0.8 ms and each one 0.55 of the last, the open intervals alone add up to \(0.8/(1-0.55)=1.8\) ms and, with the brief contacts in between, the leaf settles after 5.2 ms and 7 rebounds. Scanned at 1000 Hz, an eager debouncer reports the press 0.4 ms after first contact, but needs at least 3 ms of lock-out or it reports the key twice. A deferred debouncer set to 5 ms reports once, 8.4 ms after first contact. A worn switch with rebounds of 2 ms and a ratio of 0.75 takes 13.7 ms to settle, and the eager debouncer at 5 ms types the letter twice.

Watch out. The bounce pattern is an idealised bouncing-ball sequence that repeats exactly; a real switch bounces differently on every press and on release as well as on closing. Controllers often combine the two methods, and they also add the time to send the report to the computer, which is not included.

Why does an old keyboard start typing a letter twice from one press?

Its contacts have worn or become dirty and now bounce for longer than they did when new. If the bouncing lasts longer than the time the keyboard has been set to ignore after the first closure, a late rebound is read as the key being released and pressed again.

Reference: J. Ganssle · A Guide to Debouncing

3. The key matrix and ghosting

Giving every key its own wire to the controller would need more than a hundred pins. Keyboards instead lay the switches out electrically as a grid: each switch joins one row wire to one column wire. The controller puts a voltage on one column, looks at which rows show it, and moves on to the next column, covering the whole board thousands of times a second. The saving is large, since rows plus columns grows only as the square root of the number of keys. The cost appears when several keys are down. A closed switch conducts both ways, so current put on one column can run along a row through one pressed key, back up another column through a second, and along a different row through a third, making that row light up for a column where no key is pressed. A diode in series with every switch, allowing current one way only, removes the false paths.

Worked example. In the 4 by 5 matrix, 20 keys use 9 pins. Press Q, W and S: Q and W share a row, W and S share a column, so the row of S is joined to the column of Q and the key A at their crossing reads as pressed although it is not. With diodes only Q, W and S are read. Three keys that share no row or column, such as 1, W and D, cause no ghost. A full-size keyboard of 104 keys needs 21 pins as an 11 by 10 matrix, against 104 pins wired singly, and 11 pins by the paired-pin scheme.

Watch out. The matrix is idealised: a real controller sets thresholds on the row voltages and takes time for each column to settle, and keyboards without diodes add rules that block doubtful combinations. The paired-pin scheme, known as charlieplexing, is rare for keys and common for small LED displays.

Why do some keyboards fail to register certain combinations of three keys?

Their switches share row and column wires without diodes. When three pressed keys sit at three corners of a rectangle in that grid, current finds a path through all three to the fourth corner, which then looks pressed. To avoid typing a key that was not pressed, the keyboard discards the doubtful combination.

Reference: J. Ganssle · A Guide to Debouncing
