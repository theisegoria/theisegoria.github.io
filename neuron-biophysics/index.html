Neuron biophysics: the electricity of a nerve cell

A neuron is a battery, a leaky cable and a switch. The battery is made by ion pumps, which keep potassium concentrated inside the cell and sodium outside; each gradient pulls on the membrane voltage towards its own equilibrium, and the membrane settles where the pulls balance, weighted by how freely each ion can cross. The switch is the voltage-gated sodium channel, which opens when the membrane depolarises and so depolarises it further, until it shuts itself and the potassium channels pull the voltage back down: the action potential that Hodgkin and Huxley captured in four differential equations in 1952. The cable is the dendrite and the axon, along which charge spreads and leaks away. These experiments compute resting potentials from ion concentrations, fire action potentials in the Hodgkin–Huxley model, and follow a steady signal as it fades along a dendrite.

Before you begin: Electric circuits (resistors, capacitors), logarithms and exponentials, ordinary differential equations

1. Resting potential: Nernst and Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz

A membrane permeable to one ion only charges up until the electrical force on that ion balances its tendency to diffuse down its concentration gradient. The balancing voltage is the Nernst potential, \(E=\frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{[\text{out}]}{[\text{in}]}\): about \(-89\) mV for potassium in a mammalian neuron and \(+67\) mV for sodium. A real membrane leaks several ions at once, and the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation averages them by permeability. At rest potassium dominates and the cell sits near \(-70\) mV; at the peak of an action potential sodium permeability exceeds potassium’s and the voltage swings towards \(E_{\rm Na}\). Drag the gold bar to change the sodium permeability, or the handle on the right to change external potassium.

Worked example. With \([{\rm K}^+]_o=5\) mM and \([{\rm K}^+]_i=140\) mM at 37 °C, \(E_{\rm K}=26.73\ln(5/140)=-89.1\) mV; sodium at 145 mM outside and 12 mM inside gives \(E_{\rm Na}=+66.6\) mV. With permeabilities \(1:0.04:0.45\) for K, Na and Cl, the GHK equation gives a resting potential of \(-69.8\) mV. Doubling external potassium to 10 mM moves \(E_{\rm K}\) by 18.5 mV to \(-70.5\) mV, but the resting potential by only 8.2 mV, to \(-61.6\) mV. Making sodium 20 times more permeable than potassium, as at the peak of a spike, brings the membrane to \(+51.1\) mV.

Watch out. GHK assumes independent ions crossing a uniform field and ignores the electrogenic sodium–potassium pump, which adds a few millivolts of hyperpolarization. Chloride often sits near equilibrium, and its internal concentration varies between cell types and during development. Only a tiny fraction of the ions need to cross to charge the membrane, so the bulk concentrations hardly change during a spike.

Why does high blood potassium endanger the heart?

Resting potentials are set mostly by \(E_{\rm K}=\frac{RT}{F}\ln([{\rm K}^+]_o/[{\rm K}^+]_i)\), so raising \([{\rm K}^+]_o\) depolarises every cell. Partial depolarization inactivates sodium channels, which slows conduction and can block it altogether, so the heart’s electrical rhythm fails.

Reference: A. L. Hodgkin and A. F. Huxley · A quantitative description of membrane current and its application to conduction and excitation in nerve, Journal of Physiology 117, 500–544 (1952)

2. The action potential: Hodgkin–Huxley

Hodgkin and Huxley treated the squid giant axon membrane as a capacitor in parallel with three conductances. The sodium conductance is \(\bar g_{\rm Na}m^3h\): three fast activation gates \(m\) and one slow inactivation gate \(h\). The potassium conductance is \(\bar g_{\rm K}n^4\) with four slow gates \(n\). Each gate relaxes towards a voltage-dependent value at a voltage-dependent rate. A small depolarization opens a few \(m\) gates, the inward sodium current depolarises the membrane further, and the loop runs away into a spike; \(h\) then closes and \(n\) opens and the voltage falls below rest. Fire one pulse, then a second, and find how close together two spikes can be.

Worked example. With the original parameters (\(\bar g_{\rm Na}=120\), \(\bar g_{\rm K}=36\), \(g_L=0.3\) mS/cm², \(C=1\) µF/cm², \(E_{\rm Na}=50\), \(E_{\rm K}=-77\) mV) and the membrane at rest at \(-65\) mV, where \(m=0.053\), \(h=0.596\), \(n=0.318\), a 1 ms pulse fires a spike only if it is stronger than 6.91 µA/cm² at 6.3 °C. A 10 µA/cm² pulse produces a spike peaking at \(+39\) mV. A second identical pulse 12 ms later fails, because \(h\) has not recovered; 15 ms later it fires again. Warming the axon to 18.5 °C speeds every gate by \(\phi=3.82\) and raises the threshold to 8.9 µA/cm².

Watch out. The model is the squid axon at a temperature near 6 °C; mammalian neurons carry many more channel types and fire faster spikes. Voltages use the modern convention with rest near \(-65\) mV, shifted from the 1952 paper, which measured from rest with the opposite sign. The threshold quoted is for a 1 ms pulse; it depends on the pulse duration.

Why can the membrane not fire a second spike immediately after the first?

During the spike the inactivation gate \(h\) closes and the potassium gate \(n\) opens. Until \(h\) reopens, few sodium channels are available, and while \(n\) stays high an outward potassium current opposes any depolarization. Both gates are slow, so for several milliseconds no stimulus can fire (absolute refractoriness) and then only a larger one can (relative refractoriness).

Reference: A. L. Hodgkin and A. F. Huxley · A quantitative description of membrane current and its application to conduction and excitation in nerve, Journal of Physiology 117, 500–544 (1952)

3. The cable: how far a signal reaches

A dendrite is a thin tube of salty fluid wrapped in a leaky insulator. Current flows along the core through the axial resistance and leaks out through the membrane resistance, and in the steady state the voltage obeys \(\lambda^2V^{\prime\prime}=V\). Its solutions decay over the length constant \(\lambda=\sqrt{R_m d/4R_i}\), which grows only as the square root of the diameter. On a real neuron the two ends of a dendrite are different: the tip is sealed, so no current escapes there and the voltage flattens, while the soma is a large patch of membrane that drains current. Rall described the soma by the ratio \(\rho\) of its conductance to that of a semi-infinite cable of the same dendrite. The same cable physics limits how fast a bare axon conducts, \(v\propto\sqrt d\); wrapping the axon in myelin raises \(R_m\) and cuts the capacitance, so the signal jumps from one node of Ranvier to the next and speed grows in proportion to \(d\).

Worked example. With \(R_m=20\,000\ \Omega\,{\rm cm}^2\), \(R_i=100\ \Omega\,{\rm cm}\) and \(d=2\) µm, \(\lambda=\sqrt{20\,000\times2\times10^{-4}/400}=0.1\) cm \(=1.00\) mm. Injecting at the soma end of a 2 mm dendrite, the sealed tip reaches \(1/\cosh2=0.266\) of the injected voltage, twice the infinite-cable value \(e^{-2}=0.135\), because the sealed end reflects current. Injecting at the tip instead, the soma sees \(1/(\cosh2+\rho\sinh2)\): 0.266 with no soma load, 0.135 for \(\rho=1\), exactly \(e^{-2}\) because a soma with \(\rho=1\) looks to the dendrite like an endless continuation of itself, and 0.068 for \(\rho=3\). Quadrupling the diameter only doubles \(\lambda\). A myelinated fiber of 10 µm conducts at about 60 m/s; a bare fiber of the same size, at roughly 3 m/s.

Watch out. The cable here is passive: real dendrites carry voltage-gated channels that can boost or reshape signals, and they branch, which the one-dimensional model ignores. Transient signals decay faster than the steady state shown, because the membrane capacitance also draws current. The two speed laws are empirical rules of thumb, Hursh’s 6 m/s per µm for myelinated mammalian fibers and a \(\sqrt d\) fit for bare ones. In the 3D neuron the diameters, the spines and the myelin are drawn several times thicker than life.

Why does the length constant grow only as the square root of the diameter?

The axial resistance per unit length falls as the cross-section, \(4R_i/\pi d^2\), but the membrane resistance of a unit length also falls, as \(R_m/\pi d\), because a thicker tube has more surface to leak through. The length constant is the square root of their ratio, \(\sqrt{(R_m/\pi d)/(4R_i/\pi d^2)}=\sqrt{R_md/4R_i}\).

Reference: A. L. Hodgkin and A. F. Huxley · A quantitative description of membrane current and its application to conduction and excitation in nerve, Journal of Physiology 117, 500–544 (1952)
