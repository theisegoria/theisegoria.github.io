Touchscreens: sensing a finger through glass

A phone screen does not feel pressure. Under the glass lies a grid of transparent wires, rows on one layer and columns on another, and where a row crosses a column the two form a tiny capacitor, about two millionths of a millionth of a farad. Your body conducts, so a fingertip resting on the glass above a crossing draws some of the electric field into itself and that capacitor gets a little smaller. The screen measures every crossing a hundred times a second and looks for the dip. Three things make this work: a way to measure a change of a few hundredths of a picofarad, a way to turn a coarse grid of readings into a position far finer than the grid, and a stack of layers thin enough for the finger’s effect to reach the wires. These experiments find a finger from its readings, measure a picofarad by counting, and take the screen apart.

1. Finding the finger

The electrodes of a touchscreen are several millimetres apart, yet the screen reports where you touch to a fraction of a millimetre. The trick is that a fingertip is wider than the spacing. It lowers the capacitance of the crossing directly beneath it most, and of the crossings around it by smaller amounts that fall off smoothly with distance. If the finger sits exactly over one crossing, its neighbours on either side read the same; move it slightly to the right and the right-hand neighbour reads more, the left-hand one less. The controller takes the readings around the strongest crossing and computes their weighted average position, the centroid, which slides smoothly as the finger does. The limit is noise: every reading jitters a little, and the jitter in the readings becomes jitter in the position.

Worked example. With electrodes 5 mm apart under 0.7 mm of glass, a contact of 4 mm radius lowers the 2 pF of the crossing beneath it by 0.37 pF, 19 per cent, with a spread of 3.3 mm. Wherever the finger is placed away from the edge, the centroid of the nine readings round the peak is within 0.5 mm of the true centre; taking only the strongest crossing would be wrong by up to 3.5 mm. A contact of 2 mm radius gives 0.28 pF, so the same noise disturbs its position more.

Watch out. The bell-shaped profile and its width are a representative model, not a field solution, and the noise is a fixed pattern so that the picture holds still. Real controllers also subtract a slowly updated baseline, filter over time, reject palms and track several fingers at once, and the simple centroid is biased near the edges of the panel, where part of the profile falls off the grid.

How can a screen with electrodes 5 mm apart place a touch to within half a millimetre?

A fingertip affects several neighbouring crossings at once, each by an amount that depends on its distance from the centre of the contact. The balance between those readings shifts continuously as the finger moves, and their weighted average recovers the centre much more finely than the grid spacing.

Reference: G. Walker · A review of technologies for sensing contact location on the surface of a display (Journal of the Society for Information Display, 2012)

2. Measuring a picofarad by counting

The capacitance at one crossing is about 2 pF, and a finger changes it by a few tenths of that. No ordinary circuit can read so small a quantity directly, so the controller converts it to a count. It charges the small capacitor to the supply voltage, then connects it to a much larger one and lets the charge share out; the large capacitor’s voltage rises by a tiny step. It repeats this thousands of times, each step a little smaller than the last as the large capacitor fills, and counts the transfers needed to reach a set threshold. A smaller capacitance moves less charge each time and needs more transfers. Because the result depends only on the ratio of the two capacitors, it is steady against changes in supply voltage and temperature, and the resolution can be made as fine as wished by using a larger capacitor and waiting longer.

Worked example. With a 1.5 nF capacitor and a threshold at half the supply, an untouched 2 pF crossing takes 521 transfers (close to \(750\times\ln2=520\)) and a touched one at 1.63 pF takes 639: a difference of 118 counts, so one count is 3.8 fF. At 2 µs per transfer a reading takes 1.3 ms, and reading the twelve rows of the panel one after another, with all eight columns measured at once, takes 15 ms: 65 scans a second. A 10 nF capacitor would give 870 counts of difference but only ten scans a second.

Watch out. This is the charge-transfer method used by early touch controllers and still used for touch buttons. Phone controllers drive a row with a burst of pulses and integrate the charge arriving on every column with an amplifier, and they change the pulse frequency to avoid interference, but they face the same trade between measuring time and resolution. Stray capacitance and leakage are ignored.

Why does a touch controller measure capacitance by counting charge transfers?

The capacitance is too small to read directly, but each transfer moves an amount of charge proportional to it. Counting the transfers needed to fill a much larger capacitor turns the tiny capacitance into a large number, and the answer depends only on a ratio of capacitors, so it does not drift with supply voltage.

Reference: G. Walker · A review of technologies for sensing contact location on the surface of a display (Journal of the Society for Information Display, 2012)

3. The stack under the glass

A touchscreen is a stack. At the bottom is the display. Above it are two layers of indium tin oxide, a ceramic that conducts electricity and is nearly transparent when a few tens of nanometres thick. Each layer is etched into chains of diamonds, rows in one and columns in the other, arranged so the diamonds of one fill the gaps of the other and the chains cross only at narrow bridges. Field lines run from each row diamond over the gap to the neighbouring column diamonds, bulging up into the glass, and that fringe is what a finger disturbs. On top is the cover glass. Its thickness matters twice over: the further the finger is from the electrodes, the less of the fringing field it intercepts, and the more evenly its influence is spread over neighbouring crossings.

Worked example. For a 4 mm contact, 0.4 mm glass gives a fall of 0.40 pF. At 0.7 mm it is 0.37 pF, and at 1.2 mm, 0.31 pF. Add a 2 mm glove to 0.7 mm glass and the finger is 2.7 mm from the electrodes: the fall is 0.15 pF, spread over 4.5 mm instead of 3.3 mm, only seven times a typical noise level of 0.02 pF.

Watch out. The stack is shown pulled apart and with the glass thickness exaggerated; the real layers are bonded with clear adhesive and the electrode films are a tenth of a millimetre thick. Many current phones build the electrodes into the display itself. The dependence on thickness is a representative formula, and the picture on the display is only decoration.

Why does a touchscreen respond poorly through a thick glove?

The glove holds the finger further from the electrodes, where the fringing field between rows and columns is weak, so the finger takes less of it and the capacitance changes less. The change is also spread over more crossings. Both make the signal harder to tell from noise.

Reference: G. Walker · A review of technologies for sensing contact location on the surface of a display (Journal of the Society for Information Display, 2012)
