Pressure cookers: hotter water, faster food

Water in an open pot cannot get hotter than its boiling point, however fierce the flame: extra heat only makes more steam. A pressure cooker gets round this by sealing the pot. The steam cannot leave, the pressure inside rises, and at a higher pressure water has to be hotter before it will boil. At twice the pressure of the air the water sits near 120 °C in place of 100, and because the chemistry of cooking roughly doubles in speed for every ten degrees, food is done in about a quarter of the time. The pressure is set by nothing more elaborate than a small weight resting on a narrow pipe. These experiments follow the boiling curve up and down, open the cooker and weigh its valve, and time a dish from cold.

1. Boiling point against pressure

Molecules leave the surface of water all the time, and in a closed space they build up a pressure of vapour that depends only on the temperature. This vapour pressure climbs steeply: it is 3 kPa at room temperature, 47 kPa at 80 °C and 101 kPa at 100 °C. Water boils when bubbles of vapour can grow inside it, and they can only do that when the vapour pressure matches the pressure pressing on the water. So the boiling point is not a property of water alone. It is the temperature at which the vapour pressure curve meets whatever pressure the surroundings supply. Lower the pressure by climbing a mountain and water boils cooler; raise it by sealing the pot and water boils hotter.

Worked example. A cooker holding 100 kPa above the air has 201 kPa inside, and the curve gives a boiling point of 120.5 °C. The speed of cooking is \(2^{20.5/10}=4.2\) times that at 100 °C, so an hour of simmering becomes 14 minutes. At 3000 m the air is at 70 kPa and an open pot boils at 90 °C, where food cooks at half speed. On the summit of Everest, at 31 kPa, water boils at 70 °C.

Watch out. The vapour pressure comes from the Antoine equation with two sets of constants, good to about half a per cent between 1 and 200 °C. The doubling of speed for every 10 degrees is a rule of thumb: real foods range from about 1.5 to 3, and some changes, such as browning, depend on dryness more than on temperature.

Why does turning up the flame under an open pot of boiling water not cook the food faster?

The water is already at the temperature where its vapour pressure equals the pressure of the air, so it cannot get hotter. The extra heat goes into turning water to steam, and the food sits in water at the same temperature as before.

2. The cooker and its weighted valve

The simplest regulator is a weight sitting on the open end of a narrow pipe. Steam inside pushes up on the weight with a force equal to the pressure difference times the area of the bore, and gravity pulls down with the weight’s mass times \(g\). While the steam force is the smaller, the pipe stays shut and the pressure climbs. Once it is the larger, the weight lifts, steam escapes and the pressure falls back, so the weight hovers and rocks at exactly the pressure where the two balance. Nothing about that pressure depends on the heat: it is fixed by a mass and a diameter. The same pressure acts on the whole lid, whose area is thousands of times that of the pipe, so the lid must lock to the pot, and a second, larger outlet stands by in case the pipe blocks.

Worked example. A 74 g weight on a 3 mm bore: the bore has an area of 7.07 mm², the weight is 0.726 N, and the pressure held is \(0.726/7.07\times10^{-6}=103\) kPa, where water boils at 121 °C. On a 22 cm lid the same pressure pushes with 3.9 kN, the weight of 400 kg. A 1500 W ring left on full boils off \(1500/2.2\times10^{6}=0.68\) g of water a second, 41 g a minute, so half a litre lasts 12 minutes.

Watch out. The cooker is a generic 22 cm stovetop design drawn to scale, with the vent pipe and weight enlarged enough to be seen. Modern cookers mostly use a spring-loaded valve, which obeys the same balance with a spring force in place of the weight. All the heat is taken to reach the water, and the latent heat is taken as a constant 2.2 MJ/kg.

A cooker’s weight is replaced by one twice as heavy. What happens to the pressure, and to the temperature?

The pressure above the air outside doubles, because the same bore must now support twice the force. The temperature rises by much less than double: from about 121 °C at 103 kPa to about 134 °C at 205 kPa, since the boiling curve steepens as it climbs.

3. From cold to done

A pressure cooker does not save time at every stage. First the water and the pot must be heated, and that takes as long as it would in any pot: the heat needed is the heat capacity times the rise in temperature, and it arrives at the power of the ring. The sealed pot then keeps climbing past 100 °C to its regulated temperature, which costs a little more time. Only then does the gain begin, with every minute at 120 °C worth about four at 100. Adding up cooking progress along the whole temperature curve gives the time at which the dish is done. Because the heating stage is untouched, the total falls quickly with the first 50 kPa and then levels off, which is why domestic cookers stop near 100 kPa.

Worked example. One litre of water in a 1.5 kg aluminium pot has a heat capacity of \(4186+1350=5536\) J/K. On 1500 W it reaches 100 °C from 20 °C in \(5536\times80/1500=295\) s, 4.9 minutes, and 120.5 °C in 6.2 minutes. A dish needing 60 minutes at a simmer is done 64 minutes after lighting the ring in the open pot and 20 minutes after in the sealed one. At 150 kPa it would take 14 minutes, of which 6.6 are spent heating up.

Watch out. The pot is a 1.5 kg aluminium one starting at 20 °C, all the heat is taken to reach it, and losses to the room are ignored. The heat needed to warm the food itself and the time for the pressure to fall afterwards are left out; both lengthen a real recipe by several minutes.

Why does a cooker that held 300 kPa not cook much faster than one that holds 100 kPa?

At 100 kPa the cooking stage is already down to a few minutes, and most of what remains is the time to heat the water and pot, which higher pressure lengthens slightly. There is little left to save, while the forces on the lid would be three times as large.
