Linear algebra: the geometry of transformations

Coordinates, area, eigenvectors, projection, and singular values.

Before you begin: Vectors and elementary algebra

Predict, manipulate, then test yourself: every experiment ends with practice questions that are generated fresh at three levels. Graphs illustrate the mathematics; they do not replace a proof.

1. Basis and coordinates

Drag the two circular basis handles or use the sliders. The highlighted physical vector stays at (1,1), while its coordinates in the new basis change. The columns of a matrix are the images of the standard basis.

Worked example. For B=diag(2,1), the coordinates of (1,1) are (1/2,1).

Watch out. Dependent columns do not form a basis; an inverse does not exist.

What happens when the two columns coincide?

The plane collapses to a line. Coordinates are no longer unique when a solution exists.

Reference: MIT OpenCourseWare · Linear Algebra

2. Determinant, volume and rank

The columns of A are the images of the basis vectors, so A sends the unit cube to the parallelepiped spanned by Ae₁, Ae₂ and Ae₃, and its signed volume is det A. Here the first two columns lie in the floor, so the floor face is the parallelogram of the 2 × 2 block, with area |ad|, and the volume is that base times the height k: det A = adk. Sliding the top vector sideways (p and q) is a shear and leaves the volume alone. A negative determinant reverses orientation, and a zero factor flattens the solid, so the rank falls. Press Play to watch the cube turn into the image.

Worked example. For diag(−2, 1, 1) the volume doubles and the orientation reverses; for a = d = k = 1 with c = 0.7, p = 0.4, q = 0.3 the cube is sheared but its volume is still 1.

Watch out. A determinant of one preserves area, but need not preserve lengths or angles.

Can a shear change shape without changing area?

Yes. The matrix with rows (1,s) and (0,1) has determinant one for every s.

Reference: MIT OpenCourseWare · Linear Algebra

3. Eigenvectors and iteration

Rotate the eigenbasis and change its two eigenvalues. Repeated application scales each eigenvector component separately. Dashed lines mark the invariant directions.

Worked example. With eigenvalues 1.2 and 0.5, one component grows while the other decays.

Watch out. This laboratory uses real symmetric matrices. A real rotation need not have a real eigenvector.

If both eigenvalues have magnitude below one, where does every orbit go?

To the origin, because both scalar powers tend to zero.

Reference: MIT OpenCourseWare · Linear Algebra

4. Least squares as projection

Fit a line y = x₀ + x₁t to three data points at t = 0, 1, 2. No line passes through all three in general, so Ax = b has no solution: the data vector b lies off the plane of all vectors Ax, the column space of A. Least squares picks the point p = Ax̂ of that plane closest to b, which is the orthogonal projection, and the residual e = b − p is perpendicular to the plane. Its three components are the vertical misses in the data plot. Drag the data points, or drag b in the 3D view.

Worked example. For b = (0.6, 2.4, 1.8): Aᵀb = (4.8, 6), and AᵀA = [[3, 3], [3, 5]] gives x̂ = (1, 0.6). So p = (1, 1.6, 2.2) and e = (−0.4, 0.8, −0.4), which is perpendicular to both columns (1, 1, 1) and (0, 1, 2).

Watch out. Least squares minimises the sum of squared vertical misses. It is not the line closest to the points in perpendicular distance, and a single outlier pulls it strongly.

When is the residual zero?

When b already lies in the column space, that is, when the three points are collinear: b₁ − 2b₂ + b₃ = 0, since (1, −2, 1) is perpendicular to both columns.

Reference: MIT OpenCourseWare · Linear Algebra

5. Singular values and rank

Rotate the input, stretch along two perpendicular axes, then rotate the output. The image of the unit circle is an ellipse. Removing the smaller stretch gives a rank-one approximation.

Worked example. For diag(2,0.5), the best rank-one approximation is diag(2,0), with operator-norm error 0.5.

Watch out. Singular values are nonnegative; they are not generally the eigenvalues of A.

What happens when the second singular value is zero?

The ellipse collapses to a segment and the rank is one.

Reference: MIT OpenCourseWare · Linear Algebra
