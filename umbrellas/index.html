Umbrellas: a triangle that opens

An umbrella has to be a rigid dome a metre across and, a moment later, a stick. It manages both with one idea repeated eight times round a shaft: a rib hinged at the top, propped by a strut whose other end slides. Rib, strut and shaft form a triangle with two sides of fixed length and one that the hand changes, and as that side shortens the rib swings out. The same triangle, with different proportions, opens folding chairs, deck loungers, car bonnets and the legs of an ironing board. In an umbrella it must also survive the wind, which loads the thin ribs as beams. These experiments open a frame, vary the proportions of the triangle, and bend the ribs in a gale.

1. The frame

Every rib is hinged in a slotted ring at the top of the shaft, and a thin wire through all the slots is its pivot. Part-way along each rib a stretcher is hinged, and the other ends of all the stretchers are gathered in a second slotted ring, the runner, which slides freely on the shaft. When the umbrella is closed the runner is at the bottom of its travel and each rib and its stretcher lie flat against the shaft. Pushing the runner up shortens one side of every triangle at once, and since the other two sides cannot change, the angle at the top must open. The cloth is cut so that it becomes tight when the ribs are a little short of square to the shaft, and at that point a sprung catch pops out of the shaft under the runner and holds it. The tension in the cloth then pulls every rib tip inward against its stretcher, and the whole frame is locked in a state of balanced stress.

Worked example. With the stretcher joined 250 mm along the rib and 270 mm long, the closed runner is \(250+270=520\) mm below the top. Raising it to 400 mm gives \(\cos\phi=(250^{2}+400^{2}-270^{2})/(2\times250\times400)=0.748\), so the ribs stand at 42°. At 166 mm they reach 78°, and ribs 550 mm long span \(2\times550\times\sin78^\circ=1076\) mm. The runner has travelled 354 mm.

Watch out. The frame is a generic eight-rib stick umbrella drawn to scale. The ribs are given a fixed gentle curve and the cloth is stretched straight between neighbouring ribs with a slight sag; real cloth is cut in shaped gores and pulls the ribs into their curve. The catch, the tip cups and the stitching that ties the cloth to each rib are not drawn.

Why do all the ribs open by exactly the same angle, however unevenly the runner is pushed?

The runner is one rigid ring on a straight shaft, so every stretcher has its lower end at the same distance below the top. All eight triangles have the same three sides, and three sides fix a triangle completely.

Reference: J. J. Uicker, G. R. Pennock and J. E. Shigley · Theory of Machines and Mechanisms, fifth edition (Oxford University Press, 2016)

2. One triangle, many umbrellas

Where the stretcher joins the rib, and how long it is, decide how the umbrella behaves. A stretcher joined near the top needs only a short travel of the runner but moves the tips a long way for each millimetre, so it takes a hard push to tighten the cloth. Joined further out, it gives the hand more leverage and props the rib nearer its middle, at the cost of a longer runner travel and a longer frame when closed. The link between the two is exact. Since no energy is stored in rigid bars, the work done on the runner equals the work done at the tips, so the force needed at the runner is the pull at the tips multiplied by the ratio of tip travel to runner travel. That ratio is large when the umbrella is nearly closed and falls as it opens, which suits the task: the cloth resists only at the end. A stretcher shorter than the distance to its joint cannot open the rib past the point where it stands square to the shaft.

Worked example. For the frame above, with ribs 550 mm long, the tips move 4.0 mm for each millimetre of runner when the runner is at 500 mm, 1.8 mm at 400 mm, and 1.5 mm when fully open at 166 mm. Holding a pull of 1 N at each of eight tips there takes \(8\times1.5=12\) N at the runner. A stretcher 200 mm long joined at 250 mm could open the ribs no further than \(\arcsin(200/250)=53^\circ\).

Watch out. The bars are rigid and the hinges frictionless, and the force relation ignores the weight of the frame and the spring that some umbrellas use to help the runner up. The rib length is fixed at 550 mm and the open position is taken as 78° wherever the frame can reach it.

An automatic umbrella opens by itself at the press of a button. Where does the energy come from, and when was it put in?

From a coil spring round the shaft that pushes the runner up. The user compressed it when closing the umbrella, pulling the runner down against the spring until a catch held it. Closing takes more effort than with a plain umbrella, and opening takes none.

Reference: J. J. Uicker, G. R. Pennock and J. E. Shigley · Theory of Machines and Mechanisms, fifth edition (Oxford University Press, 2016)

3. Wind

Held against the wind, an open canopy is a bluff cup, and the air piles up inside it. The force is the pressure of the moving air, half its density times the square of its speed, times the area of the canopy and a drag coefficient somewhat above one. Doubling the wind speed quadruples the force. That force reaches the hand through the ribs. Each rib is supported at its pivot and at the stretcher, and the part beyond the stretcher is a cantilever carrying its share of the wind along its length, so the greatest bending is at the stretcher joint. Two things can then happen. If the ribs are flexible, their tips are bent back until the canopy passes through flat and snaps inside out, after which it can be pushed back. If they are stiff, they hold their shape until the stress at the joint exceeds what the material can bear, and then they kink there and stay kinked.

Worked example. A canopy with 550 mm ribs has a projected area of 0.92 m². In a 15 m/s wind the force is \(\tfrac12\times1.2\times15^{2}\times1.4\times0.92=174\) N, the weight of 18 kg. Eight ribs each carry 39.6 N/m, and the 300 mm beyond the stretcher bends with \(39.6\times0.3^{2}/2=1.78\) N m. A steel rib is stressed to 446 MPa, 37 per cent of its strength, and its tip moves 50 mm. A glass-fibre rib moves 136 mm: it turns inside out at 13 m/s and does not break until 22 m/s. Steel turns inside out at 22 m/s and breaks at 25 m/s.

Watch out. The wind is steady and square to the canopy, with one drag coefficient and the load spread evenly along every rib; real wind gusts, strikes at an angle and loads the windward ribs most. The three rib sections are representative of a fluted steel rib, a 3.5 mm glass-fibre rod and a carbon-fibre rod, and the canopy is said to turn inside out when the tips have moved by a fixed fraction of the overhang.

Why do umbrellas made for storms often have more ribs, such as sixteen in place of eight?

The wind force on the canopy is the same, but it is shared among twice as many ribs, so each carries half the load and bends half as far, with half the stress at its stretcher joint. The canopy also keeps a rounder shape between ribs.

Reference: J. J. Uicker, G. R. Pennock and J. E. Shigley · Theory of Machines and Mechanisms, fifth edition (Oxford University Press, 2016)
