Car suspension: springs, dampers and the road

A car’s suspension has two jobs that pull in opposite directions. It must let the wheels follow every rise and dip of the road so that the tyres keep pressing on it, and it must stop those rises and dips reaching the people inside. Springs do the separating: a body on soft springs has a slow rhythm of its own and ignores anything faster. But a spring alone gives back all the energy it takes in, so a sprung body would bounce for ever. The damper is there to throw that energy away as heat. How stiff the damper should be has no single answer, because what calms the body unsettles the wheel. These experiments sweep the road from slow swells to sharp ripples, drive one corner of a car over a bump, and look at the oil and valves inside the damper.

1. Comfort against grip

The simplest useful picture of a suspension is one corner of the car: a share of the body resting on a spring and a damper, which rest on the wheel, which rests on the tyre, itself a short stiff spring. This system has two natural rhythms. The body bounces on the suspension spring about once a second, and the much lighter wheel bounces between the tyre and the suspension about twelve times a second. A road that rises and falls slowly lifts everything together. One that moves near the body’s rhythm makes the body swing further than the road. One that moves much faster is absorbed by the wheel while the body stays level, which is the whole purpose of the spring. The damper limits both resonances, but it also links body and wheel, so a stiff damper passes more of the fast movements through. Comfort is measured by how much the body moves, grip by how much the squash of the tyre varies, and the two respond differently to the same damper.

Worked example. A body share of 350 kg on a spring of 25 kN/m bounces at \(\sqrt{25000/350}/2\pi=1.35\) Hz, and sags 137 mm under its own weight. A 40 kg wheel between that spring and a tyre of 200 kN/m hops at \(\sqrt{225000/40}/2\pi=11.9\) Hz. With a damping ratio of 0.3 the damper is 1775 N s/m; the body then moves 2.2 times as far as the road at 1.35 Hz and only a tenth as far at 11.9 Hz, where the squash of the tyre varies by 1.8 times the road’s movement.

Watch out. This is the quarter-car model: one wheel, moving only up and down, with a spring and damper that are perfectly linear and a tyre that never leaves the road. Pitch and roll of the whole body, the geometry of the arms, the rubber bushes and the friction in the damper are all left out.

Why are sports cars sprung more stiffly, when stiffer springs let more of the road through?

Stiff springs keep the body level in corners and under braking, so the tyres stay square to the road, and they raise the body’s rhythm so that it responds faster to the driver. The price is paid in comfort between the two resonances, which matters less on a smooth track.

Reference: T. D. Gillespie · Fundamentals of Vehicle Dynamics (SAE International, 1992), chapter 5 on ride

2. Over a bump

Follow one event in time. The tyre meets a bump and is pushed up in a few hundredths of a second. The wheel, being light, goes with it. The spring between wheel and body is squeezed and begins to push the body upward, but the body is heavy and has hardly moved by the time the bump has passed, so the wheel comes down again and most of the push is withdrawn. What the body receives is a short shove, after which it rises and falls at its own slow rhythm. With no damper it would go on doing so. With a damper, each swing is smaller than the last by a fixed fraction, and the fraction is set by the damping ratio: at 0.3 the second swing is about a third of the first, and at 1 the body returns without overshooting at all. Too much damping, though, makes the first shove harder, because the damper resists the wheel’s rise as stiffly as it resists the body’s bounce.

Worked example. A bump 80 mm high and 0.5 m long crossed at 20 km/h lasts \(0.5/5.56=90\) ms. With a damping ratio of 0.3 the body rises 32 mm, 40 per cent of the bump, feels a peak of 1.9 \(g\), and has settled within 1.5 s. At 0.1 it rises 33 mm but takes 2.2 s to settle. At 0.8 it rises 47 mm with a peak of 3.0 \(g\), and settles in 0.6 s.

Watch out. The corner is a generic strut suspension drawn to scale and shown in slow motion, with 350 kg of body on a 25 kN/m spring. The tyre is a linear spring fixed to the road, so where the model says it unloads by more than its 19 mm of resting squash, a real wheel would leave the ground. The strut moves straight up and down, and its lean and the swing of the lower arm are ignored in the forces.

A car with worn-out dampers is pushed down on one corner and released. What does it do, and what should a healthy one do?

With little damping the corner rises past its resting height, sinks again and repeats several times. A healthy damper, with a ratio near 0.3, lets it rise, overshoot slightly once, and stop.

Reference: T. D. Gillespie · Fundamentals of Vehicle Dynamics (SAE International, 1992), chapter 5 on ride

3. Inside the damper

A damper is a piston in a tube of oil. For the piston to move, oil must pass from one side of it to the other through small holes, and forcing a liquid through a small hole takes a pressure that rises as the square of the flow. A damper with nothing but a fixed hole would therefore be far too soft at low speeds, where it must control the slow bounce of the body, and far too hard at high speeds, where a sharp bump would be passed straight to the body. So the holes are covered by thin sprung discs. At low speed the oil uses a small bleed hole and the force rises steeply; at a set pressure the discs lift, uncovering a larger passage, and the force rises only gently after that. Separate discs for the two directions make the damper firmer when it is pulled out than when it is pushed in. Whatever the curve, the work done against the damper becomes heat in the oil.

Worked example. A piston 36 mm across has an area of 10.2 cm². At 0.1 m/s through a 3 mm hole the force is 183 N; by the square law it would be 1650 N at 0.3 m/s, but the valve lifts at 1200 N, reached at 0.26 m/s, and the force at 0.3 m/s is 1226 N pushing in and 2450 N pulling out. At 1 m/s it is 1650 N, where the fixed hole alone would give 18 kN. At 0.3 m/s the damper makes 370 W of heat.

Watch out. The valve is modelled as a sharp change from a square law to a straight line; real disc stacks open progressively and are tuned shim by shim. The pull-out force is simply twice the push-in force. The gas charge that stops the oil foaming, and the thinning of the oil as it warms, are not included, and the temperature rise ignores cooling by the air.

Why is a damper made firmer when the wheel drops than when it rises?

When the wheel rises over a bump, a soft damper lets it move out of the way without kicking the body upward. When the spring then pushes the wheel back down, a firm damper stops the stored energy throwing the body up and controls the bounce. Most of the damping is done on the way out.

Reference: T. D. Gillespie · Fundamentals of Vehicle Dynamics (SAE International, 1992), chapter 5 on ride
