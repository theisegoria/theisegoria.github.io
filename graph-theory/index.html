Graph theory: routes, trees, and networks

Shortest paths, spanning trees, and centrality.

Before you begin: Sets and elementary algorithms

1. Shortest paths2. Minimum spanning trees3. Centrality and influence

Predict, manipulate, then check your reasoning against the example and question. Graphs illustrate the mathematics; they do not replace a proof.

1. Shortest paths

Adjust edge weights and watch Dijkstra’s frontier expand from the source.

Worked example. With nonnegative weights, the settled label is final.

Watch out. Dijkstra fails with negative edge weights.

Why does the frontier stay optimal?

Every alternative path must pass through a node with a no-smaller settled distance.

Reference: Diestel · Graph Theory

2. Minimum spanning trees

Compare a greedy tree with every network edge and reveal the cut property.

Worked example. A tree on six vertices has five edges.

Watch out. A spanning tree connects all vertices without cycles; it need not preserve shortest routes.

Why does the lightest crossing edge belong to some MST?

Exchange it with a heavier edge on the corresponding tree path.

Reference: Diestel · Graph Theory

3. Centrality and influence

Compare degree, closeness, and betweenness on a small network.

Worked example. A bridge node can have modest degree but high betweenness.

Watch out. Centrality is a model choice, not a universal ranking.

Which measure rewards many direct neighbours?

Degree centrality counts immediate neighbours.

Reference: Diestel · Graph Theory
