Sewing machines: the lockstitch and the hook

Hand sewing passes the whole needle through the cloth and pulls the thread after it, and no machine can do that, because a machine cannot let go of its needle. The sewing machine solved the problem by changing the stitch. Its needle has the eye at the point and never passes through; it only dips a loop of thread below the cloth and withdraws. Underneath, a second thread is passed through that loop before it is pulled tight, so that the two threads lock together inside the cloth. Passing a thread through a loop sounds simple and is the whole difficulty: it means taking a loop that is fixed at both ends right round a spool of thread. These experiments run the needle and hook through a stitch, balance the two threads, and speed the machine up.

1. One stitch

Follow one turn of the main shaft. The needle descends, pierces the cloth and carries the upper thread, which runs down a groove in its front, to the bottom of its stroke. As it starts to rise, the thread on the grooved side slides back up with it, but the thread on the other side is gripped by the cloth and cannot, so it buckles outward into a small loop just above the eye. At that instant the point of a hook, turning in a circle beside the needle, enters the loop. The hook carries the loop outward and round a case that holds the bobbin of lower thread; the case sits loose in the hook, so the loop can slide over it completely. Having gone all the way round, the loop drops off the hook. A lever above the needle, which has been feeding out slack to let the loop grow, now rises and pulls the loop up into the cloth, dragging the lower thread with it. Then the cloth is moved on by one stitch.

Worked example. The needle point moves between 14 mm above the cloth and 10 mm below it. It is at the bottom at 180° and has risen 1.1 mm by 205°, when the hook point arrives. The hook turns twice for each stitch, so it has carried the loop through 340° by the time the shaft reaches 375°, that is 15° into the next turn. The take-up lever is lowest at 250° and highest at 70°, a movement of 18 mm, and the cloth is fed between 290° and 70°, while the needle is clear of it.

Watch out. The parts are drawn to scale but simplified: a plain ring for the hook, no presser-foot spring, feed dogs or tension springs, and the needle moving as a pure cosine. The thread is drawn as straight runs and a smooth loop, and the stitch is made longer than usual so that it can be seen. Real hooks are shaped so that the loop is spread wide enough to clear the bobbin case and is released at exactly the right moment.

Why must the needle have already begun to rise when the hook arrives?

While the needle is still going down the thread lies tight along it and there is nothing for the hook to enter. The loop is thrown only when the needle reverses and the thread, held by the cloth, has to buckle. Too early and the hook misses; too late and the loop has collapsed. This timing is what a mechanic adjusts.

Reference: E. Howe · Improvement in sewing-machines, US Patent 4,750 (1846)

2. Where the threads lock

In a finished lockstitch the upper thread runs along the top of the cloth and the lower thread along the bottom, and at every needle hole they cross. Where in the thickness of the cloth they cross is decided by a tug of war. The upper thread is pinched between two sprung discs on its way to the needle; the lower thread is pinched under a leaf spring on the bobbin case. When the take-up lever pulls the stitch tight, each thread resists with its own tension, and the crossing is dragged toward whichever pulls harder. With the tensions equal it settles in the middle of the cloth, where it cannot be seen and cannot be snagged, and each face of the seam shows a neat line of its own thread. The amount of thread a seam uses follows from the same picture: each stitch lays one stitch length along each surface and then goes down to the crossing and back.

Worked example. With stitches 2.5 mm long in cloth 1 mm thick and the crossing in the middle, each stitch uses \(2.5+2\times1\times0.5=3.5\) mm of each thread. A metre of seam has 400 stitches and takes \(7.0/2.5=2.8\) m of thread, 1.4 m from the spool and 1.4 m from the bobbin. Shorten the stitch to 1.5 mm and a metre of seam takes 3.3 m.

Watch out. The position of the crossing is a simple function of the ratio of the two tensions, chosen to sit in the middle when they are equal and to reach a surface when one is twice the other. The threads are drawn as straight lines and the cloth as incompressible; in practice the cloth is squeezed at each stitch and stretchy fabrics need the balance set by trial.

A seam shows tiny loops of the upper thread on the underside. Which tension should be changed, and which way?

The upper thread is being pulled through to the bottom, so the lower thread is winning. Tighten the upper tension, which is the one with a dial, until the crossing is drawn back into the middle of the cloth.

Reference: E. Howe · Improvement in sewing-machines, US Patent 4,750 (1846)

3. Speed

The stitching rate is the speed of the main shaft: one turn, one stitch. Everything on the machine is driven from that shaft by cranks, cams and gears, so that the needle, hook, take-up and feed can never fall out of step. The needle bar is the end of a slider-crank. Its speed is greatest near mid-stroke, where the crank is at right angles to it, and its acceleration is greatest at the two ends, where it must be stopped and thrown back. That acceleration is the crank radius times the square of the shaft speed, increased a little at one end and reduced at the other by the shortness of the connecting rod. Doubling the stitching rate doubles the needle’s speed and quadruples the forces on its drive. Meanwhile the thread is working harder than it looks: each stitch draws tens of millimetres through the needle’s eye and takes nearly all of it back.

Worked example. At 800 stitches a minute the shaft turns at 83.8 rad/s. With a stroke of 30 mm, so a crank of 15 mm, and a 45 mm rod, the needle reaches 1.33 m/s and its greatest acceleration is \(0.015\times83.8^{2}\times1.33=140\) m/s², 14 \(g\). At 5000 stitches a minute the speed is 8.3 m/s and the acceleration 560 \(g\). With 2.5 mm stitches the seam advances at 2 m/min and 12.5 m/min. If each stitch draws 60 mm of thread through the eye and back, and lays down 3.5 mm, a given piece passes through the eye 34 times.

Watch out. The needle bar is an ideal slider-crank with a 45 mm rod, and the loop is taken as a fixed 60 mm of thread in 1 mm cloth. Heating of the needle by friction with the cloth, which limits the speed of industrial machines on synthetic fabrics, and the stretch of the thread are not modelled.

Why does sewing thread need to be smoother and stronger than its job of holding cloth together would suggest?

Before it becomes part of the seam, each length of thread is dragged back and forth through the needle’s eye and round the hook dozens of times at several metres a second. It has to survive that abrasion and the jerk of every stitch without fraying or breaking.
