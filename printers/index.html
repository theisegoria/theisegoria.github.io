Printers: drops of ink and charged powder

There are two ways to put a page of text on paper in a few seconds, and they have almost nothing in common. An inkjet printer throws liquid: a head carrying thousands of nozzles sweeps across the page, and each nozzle fires drops a few thousandths of a millimetre across, tens of thousands of times a second, at spots chosen one by one. A laser printer never touches the paper with a liquid at all. It paints the page in static electricity on a turning drum with a scanning beam of light, lets a fine plastic powder cling to the painted parts, rolls the powder onto the paper and melts it in. One is limited by how small and how accurately a drop can be thrown, the other by how sharply light can be focused and how fast a mirror can spin. These experiments look at the drops, the charge on the drum, and the whole laser engine.

1. Inkjet: drops and dots

An inkjet nozzle is a tiny chamber with a hole in it. In a thermal head a heater behind the ink boils a film of it in a few millionths of a second, and the bubble pushes a drop out of the hole; in a piezoelectric head a crystal flexes the chamber wall to the same effect. The drop is a sphere of a few picolitres that flies about a millimetre to the paper, where it flattens and soaks in to make a dot about twice its own width. To print a solid colour the dots must overlap enough to leave no paper showing, which fixes the smallest useful drop for each resolution. And because the head is moving while it fires, every drop keeps that sideways speed in flight and lands beyond the point it was fired from, in opposite directions on the outward and return passes.

Worked example. A 4 pL drop is a sphere 19.7 µm across and, spreading two-fold, a dot of 39 µm. At 600 dpi the grid spacing is 42.3 µm and full cover needs 59.9 µm, so these dots cover only 68 per cent of the paper; at 1200 dpi they cover all of it. With the head moving at 0.8 m/s, a 1.2 mm gap and drops at 8 m/s, the flight takes 150 µs and each drop drifts 120 µm, so uncorrected the two passes would disagree by 240 µm, nearly six dots. An A4 page at 5 per cent cover and 600 dpi uses about 0.007 mL of ink.

Watch out. The drops are taken as perfect spheres that land as round dots on a square grid. Real drops trail a thin tail that can break into smaller satellite drops, the spread depends strongly on the paper, and printers overlap several passes and vary the pattern to hide the grid and any failed nozzle. Air drag on the drop is ignored.

Why does an inkjet printer ask you to print an alignment page?

The head fires while moving, so each drop lands a little beyond the point it was fired from, by an amount that depends on the head’s speed, the gap to the paper and the drop’s speed. On the return pass the shift is in the opposite direction. The alignment page measures the mismatch so the printer can fire early by the right amount.

Reference: D. M. Pai and B. E. Springett · Physics of electrophotography (Reviews of Modern Physics, 1993)

2. Laser: writing with light on a charged drum

The drum of a laser printer is coated with a material that is an insulator in the dark and a conductor in the light. A roller first charges its surface evenly to several hundred volts. A laser beam, switched on and off as it sweeps across, then lets the charge leak away wherever it lands, leaving an invisible picture in voltage. Next the drum passes a roller carrying toner, a powder of plastic particles charged with the same sign as the drum and held at a voltage between the charged and discharged levels. The powder is pushed away from the untouched areas and onto the discharged ones. How wide a printed line comes out depends on the size of the laser spot, which is larger than a pixel, on how much light it delivers, and on where the developer voltage sits.

Worked example. With the drum at 600 V, a residual of 60 V and the developer at 300 V, a spot of radius 30 µm writing alternate 42 µm pixels at an exposure of 1.5 prints lines 39 µm wide separated by 45 µm gaps, close to the intended pattern; the lowest voltage reached is 213 V. At an exposure of 3 the lines are 62 µm wide and the gaps 22 µm; at 6 the gaps close completely. At an exposure of 1 the surface only reaches 293 V and the lines are 11 µm wide and barely print.

Watch out. Voltages are given as magnitudes; in most printers drum, toner and developer are all negative. The discharge law is the simplest one, development is taken as proportional to the voltage difference, and the profile is shown along the scan direction only. Printers also modulate the laser within a pixel to smooth edges, which is not shown.

Why do thin lines from a laser printer get thicker when the print density is turned up?

The laser spot is wider than one pixel and fades gradually at its edges. More exposure, or a developer voltage nearer the charged level, means the faint edges of the spot discharge the drum far enough to take toner, so each line grows outwards on both sides.

Reference: D. M. Pai and B. E. Springett · Physics of electrophotography (Reviews of Modern Physics, 1993)

3. The print engine

Every part of a laser printer is tied to one speed, the speed of the paper. The drum’s surface must move exactly with it, so the image neither stretches nor smears as it is rolled off. The laser draws the page one line at a time as the drum turns beneath it, sweeping across by reflection from a spinning mirror with several flat faces: each face that passes makes one sweep. Lines must be laid down at the resolution’s spacing, so the number of sweeps per second is the paper speed divided by that spacing, and the mirror’s speed follows from its number of faces. Within each sweep the laser must switch for every pixel across the page. Last, the fuser has to raise the paper and toner by well over a hundred degrees in the fraction of a second they spend in its grip.

Worked example. At 30 A4 pages a minute, with 40 mm between sheets, the paper moves at 168.5 mm/s and a 30 mm drum turns at 107 rpm. At 600 dpi that is 3980 lines a second, so a six-faced mirror must spin at 39 800 rpm, and with 5100 pixels in a line and 70 per cent of each sweep usable the laser switches at up to 29 MHz. Heating 80 g/m² paper by 160 K takes 607 W, 1.2 kJ a page. At 60 pages a minute and 1200 dpi one beam would need 159 000 rpm.

Watch out. The engine is a generic single-colour design with representative dimensions, shown at about a fiftieth of its speed, with the beam path simplified to one fold. Colour printers repeat the drum and developer four times. The fuser figure counts only the heat that goes into the paper, not what is lost to the air or spent warming up.

Why do fast laser printers use several laser beams at once?

Each face of the spinning mirror gives one sweep of the beam, and the number of sweeps needed each second rises with both paper speed and resolution. One beam would soon need a mirror turning faster than any bearing allows. Two or four beams side by side write that many lines per sweep, so the mirror can turn a half or a quarter as fast.

Reference: D. M. Pai and B. E. Springett · Physics of electrophotography (Reviews of Modern Physics, 1993)
